# Chapter 35: Anvil Again

Yara gave him a slab at the hatch and did not say what it was for.

"Crew gets a share," she said. "It's a Halyard ship. Hal's cousin worked the hold thirty-one days and never once put his plate up. That's a share." She put it in his hands. A hand deep, an arm long, the Vein's dark glass, the first piece of it he had held since the loading gallery. Tacitus looked at it and did not lean; it had been told. "Cut it small. Nobody on Anvil takes a slab off a Halyard without asking where the rest is. Shards. You know how."

"I know how."

"Hood up. Dock gate's got auxiliaries on it now, not vigiles. They read arms." She looked at his. "Three days. Then I've a College dock to lie to, and I'll not have you on my slate when I do it." She stepped back from the hatch. "Go on. Say the names somewhere else for three days. Pike's not slept."

He went down the ramp with the hood up and the slab under his coat, and did not look at her; she would not have wanted him to. The ramp ended on the rock of Anvil's lower docks.

Seven years. He had come down a ramp onto this rock in a good coat with his hat in his hand for the inspector, his father at the gate. He stood on it now in a Halyard crewman's grey with a hood over a shaved head and a white line on his arm. Under his ribs was Tacitus, forty-two and settled, with a name now. The rock was the same rock. He felt it through his boots, as he felt everything, and it was not the same.

It was full. The Deep road had had sixty hearts, and he had learned to count them. The lower docks had thousands, going fast, every way. He sorted it, the way the old man had taught him to sort a dinner table: dock gate, twelve plated, slow-hearted, the ones with the fangs. Everyone else fast. Everyone else a Collar in a war.

He walked to the gate.

---

The gate was the same gate. The men on it were not.

They wore grey with a black band at the shoulder. Not vigiles; vigiles were Blanks with clubs who took a bottle to look the other way. These were Collars. He saw the line on the first one's arm as the man put his hand out for papers, the same line Jonah had had for nineteen years. Above it, a Thinstripe's band sewn on grey. He understood what the war had done to Anvil.

It had made auxiliaries. A Collar who'd put a fang in other Collars for the Empire got a band, a cutting, a Blank's ration, and a stripe if he lived. The docks were policed by men who knew a Collar's arm better than any Blank did.

Two men ahead of him a dockhand said something to the auxiliary reading his papers. The auxiliary hit him across the face, once, hard, and the man went down against the rail. Nobody in the line moved. The auxiliary picked the papers off the ground, read them, and handed them back without a word. The dockhand got up holding his jaw and did not look at anyone. The line went on.

"Papers."

"Halyard crew. Off the Heron. No papers; captain's got the slate." Jonah said it in a Belt turn he had had thirty-one days to learn from Pike. "Hal's cousin."

The auxiliary looked at the hood. "Arm."

He gave it. The left one; he had counted that too. The auxiliary pushed the sleeve back and looked at a forearm with nothing on it, no line, no cutting, never collared, never invested. The plate under the skin lay so still it was not there. The man let the sleeve fall.

"Other one."

"Burned. Torch. Six years back. You'll not want to see it," Jonah said.

The auxiliary looked at him a moment longer. He was twenty, a fang he had not used, a band a week old. Jonah stood with his face doing nothing, a man who'd rather not show a burn, bored, tired, Belt. The auxiliary said, "Go on," and Jonah went, past the man still holding his jaw against the rail.

Sabinus said, behind his eyes, without words: that one's nothing.

"He's a boy with a band," Jonah said, in the old tongue, under his breath, walking. "What I'd have been if I'd stayed. The Empire doesn't trust a Blank to check an arm. It only trusts a Collar who's already put a fang in one. That's the whole design, and it's tidy. Shut up."

---

The melt-house was on the north side, where the yards' waste went, and the Flamens kept a clerk in it.

He had never been inside. Nobody went inside who did not have to. The clerk was a Blank with a stripe on his wrist so thin it might have been a scar, and a ledger.

"Six years back. Winter. South yards. A man named Vale," Jonah said.

The clerk did not look up. "The melt doesn't take names."

"It takes something."

"It takes a count." The clerk turned the ledger round, unasked. "Winter of three eighty-nine. There. South yards, adult male, one. South yards, adult male, one. South yards, adult female, one. Paid by the yard." He put his finger on the column. "That's what a Collar gets. A line and who paid. If your man was south yards and winter, he's one of those. Nobody writes which."

Jonah looked at the column. South yards, adult male, one. Paid by the yard. Morrow's yard. A line in a clerk's hand that was his father, or was not. No record of Tom Vale anywhere else: not the quaestor's crates, not the square. The only place he was written was in Jonah, and on a slab on Tessera in a woman's lap.

"Paid by the yard," he said. "Which yard?"

"Doesn't say. Morrow's, if it's south. He's the only one who pays. Or was." The clerk shut the book. "You'll want the Halyard. Gaspar's there. He was there for all of them."

"Thank you," Jonah said. The clerk did not look up for that either, but Jonah said it anyway, because the man had given him a column and asked for nothing back.

---

The Halyard was the same. A tavern cut into rock under a festival square did not change. The corner table was the corner table. He had sat at it with his father on his tenth birthday, and with Mara the night before the Kestrel went out.

The drunk was at it now.

Gaspar Roake was fifty-eight. He had been fifty when Jonah saw him last, and looked eighty. Head on his arms, a bottle in front of him, north-yard, half gone at the fourth bell. Jonah stood in the door with the hood up and looked at him. Sabinus, behind his eyes, had an opinion. Jonah said, in his head, in the old tongue: no. Not yours. This one's mine. I've had it seven years.

He went and sat down.

Gaspar did not lift his head. Jonah put a shard on the table, small, cut from the Heron's slab with the crooked fang, the width of a thumbnail, dark. More matter than a Collar on Anvil saw in a year. He set it by the bottle where a man with his head on his arms would see it when he opened his eyes.

Gaspar opened his eyes.

"Halyard," he said, after a while. Not the tavern. The man.

"Off the Heron."

"Heron's Yara's. A skimmer. You've come to sell that to Morrow, and Morrow can't buy; his paper's in the bank." He said it to the shard, slurred and clear at once, the slur worn so long it had become the voice. "What d'you want for it?"

"A story."

"Everybody wants a story. I've a hundred. Which."

"Tom Vale."

Gaspar lifted his head. He looked at the hood with wet half-open eyes and saw nothing a man drunk seven years could read. His eyes went back to the shard.

"Who's asking."

"Does it matter, if the story's the same either way?" Jonah said, and did not wait for Gaspar to decide it did. "A man off the Belt who heard the name. From somebody who'd been to Tessera." He kept the Belt in his voice. "Said a Collar off Anvil died in a Pit with a story on him, and a man named Gaspar had it, and would sell it."

"I'd sell it. I've sold it before. Nobody's paid that much." He put his hand over the shard and did not pick it up. "Tom's boy."

"Yes."

"Jonah. His name was Jonah. Flew a barge." His hand stayed on the shard. "I'm going to tell it once and tell it right, because you've paid, and I've never once told it right. Everybody I've told it to, I've told it so it looks good on me. There's no way it looks good on me. So I've told it wrong."

Jonah waited.

"Barge came in. Kestrel. Ochoa dead; everybody knew that by the second bell. Tom's boy walked off it on his own feet with something in his hands he wasn't showing anybody, and Tom at the gate in his good coat. I saw the boy's face when he saw his father, and I thought, that's a boy who's been somewhere. Then the festival. Invested. Citizen. Tom crying in the square." Gaspar's eyes stayed on the shard. "That night I came down here with a bottle. Sat at this table, nearest the stair. Two men at it already. The purser off the Kestrel, with paper and a pen. Corin Aldane, who'd won the sharpening that morning and wanted Mara. The purser was writing."

"Writing what."

"A letter. To the quaestor. I heard every word; he read it out to Corin so Corin would sign, and Corin didn't want to and did." Gaspar's voice did not change. "Unregistered genius. That's what he wrote. Something in the boy that wasn't a cutting, wasn't invested, read wrong under the glass. He'd seen it through a hatch in the rings, and a decurion was dead. And a seal, a packet Ochoa had, for a senator. He wrote the senator's name and I've never remembered it. Corin signed. Witness. Then they both looked at me."

"And you said."

"Tom's boy." Gaspar looked at his own hand. "I said, Tom's boy. He's a good boy. Then I put my head down and went to sleep. I heard the purser fold the paper, and I let him, because a man asleep in a tavern wasn't there. That's what I've been. Seven years." He picked up the bottle and did not drink from it. "Vigiles came for the boy the next night, at his own engagement dinner. Mara screaming. Tom went to the quaestor's door every morning for a month, hat in hand. I watched him go past my window and never once went down to say I knew what was in the letter. I'd a bottle every morning he went past."

Jonah sat very still.

"And then he stopped going," Gaspar said. "Sat. Door open. Hat on his knee. Winter came, and somebody went in, and he'd been sitting some time." He put the bottle down. "Morrow paid the melt, that's what they say. It's true, and it's not the whole. Morrow's yard paid it because Morrow pays everything. But the money wasn't his; the bank had his paper by then. The purser'd gone to the bank. Marchand. Came off the Kestrel, went to the quaestor with a letter, and the quaestor saw to him. A year on he'd a house on the upper ring, the tax farm, the bank that held Morrow's yard by the throat. When Tom died Morrow went to the bank for the melt, and Marchand gave it him out of his own purse, through the yard, so it'd say the yard." Gaspar looked at the shard. "I know because I was at the melt. I'm always at the melt. Marchand stood at the back of it in a good coat, watching the money burn. His face wasn't sorry. It was a man checking a sum."

"And the name."

"You don't say the name. Not at a melt. It calls the grain-men." His mouth did something. "Mara said it. Stood at the front with the fire on her face and said, Tom Vale, out loud, once. Every Collar there went cold. Corin took her home. She married him the year after. He's a Stripe now, thin, got it breaking Collars in the rising on Tessera. Mara wears his ring, and there's a boy, three. She wears the hull ring too, Tom's boy's, on a chain under her shirt. Everybody knows, and Corin knows, and nobody says." Gaspar drank. "That's the whole of it. I've told you because you've paid and I'm drunk, and because you're a Halyard who'll be gone in three days. I'll never see your face, and I've not seen it now."

Jonah did not say anything for a while.

"Where's the boy," he said, at last. "Tom's boy. They said a Pit."

"Tessera. The quaestor's paper said. Died in the Throat, the year he went. Corin read it out in the square when he came back with the band, so everybody'd know, so Mara'd know, so she'd stop." Gaspar looked at the bottle. "She didn't stop. She wears the ring. But he read it. Died in the Throat. Three eighty-nine."

"Do you believe it?"

Gaspar looked up.

He looked at the hood with his wet eyes for a long time, longer than a man looks at a stranger. Jonah felt his heart through the table, an old drunk's heart, slow and thick, going. It skipped once, and went on.

"No," Gaspar said. "I never have. Tom's boy walked off a barge with a dead captain and that face on him. He'd not die in a Throat. He'd die somewhere he chose." He put his hand on the shard again, and this time he picked it up. "You've got his hands."

Jonah's heart did nothing. He had learned that from a man across a desk, through a cell slot, by having it done to him.

"Halyards have hands," he said. "It's the ships."

"Aye." Gaspar put the shard inside his coat and put his head down. "If you see him. Tell him Gaspar Roake heard the letter and said nothing and has had a bottle every morning since. Tell him I loved his father. Tell him I'm sorry." His eyes closed. "He'll not care. Nobody does. It's what a drunk has. It's not much."

He was asleep before Jonah stood up.

---

He said the names that night at the rail of the lower docks, where he had once said he would come and had not. The hull ring of Anvil hung above him, catching the sun-lamps at their night setting. The Heron's ramp was behind him, Pike on it, not sleeping.

He said them in order. Tacitus and the old man and the rest. When he came to his father he said the whole of it: nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder, sat in a chair with the door open. Then what a drunk had given him: the melt paid out of a purser's purse through a good man's yard, and a man in a good coat at the back checking the sum. He said Ullan. Decimus, Sula's father first. The two boys at the road's end, the mate with half an arm, the Centurion who said oh.

Then he said the four.

He said them to Tacitus, in the old tongue, the way he had said its name in the only room in the Pit that knew what a name was for. One at a time, with what each had done.

"Silas Marchand. Purser. Saw the plate through a hatch and was afraid, and wrote it down. Made a frightened man sign it, and took it to a door himself. Was seen to. Has a house on the upper ring and a bank with a good man's yard by the throat. Paid my father's melt out of his own purse so the ledger would say somebody else. Checking the sum."

It listened. It had one word and did not use it.

"Corin Aldane. Cutter. Signed because I'd won a bout he couldn't. Told himself it was for her. Went to the auxiliaries. Got a band, then a stripe breaking Collars on Tessera, and read my death out in a square so she'd stop. She didn't stop. He's got her and a boy and a stripe. She wears my ring under her shirt where he can see the chain."

The docks went on being full behind him. Thousands, fast.

"Gaspar Roake. Welder. Heard the letter read, said Tom's boy, and went to sleep. Watched my father go past his window for a month with a bottle in his hand, and said nothing. Loved him. Says so. Sold me the story for a shard, and told it right for the first time. Is sorry. It's what a drunk has, and it's not much."

He stopped.

He stood at the rail with his hands on it, and Tacitus waited. He thought of a man who had said, no, I never believed it, he'd die somewhere he chose. Who had looked at a hood longer than a man looks at a stranger, and said you've got his hands.

"He's on the slate," Jonah said. "He did nothing. That's what he chose, every morning, with a bottle. The old man said no innocents. A count that leaves out the ones who did nothing isn't a count, it's a story you tell so you can sleep, and I'll not tell it." He breathed. "Marcus Vibius Priscus. Quaestor. Read my heart and found it doing nothing. Sent me down a hole for what I carried, and burned it. Wrote me dead so nobody would look, and told his sister I'd gone home."

The hand with no hand.

"Not yet. Not for him. For me," Jonah said. He took his hands off the rail. "Four. That's the count. Three on this rock and one in the City. I've a slab under my coat, three days, and a hood, and a Thinstripe reading arms at the gate who's what I'd have been." He looked up at the hull ring. "And she said his name. At the fire. Where you don't."

Behind him, up the dock road, a bell went.

He knew it, though he had never heard it: a curfew bell, a garrison bell, the bell Tullus's men had rung on the Throat when the line went in. After it, boots from the tenement stair. Twelve. Plated. Coming down the dock road at the pace of men told to clear it. Ahead of them, fast, a great many hearts that were not plated. One voice, carrying, careful, saying to a Collar in the road what Jonah had heard it say to a man in the sand seven years ago.

"Friend," Corin Aldane said, up the road, in the dark. "You'll want to be indoors."
