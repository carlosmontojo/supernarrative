# Chapter 35: Anvil Again

Yara gave him a slab at the hatch and did not say what it was for.

"Crew gets a share," she said. "It's a Halyard ship. Hal's cousin worked the hold thirty-one days and never once put his plate up, and that's a share." She put it in his hands, a hand deep and an arm long, the Vein's dark glass, the first piece of it he had held since the loading gallery, and the thing in the doorway of him looked at it and did not lean, because it had been told. "Cut it small. Nobody on Anvil takes a slab off a Halyard without asking where the rest is. Shards. You know how."

"I know how."

"Hood up. Dock gate's got auxiliaries on it now, not vigiles. They read arms." She looked at his. "Three days. Then I've a College dock to lie to and I'll not have you on my slate when I do it." She stepped back from the hatch. "Go on. Say the names somewhere else for three days. Pike's not slept."

He went down the ramp with the hood up and the slab under his coat and did not look at her, because she would not have wanted him to, and the ramp ended on the rock of Anvil's lower docks, and he stood on it.

Seven years. He had come down a ramp onto this rock in a good coat with his hat in his hand for the inspector and his father at the gate, and he stood on it now in a Halyard crewman's grey with a hood over a shaved head and a white line on his arm and a thing under his ribs that was forty-two and settled and had a name, and the rock was the same rock, and he felt it through his boots the way he felt everything, and it was not the same.

It was full. That was the first thing. The Deep road had had sixty hearts on it and he had learned to count them; the lower docks had thousands, going every way, and they were going fast, all of them, the way a road's went when Tullus's men came down with a lamp. He stood at the foot of the Heron's ramp and let it come through him and sorted it, the way the old man had taught him to sort a dinner table: dock gate, twelve plated, Tiro, in a line, and their hearts slow because they were the ones with the fangs. Everyone else fast. Everyone else a Collar in a war.

He walked to the gate.

---

The gate was the same gate. The men on it were not.

They wore grey with a black band at the shoulder, and they were not vigiles; vigiles were Blanks with clubs who took a bottle to look the other way. These were Collars. He saw the line on the first one's arm as the man put his hand out for papers, a Collar's line, the line Jonah had had for nineteen years, and above it, on the shoulder, a Thinstripe's band sewn on grey, and the plate on the hand that took the papers was thin and grey and new, and he understood, standing in the line at the dock gate with his hood up, what the war had done to Anvil.

It had made auxiliaries. A Collar who would put a fang in other Collars for the Empire got a band and a cutting and a Blank's ration, and a stripe at the end of it if he lived, and the yards were full of men who had done the sum and come out at the band, and the docks were policed by them, and they read arms, because a Collar knew a Collar's arm better than any Blank did.

"Papers."

"Halyard crew. Off the Heron. No papers; captain's got the slate." Jonah said it in a Belt turn he had had thirty-one days to learn from Pike. "Hal's cousin."

The auxiliary looked at the hood.

"Arm."

He gave it. The left one; he had counted that too. The auxiliary pushed the sleeve back and looked at a forearm with nothing on it, no line, no cutting, a Belt man's arm that had never been collared and never been invested, and the plate under the skin lay so still that it was not there, and the man let the sleeve fall.

"Other one."

"Burned," Jonah said. "Torch. Six years back. You'll not want to see it."

The auxiliary looked at him a moment longer. He was twenty, and he had a fang he had not used, and a band that was a week old, and behind him the docks were full of Collars going fast, and he was one of twelve, and Jonah stood in front of him with his face doing nothing at all, being a man who had been asked to show a burn and would rather not, bored, tired, Belt, and the auxiliary said, "Go on," and Jonah went.

Sabinus said, behind his eyes, without words: that one's nothing.

"He's a boy with a band," Jonah said, in the old tongue, under his breath, walking. "He's what I'd have been if I'd stayed. Shut up."

---

The melt-house was on the north side, where the yards' waste went, and the Flamens kept a clerk in it.

He had never been inside. Nobody went inside who did not have to; it was a low rock building with a chimney that had been burning for ninety years, and the smell of it was the smell of every winter of his life, and the clerk was a Blank with a stripe on his wrist so thin it might have been a scar, and a ledger, and the ledger was the thing Jonah had come for.

"Six years back," he said. "Winter. South yards. A man named Vale."

The clerk did not look up.

"The melt doesn't take names."

"It takes something."

"It takes a count." The clerk turned the ledger round without being asked; a Halyard crewman with a hood was a thing that did not matter enough to refuse. "Winter of three eighty-nine. There. South yards, adult male, one. South yards, adult male, one. South yards, adult female, one. Paid by the yard. Paid by the yard. Paid by the family." He put his finger on the column. "That's what a Collar gets. A line and who paid. If your man was south yards and winter, he's one of those. Nobody writes which."

Jonah looked at the column.

South yards, adult male, one. Paid by the yard. Morrow's yard. Hollen had said it on the Deep road with sixty men listening. He looked at a line in a clerk's hand that was his father, or was not, and had no name on it because a Collar's name was not written, and he thought, with the part of him that counted: there's no record of Tom Vale. Not here. Not in the quaestor's crates, where there's a register that says his son died in the Throat. Not in the square. The only place he's written is in me, and in a slab on Tessera in a hand that learned to write by cutting, and a woman with a book has that in her lap.

"Paid by the yard," he said. "Which yard?"

"Doesn't say. The yard." The clerk took the ledger back. "Morrow's, if it's south. He's the only one who pays. Or was." He shut the book. "You'll want the Halyard. Everybody who asks about a Collar wants the Halyard after. Gaspar's there. He was there for all of them."

---

The Halyard was the same.

That was the thing about a tavern cut into rock under a festival square; it did not change, because nothing in it could. The corner table was the corner table. He had sat at it with his father on his tenth birthday and with Mara on the night before the Kestrel went out, and a purser had sat at it with a pen and a sheet of manifest paper, and a Collar cutter had signed, and a drunk had lifted his head off his arms and said Tom's boy and put it down again.

The drunk was at the table.

Gaspar Roake was fifty-eight and had been fifty when Jonah saw him last and looked eighty. He sat with his head on his arms and a bottle in front of him that was north-yard and half gone at the fourth bell, and Jonah stood in the door of the Halyard with the hood up and looked at him for a long time, and the thing behind his eyes that was Sabinus looked too, and had an opinion, and Jonah said, in his head, in the old tongue: no. Not yours. This one's mine. This one I've had for seven years.

He went and sat down.

Gaspar did not lift his head. Jonah put a shard on the table, small, cut from the Heron's slab in the hold with the crooked fang, the width of a thumbnail, dark, and it was more matter than a Collar on Anvil saw in a year, and he put it by the bottle where a man with his head on his arms would see it when he opened his eyes.

Gaspar opened his eyes.

"Halyard," he said, after a while. Not the tavern. The man.

"Off the Heron."

"Heron's Yara's. Yara's a skimmer. You've come to sell that to Morrow and Morrow can't buy; Morrow's paper's in the bank." Gaspar said it to the shard. His voice was slurred and it was clear; it had been slurred for so long that the slur was the voice. "What d'you want for it?"

"A story."

"Everybody wants a story. I've a hundred. Which."

"Tom Vale."

Gaspar lifted his head.

He looked at the hood. He looked at it with the wet half-open eyes and Jonah let him and did not do anything with his face, because the face was in the hood and the hood was in the Halyard's dark and there was nothing on it that a man who had been drunk for seven years could read, and Gaspar looked, and did not see, and his eyes went back to the shard.

"Who's asking."

"A man off the Belt who heard the name. On a ship. From somebody who'd been to Tessera." Jonah kept the Belt in his voice. "They said a Collar off Anvil had died in a Pit with a story on him, and the story was here, and a man named Gaspar had it, and would sell it."

"I'd sell it," Gaspar said. "I've sold it before. Nobody's paid that much." He put his hand over the shard. He did not pick it up. "Tom's boy."

"Yes."

"Jonah. His name was Jonah. Tom's boy. He flew a barge." The old man's hand stayed on the shard. "I'm going to tell it once and I'm going to tell it right, because you've paid, and because I've never once told it right. Everybody I've told it to I've told it the way it looks good on me, and there's no way it looks good on me, so I've told it wrong."

Jonah waited.

"Barge came in. Kestrel. Ochoa dead; everybody knew that by the second bell. Tom's boy walked off it on his own feet with a thing in his hands he wasn't showing anybody and Tom at the gate in his good coat, and I saw that, I was at the gate, I saw the boy's face when he saw his father and I thought, that's a boy who's been somewhere. And then the festival. And the boy was invested. Citizen. Tom's boy with a line on his arm that was a citizen's, and Tom crying in the square, and everybody said, there, the ladder's real, Tom always said." Gaspar's eyes were on the shard. "And that night I came down here with the ration in me and a bottle, and I sat at this table because it was nearest the stair, and there were two men at it, and one of them was the purser off the Kestrel with paper and a pen, and the other one was Corin Aldane who'd won the sharpening that morning and wanted Mara, everybody knew that, and the purser was writing."

"Writing what."

"A letter. To the quaestor. I heard it. I heard every word; he read it out to Corin so Corin would sign it, and Corin didn't want to and did." The old man's voice did not change. "Unregistered genius. That's what he wrote. That the boy had a thing in him that wasn't a cutting and wasn't invested and read wrong under the glass, and the purser'd seen it through a hatch in the rings, and a decurion was dead. And a seal. A packet Ochoa had, with a seal on it, for a senator, and the purser wrote the senator's name and I've never remembered it and I've tried. And Corin signed. Witness. And then they both looked at me."

"And you said."

"Tom's boy." Gaspar took his hand off the shard and looked at the hand. "I said, Tom's boy. He's a good boy. That's what I said. Then I put my head down and I went to sleep, and I heard the purser fold the paper, and I let him, because a man who's asleep in a tavern's a man who wasn't there. That's what I've been. Seven years. A man who wasn't there." He picked up the bottle and did not drink from it. "Vigiles came for the boy the next night at his own engagement dinner. Everybody heard. Mara screaming. Tom went to the quaestor's door every morning for a month with his hat in his hand and I watched him go past my window and I didn't once go down and say, Tom, I know what was in the letter, I heard the purser read it. I'd a bottle. I'd a bottle every morning he went past."

Jonah sat very still.

"And then he stopped going," Gaspar said. "And he sat. Door open. Hat on his knee. And then it was winter, and somebody went in, and he'd been sitting some time." He put the bottle down. "Morrow paid the melt. That's what they say and it's true and it's not the whole. Morrow's yard paid it because Morrow pays, he pays everything, he's the only Blank on Anvil who'd pay a Collar's melt and he did. But the money wasn't his. He'd none by then; the bank had his paper. The purser'd gone to the bank. Marchand. He came off the Kestrel and he went to the quaestor with a letter and the quaestor saw to him, that's what they said, saw to him, and a year on he'd a house on the upper ring and the tax farm and the bank, and the bank held Morrow's yard by the throat, and when Tom died Morrow went to the bank for the melt and Marchand gave it him. Out of his own purse. Through the yard. So it'd say, paid by the yard." Gaspar looked at the shard. "I know because I was at the melt. I'm always at the melt. And Marchand was at the back of it in a good coat, watching the money burn, and I saw his face, and it wasn't sorry. It was a man checking a sum."

"And the name."

"You don't say the name. Not at a melt. It calls the grain-men." The old man's mouth did something. "Mara said it. She stood at the front with the fire on her face and she said, Tom Vale, out loud, once, and every Collar there went cold, and Corin took her arm and took her home. She'd have said the boy's too, I think, if Corin'd let her. She married Corin the year after. He'd a band by then. He's a Stripe now. Thin. He got it in the rising on Tessera, breaking Collars, and he came home with it and Mara wears his ring, and there's a boy, three, and she wears the other thing too, the hull ring, Tom's boy's, on a chain under her shirt, and everybody knows, and Corin knows, and nobody says." Gaspar drank. "That's the story. That's the whole of it. I've never told it whole. I've told you because you've paid and because I'm drunk and because you're a Halyard and you'll be gone in three days and I'll never see your face, and I've not seen it now."

Jonah did not say anything for a while.

"Where's the boy," he said, at last. "Tom's boy. They said a Pit."

"Tessera. The quaestor's paper said. Died in the Throat, the year he went. Corin read it out in the square when he came back with the band; he'd got it from the quaestor's clerk, a copy, and he read it out so everybody'd know, so Mara'd know, so she'd stop." The old man looked at the bottle. "She didn't stop. She wears the ring. But he read it. Died in the Throat. Three eighty-nine."

"Do you believe it?"

Gaspar looked up.

He looked at the hood with his wet eyes for a long time, longer than a man looks at a stranger, and Jonah felt his heart through the table, an old drunk's heart, slow and thick and going, and it did a thing, once, and went on.

"No," Gaspar said. "I never have. Tom's boy walked off a barge with a dead captain and a thing in his hands and his face like that. He'd not die in a Throat. He'd die somewhere he chose." He put his hand on the shard again, and this time he picked it up. "You've got his hands."

Jonah's heart did nothing. He had learned that from a man he had never met, across a desk, through a cell slot, by having it done to him.

"Halyards have hands," he said. "It's the ships."

"Aye." Gaspar put the shard inside his coat. "That'll be it." He put his head down on his arms. "If you see him. On the Belt. Wherever. Tell him Gaspar Roake heard the letter and said nothing and has had a bottle every morning since. Tell him I loved his father. Tell him I'm sorry." His eyes closed. "He'll not care. Nobody does. It's what a drunk has. It's not much."

He was asleep before Jonah stood up.

---

He said the names that night at the rail of the lower docks, where he had once said he would come and had not, with the hull ring of Anvil above him catching the sun-lamps at their night setting and the Heron's ramp behind him with Pike on it, not sleeping.

He said them in order. He said Tacitus and the old man and the rest, and when he came to his father he said the whole of it, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder, sat in a chair with the door open, and then he said the new thing, the thing he had not had until a drunk gave it him for a shard: the melt paid out of a purser's purse through a good man's yard so the ledger would say the yard, and a man in a good coat at the back checking the sum. He said Ullan. He said Decimus, Sula's father first. He said the two boys at the road's end and the mate with half an arm and the Centurion who said oh.

Then he said the four.

He said them to the thing in the doorway of him, in the old tongue, the way he had said its name in the only room in the Pit that knew what a name was for, one at a time, with what each had done, because the old man had said you write what is said and you write who said it.

"Silas Marchand. Purser. Saw the plate through a hatch and was afraid, and wrote it down, and read it out so a frightened man would sign, and took it to a door himself, and was seen to, and has a house on the upper ring and a bank with a good man's yard by the throat, and paid my father's melt out of his own purse so the ledger would say somebody else. Checking the sum."

It listened. It had one word and it did not use it.

"Corin Aldane. Cutter. Won a bout he could not have won against me, and signed, because I'd won one he couldn't, and told himself it was for her. Went to the auxiliaries. Got a band. Got a stripe breaking Collars on Tessera and read my death out in a square so she'd stop. She didn't stop. He's got her and a boy and a stripe, and she wears my ring under her shirt where he can see the chain."

The docks went on being full behind him. Thousands, fast.

"Gaspar Roake. Welder. Heard the letter read and said, Tom's boy, and went to sleep, and watched my father go past his window every morning for a month with a bottle in his hand and said nothing. Loved him. Says so. Sold me the story for a shard and told it right for the first time, and is sorry, and it's what a drunk has, and it's not much."

He stopped.

He stood at the rail with his hands on it and the thing in him waited, and he thought about a man with his head on his arms who had said, no, I never believed it, he'd die somewhere he chose, and who had looked at a hood for longer than a man looks at a stranger, and had said you've got his hands, and had been told it was the ships.

"He's on the slate," Jonah said. "He did nothing. That's what he did. That's the thing he chose, every morning, with a bottle, and my father went past the window, and it's on the slate. The old man said no innocents. He said a count that leaves out the ones who did nothing isn't a count, it's a story you tell so you can sleep, and I'll not tell it." He breathed. "Marcus Vibius Priscus. Quaestor. Read my heart and found it doing nothing and sent me down a hole for what I carried, and burned it, and wrote me dead in a clerk's hand so nobody would look, and told his sister I'd gone home."

The thing in the doorway did the small thing. The hand with no hand.

"Not yet," Jonah said. "Not for him. For me." He took his hands off the rail. "Four. That's the count. Three on this rock and one in the City and I've a slab under my coat and three days and a hood, and a Thinstripe reading arms at the gate who's what I'd have been." He looked up at the hull ring. "And she said his name. At the fire. Where you don't."

Behind him, up the dock road, a bell went.

He knew it. He had never heard it and he knew it, the way he had known the water step the first time he saw it done right, because it was a thing he had drilled in a different shape: a curfew bell, a garrison bell, the bell Tullus's men had rung on the Throat when the line went in, and after it, from the tenement stair, boots. Twelve. Plated. Coming down the dock road at the pace men walk when they have been told to clear it, and ahead of them, going fast, a great many hearts that were not plated, and one voice, carrying, careful, saying a thing to a Collar in the road that Jonah had heard it say to a man in the sand seven years ago.

"Friend," Corin Aldane said, up the road, in the dark. "You'll want to be indoors."
