# Chapter 7: Ad Metalla

The lights came up three hours out from Anvil, and Jonah saw what he'd been sitting among.

A cargo hold. The same long steel box as the Kestrel's, with rings in the floor for strapping down freight. The freight was men. Forty of them, along the walls and down the middle, hands strapped in front of them. Most wore collars. Some had lines on their arms. One, near the ramp, had a thin purple stripe and a face that had been hit recently and often.

Nobody spoke. Forty men in a box, and the only sound was breathing and the drive.

They looked at him and decided. Collar. Young. A line on the arm, so a Blank who'd lost it inside a week. Bound. Nothing.

He let them. He sat with his back to the wall, hands in his lap, the ring on his finger. The grey Blanks had not taken it. They hadn't been the kind who took things. But wherever this ended there would be a Flamen, a glass, and a man with a list, and the first thing that happened to a man on a list was that everything he had came off him.

He turned the ring on his finger.

Under his ribs, it watched him do it. It had come up when the grey Blanks took him and had not gone back down. It lay in his arms now, not ready, just there, eyes open. Hungry, always. And it looked at the ring with the flat, total interest it gave to anything the body did that it hadn't seen before.

"It's hers," he said under his breath. "It stays."

The interest didn't change. Something else did. The plate came up on the inside of his left forearm. Not out. Up. A patch under the skin, dark, the size of a coin. It lay there, and it was soft.

He looked at the patch. He looked at the ring.

He didn't decide. He'd stopped pretending he decided things. He took the ring off and pressed it with his thumb against the soft dark place. The plate took it. The ring went down through the skin without breaking it, and the plate closed over it and went back to being skin. A small hard lump under the flesh of his forearm, where no bone was. The Halcyon. Hers, and staying.

He sat back with his heart going.

Across the hold, the Thinstripe with the hit face was looking at him.

---

"They'll Hush us," the man beside him said, to the floor.

Old, a Collar, with a farmer's hands. Jonah hadn't heard him speak before. He wasn't sure the man was speaking to him now.

"Every shift. Crew comes down and says the word. Puts the Blanks to sleep. Not the men. The things in the men." A nod toward the ramp. "Watch the Stripe. He hates it."

"How do you know?"

"Been in a hold before. Not this one." The old man didn't look up. "Pell."

"Jonah."

"Won't matter, where we're going. Doesn't hurt to say it once."

The crew came down at the sixth hour. Four Blanks in grey with a Flamen's mark on the shoulder, rifles, down the ladder from the cabin. One of them said a word.

Not the word from his father's room. Another one, in the old Latin. It went through the hold, and Jonah watched it go. It reached the Blanks with lines on their arms one after another. Each of them flinched, a shoulder going up, and then went slack. Not the bodies. Something behind the bodies. It reached the Thinstripe by the ramp and the man's jaw locked and his face went white, then grey. He hated it with his whole body and went slack anyway.

Then it reached Jonah.

It was aimed at the line on his arm, at a stupid loyal friend fused at nine that would hear it and lie down. It went through him and found the thing under his ribs instead. That looked at it with the same flat interest it had given the collar's word in the Kestrel's hold, and let it pass.

Jonah flinched. On purpose, a shoulder going up. He let his eyes go dull. The crewman's gaze went over him and on.

"Water," the crewman said. "Twice a shift. Ration's by the ring. One cup, one man, one ring. Anyone takes two, it's between him and the man he took it from. We don't come down for that. We come down for the Hush and the count and that's all." He looked along the hold. "Thirty-one days. Make it easy."

They put a barrel by the ladder with a cup chained to it, and went up.

"See," Pell said, to the floor.

---

He learned the hold in the dark, by paying attention. Nine Blanks, who sat together by the barrel. Thirty-one Collars, who did not. And Pell, who sang to the floor at the change of shift, quietly, a different song each time.

"Fields," Pell said, when Jonah asked. "Marl. One song for every field. I had four."

"What did you do?"

"Didn't make quota. One field, one bad year." He shrugged. "That's a sentence, on Marl. What did you do?"

"I don't know."

"That's a sentence too."

"Who's the Stripe?"

"Aulus something. Garrison officer, Marl. Killed a Blank in a sharpening he shouldn't have. Blank had the wrong friends." Pell looked at the man by the ramp, then away. "They've took his licence and his office. They'll take the rest at the other end. He knows it. Look at his face."

Jonah looked. The Thinstripe sat by the ramp with his hit face and his dead stripe and watched the ladder. Jonah knew the expression from the inside.

He learned the water.

The first shift it was what the crew had said. Forty men in a line with their strapped hands out, the cup going down the line, Blanks first because they were nearest the barrel. Second shift the same. On the third shift a Collar boy from Marl, sixteen, who had been crying since the lights came up, was fourth in line. A big Blank named Hask, with a line on his arm and something in his hand, put the something against the boy's neck.

"Mine," Hask said.

The boy gave it to him. There was a piece of hull steel at his throat, sharpened on the deck rings until it had an edge. He gave up the cup and went and sat down. Nobody spoke. The crew did not come down. It was between him and the man he took it from. The law had been said out loud.

Jonah understood what he was looking at. He'd read it in Morrow's book, four lines. He'd seen it in the sand on Founding Day with a rail round it, a prayer over it, and a quaestor on a dais to say who'd won. This was the same, with the rail and the prayer and the dais taken off. A man with something in his hand said mine, and a man with nothing gave it to him. The ones who could have stopped it did not come down.

"That's how it goes," Pell said.

Hask took the boy's water for eleven days.

On the twelfth the boy tried to keep it.

Not much of a try. He'd been on half rations for eleven days. He got his strapped hands around the cup and pulled it to his chest and said, no, once, in a voice that cracked. Hask put the steel into him under the ribs and turned it and took the cup out of his hands while he was still standing.

The boy sat down. He did it carefully. Jonah's hands went cold. Then the boy lay down, and that was less careful. The hold looked at the floor.

Pell had stopped singing.

"That's three," the old man said. "Two in the dark before the lights. Now three. Thirty-one days."

Jonah didn't answer. He was watching Hask.

He hadn't meant to. It was the feet. Hask had gone back to the barrel with the boy's cup and the wet steel and sat down among the Blanks to drink. Something in Jonah's legs looked at how the man sat and had an opinion. Contempt. Not the old contempt that looked at Collars. This was Varro looking at a Blank, at a dock thug with a shard of steel, sitting with his weight on one side and his knife hand slack. That one is nothing. That one is something you do on your way somewhere else.

It was the most frightening thing Jonah had felt since the box. Because he agreed with it.

---

"Not your business," Pell said, next shift, to the floor.

"I know."

"You're looking at him."

"I know."

"You're a Collar with a line on your arm. Sit by the wall. Take your cup. Get counted. That's how you come out the other end of thirty-one days."

"I know," Jonah said. "I've killed a man already."

Pell was quiet a while.

"Once is something that happens to you," the old man said. "Twice is what you are."

The crew came down for the boy on the count, looked at him, logged him, and left. Between him and the man he took it from meant that too. The body lay by the barrel for a shift with its cup beside it and its eyes open.

Jonah argued with it for a day and a night. With the feet, with what lived under his ribs, with the boy on the floor. On the thirteenth day Hask took another man's water, an old Collar who did not try to keep it. Jonah watched Hask's hands do it and thought, he'll do this all the way to the other end. Everybody here knows it. Everybody here will let him.

On the fourteenth day Hask stood up for the water. The line formed. Jonah was seventh in it, and found the arguing had stopped some time ago without telling him. The deciding had happened in the same place the ring had gone into his arm.

He stepped out of the line.

He walked up the hold past six bound men, past the barrel, to where Hask stood with the cup in one hand and the steel in the other. He stopped close, and held out his strapped hands, palms up, empty.

"Mine," he said.

The hold went still.

Hask took a moment. He was a slow man. He had been the only thing in the hold for fourteen days and had stopped expecting anything to look back. He looked at Jonah's hands, Jonah's face, the line on Jonah's arm. Then he grinned. Almost friendly. A man handed something to do.

"Yeah?"

"Yeah."

Hask put the cup on the barrel and came with the steel.

He was not fast. The feet had known that and Jonah hadn't wanted to believe it. He came in straight, steel low, weight on the front foot, other hand out to grab. It had worked on a sixteen-year-old. So it was what he did.

The feet had already left. Left and a little forward, off the line, the step Livia had named in the square. There wasn't room for more; the hold was two paces wide between the sitting men. Hask's steel went past his hip. Hask's hand closed on nothing. The big man's weight went on past the place Jonah had been. Jonah's strapped hands came up together and took the knife wrist as it went by.

He turned it. There was a way to turn a wrist when a man's weight was going past you and both your hands were on it. Varro had known it. Now Jonah's hands did. Hask's arm went where arms do not go. There was a sound. The steel dropped out of the slack fingers into Jonah's palms. Hask was on his knees, mouth open, arm at the wrong angle, and his face, finally, looking at something.

Jonah put the steel into him under the ear.

He did not choose the place. The steel went in where his hands put it, easily. Hask made a sound that was mostly air. Jonah pulled the steel out and stepped back. The big man went over sideways among the Blanks by the barrel, and one of them shifted his legs aside.

Four seconds. Less.

He stood in the middle of the hold with sharpened hull steel between his strapped hands, a dead Blank at his feet, and his heart going. Behind the heart something turned over, small, the same as it had in the sand. His hands went steady. He hated them for it.

Nobody spoke.

Eight Blanks by the barrel now. They looked at him with the faces of men recalculating a sum, and none of them moved. The Collars along the walls looked at him too, and their faces were different. He didn't look long.

He put the steel down on the barrel. He filled the cup with both hands and walked back down the hold to the old Collar from the thirteenth day, and crouched, and held it out.

The old Collar looked at the cup. Then at him.

"It's yours," Jonah said. "It was always yours."

The old man took it. His hands shook. He drank.

Jonah went back to his place and sat down beside Pell. Pell had not moved through any of it.

"Four," Pell said, to the floor.

"Four."

"That's how it goes."

"Yeah."

Pell was quiet a moment.

"You gave it back."

"It was his."

"Nobody gives it back." Pell turned his head and looked at Jonah properly for the first time. "Sixty years on the Limes. I've seen the sand. I've seen this. Nobody gives it back. You take the water, you keep the knife, you sit by the barrel. That's what the knife's for." He looked at Jonah's empty hands. "You left it on the barrel."

"I don't want it."

"Everybody wants it."

"Then everybody can have it. I've got what I've got."

Pell looked at him a while longer. Then he turned back to the floor and began to sing again, very quietly, a field song from Marl, and did not stop until the shift changed.

The crew came down on the count. They looked at Hask, logged him, and left. Jonah watched them go up the ladder and thought about the sergeant on Anvil, who had said please. These four had not, and did not need to. The law had been said once, out loud. After that it said itself.

---

He didn't sleep that shift. He sat against the wall and looked at his hands.

His hands. He'd had them all his life. They had just broken a man's arm and put steel under his ear in four seconds without asking him, and afterwards they had been pleased. Steady and warm and pleased. Varro's pleasure. It was in him now, and it was his.

He made himself think about that.

He could not take it out. It had come from a dead man and it liked what it did. He was going to have to decide, every time, whether to let it out. Deciding was the only part of the whole business that would ever be his.

That was as far as he got. His hands were steady and he wished they were not.

Across the hold, the Thinstripe was looking at him again.

He'd been looking since the water. He was an officer, or had been. He had watched a bound Collar step out of a line and put a man down with a wrist-turn they taught in garrison schools. His hit face had gone through several things and settled on one, and the one was not fear.

Recognition.

Jonah looked back. After a moment the Thinstripe nodded, once, slightly. Then he turned back to the ladder and did not look at Jonah again for the rest of the voyage.

Jonah kept that with the rest. There was a lot in there now. His father's face at the door. Mara's, not looking away. A cup of the best water on Anvil, and a sister asleep believing the fourth bell. Ochoa on his back: you shouldn't have touched it. Varro, surprised. A boy from Marl who'd said no once and sat down carefully. Hask.

He'd stopped trying to put them in order. He just kept them.

---

On the thirty-first day the drive changed.

The note through the deck went down. Then the transport began to turn, slowly, lining up on something. Thirty-eight men sat up along the walls.

The crew came down for the last Hush, did it, and stood at the foot of the ladder with their rifles.

"Metalla Seven," the one who spoke said. "Intake in one hour. On the ramp you'll be read and you'll be cut and you'll be given a number. After that the number's what you are. Anybody with anything on him, leave it on the deck now. They take it at the Throat and they don't ask nicely." He paused. "Thirty-one days. Wasn't so bad."

He went up.

Metalla Seven. Everybody on the Limes had heard the name. The mines. Ad metalla, the grey Blank had said, in the corridor under the quaestor's house, and had not said whose order. Now the order had a name.

Beside him, Pell had stopped singing.

"You know it," Jonah said.

"Everybody knows it." The old man's voice was flat. "My brother went. Thirty years back. A field, same as me."

"Did he write?"

"They don't come back, boy. It's not that kind of place. It's the kind of place where they dig, and what they dig at digs back. When it's done with you they count you and that's the end of it." He looked at his strapped hands. "Three years if you're clever. Two if you're strong. My brother was strong."

Jonah didn't answer.

Under his ribs, it had been quiet for thirty-one days. Lying down, watching, hungry. In the last hour, as the drive changed and the transport turned, it had done something it had not done since the box.

It had sat up.

Not for him. Not for the crew or the Hush or Hask. It had sat up and pointed, through the deck, through the hull, at whatever the transport was turning toward. There was no hunger in the pointing, and no interest. Nothing he had a name for.

If he had to say, it was how you'd look at a door you had once walked out of.

The drive cut. The ramp lights came on, red, over the doors. Thirty-eight men stood up along the walls and Jonah stood with them. The ramp began to come down. The smell reached them before the light did. Cold, mineral, wrong. A cut in rock that had never healed.

And the thing under his ribs, all at once and with everything it had, wanted to go home.
