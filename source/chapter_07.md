# Chapter 7: Ad Metalla

There were forty-one of them in the hold when the transport left Anvil, and thirty-eight when it arrived, and Jonah had killed one of the three.

He would count it that way for the rest of his life. Not the number of the dead. The number of the ones he'd made. The old man would tell him, later, that it was the only honest way to count and that most men never learned it, and Jonah would say, I didn't learn it, I just couldn't stop.

---

The hold was a box.

He'd been in it three hours before the lights came up, and when they did he saw what he'd been sitting among. It was a cargo hold, the same as the Kestrel's, the same as any hull's, a long steel box with rings in the floor for strapping down freight, and the freight was men. Forty of them. They sat where they'd been put, along the walls and down the middle, with their hands bound in front of them with the same straps, and most of them had collars and some of them had lines on their arms and one of them, near the ramp, had a thin purple stripe and a face that had been hit recently and often.

Nobody spoke. That was the first thing. Forty men in a box and the only sound was breathing and the drive.

Jonah looked at them and they looked at him, the way you look at a new man, to see what he was, and he saw them decide. Collar. Young. A line on the arm, so a Blank, so a Collar who'd been given a line and had lost it in a week, which was a thing to laugh at if anyone had been laughing. Bound. Nothing.

He let them decide it. He sat with his back to the wall and his hands in his lap and the ring on his finger, which they had not taken, because the grey Blanks who put him in the car had not been the kind who took things, and he thought about the ring.

They would take it at the other end. He knew that the way he knew the ramp would come down. Wherever the other end was, it would have a Flamen at it and a glass and a man with a list, and the first thing that happened to a man on a list was that everything he had came off him, and the ring was a thing he had.

He turned it on his finger.

The thing under his ribs was watching him do it. It had been watching since the car. It had come up when the grey Blanks took him and had not gone down, and it was in his arms now, not ready, not the way it had been in his father's room, but there, the way a dog is there in a room, lying down, with its eyes open. It was hungry. It was always hungry. And it was looking at the ring on his finger the way it had looked at the yoke in the rings, with that flat, total interest that it gave to anything the body did that it had not seen the body do before.

"It's hers," he said, under his breath. "It stays."

The interest didn't change. He hadn't expected it to. But something else happened, and he felt it happen and did not understand it for a moment, and then did. The plate came up on the inside of his left forearm. Not out. Up. A patch of it, under the skin, dark, the size of a coin, and it lay there, and it was soft.

He looked at it. He looked at the ring.

He didn't decide. He'd stopped pretending he decided things. He took the ring off and pressed it, with his thumb, against the soft dark place on the inside of his arm, and the plate took it. It took it the way water takes a stone. The ring went down through the skin without breaking it and the plate closed over it and went back to being skin, and there was a lump, small, hard, under the flesh of his left forearm, where a bone was not, and it was the Halcyon, and it was hers, and it was staying.

He sat back against the wall with his heart going.

Across the hold, the Thinstripe with the hit face was looking at him.

---

The crew came down at the sixth hour and did the Hush.

He hadn't known it was called that. He learned it from the man beside him, an old Collar from Marl with a farmer's hands and a way of saying things to nobody in particular that Jonah would come to know very well in the years after. They'll Hush us, the old man said, to the floor, before the crew came. They do it every shift. Puts the Blanks to sleep. Not the men, the things in the men. Watch the Stripe. He hates it.

The crew were four Blanks in grey with a Flamen's mark on the shoulder, and they came down the ladder from the cabin with rifles and stood at the foot of it, and one of them said a word.

It was not the word. It was another one, in the old Latin, and it went through the hold like a draught, and Jonah watched it go. He watched it reach the Blanks with lines on their arms, one after another, and watched each of them flinch, a small thing, a shoulder going up, and then go slack, not their bodies but something behind their bodies, like a lamp going down. He watched it reach the Thinstripe by the ramp and watched the man's jaw lock and his hit face go white and then grey, and watched him hate it, the way the old man had said, hate it with his whole body, and go slack anyway.

Then it reached Jonah.

He felt it. It came to him the way the word had come in his father's room, aimed, meant for the line on his arm, meant for a stupid loyal friend fused at nine that would hear it and lie down. And it went through him and found the thing under his ribs and the thing under his ribs looked at it, with that flat interest, the way it had looked at the collar's word in the Kestrel's hold, and let it pass.

Jonah flinched. He did it on purpose, a shoulder going up, and let his eyes go dull, and sat, and the crewman's gaze went over him and on.

"Water," the crewman said. "Twice a shift. Ration's by the ring. One cup, one man, one ring. Anyone takes two, it's between him and the man he took it from. We don't come down for that. We come down for the Hush and the count and that's all." He looked along the hold. "Thirty-one days. Make it easy."

They put a barrel by the ladder with a cup chained to it, and went up.

---

Thirty-one days.

He learned the hold the way he'd learned the back room in the tenements, by lying in it in the dark and paying attention. He learned that the old man from Marl was called Pell and had been condemned for a field that had not made its quota, and that he had a song for every field and sang them, quietly, to the floor, at the change of shift. He learned that the Thinstripe by the ramp was called Aulus Something and had been a garrison officer on Marl and had killed a man he should not have killed in a sharpening, a Blank with the wrong friends, and had been stripped of his licence and his office and would be stripped of the rest at the other end, and knew it, and sat by the ramp with his hit face and his dead stripe and looked at the ladder with an expression Jonah recognised from the inside. He learned that there were nine Blanks in the hold and thirty-one Collars, and that the Blanks sat together, by the barrel, and that the Collars did not.

He learned the water.

Twice a shift, one cup, one man, one ring. The crew had said it and gone up, and the first shift it had been that, forty men in a line with their bound hands out, and the cup going down the line, and the Blanks first because they were nearest the barrel and because they were Blanks. The second shift it was the same. On the third shift a Collar boy from Marl, sixteen, who had been crying since the lights came up, was fourth in the line, and a Blank named Hask, who was big and had a line on his arm and something in his hand, put the something against the boy's neck and said, "Mine."

The boy gave it to him. Of course he gave it to him. There was a piece of hull steel against his neck, sharpened on the deck rings until it had an edge, and the boy gave up his cup and went and sat down, and nobody said anything, and the crew did not come down. It was between him and the man he took it from. That was the law, and the law had been said out loud, and Jonah, sitting against the wall with a ring under the skin of his arm, understood what he was looking at.

He'd read it in Morrow's book. Four lines. He'd seen it in the sand on Founding Day with a rail around it and a prayer over it and a quaestor on a dais to say who'd won. He'd never seen it like this, with the rail and the prayer and the dais taken off, and it was the same thing, and it was simpler than he'd thought. A man with something in his hand said mine, and a man with nothing gave it to him, and the ones who could have stopped it did not come down, because it was between the two of them.

Hask took the boy's water for eleven days.

On the twelfth the boy tried to keep it.

It was not much of a try. He was sixteen and he had been drinking half rations for eleven days and he got his bound hands around the cup and pulled it toward his chest and said, no, once, in a voice that cracked, and Hask put the steel into him under the ribs and turned it and took the cup out of his hands while he was still standing.

The boy sat down. He did it carefully. Jonah watched him do it and felt his own hands go cold, and then the boy lay down, and that was less careful, and the hold looked at the floor.

Pell, beside him, had stopped singing.

"That's three," the old man said, to nobody. "Two in the dark before the lights. Now three. Thirty-one days. That's how it goes."

Jonah didn't say anything.

He was watching Hask.

He hadn't meant to. It was the feet. Hask had gone back to the barrel with the boy's cup and the wet steel and had sat down among the Blanks and was drinking, and something in Jonah's legs was looking at how the man sat, and had an opinion, and the opinion was contempt. Not the old contempt, the one that had come up his arm with the footwork and looked at Collars. This was Varro looking at a Blank, at a dock thug with a shard of steel and a line on his arm, sitting with his weight all on one side and his knife hand slack, and thinking, that one. That one is nothing. That one is a thing you do on your way somewhere else.

It was the most frightening thing Jonah had felt since the box.

Because he agreed with it.

---

He did not do it that shift, or the next. He wanted that understood, when he told it. He sat against the wall for a day and a night and argued with it, with the feet and with the thing under his ribs and with the fact of the boy on the floor, whom the crew had come down for on the count, and looked at, and logged, and left, because between him and the man he took it from meant that too, it meant the body stayed where it fell until the next Hush, and the boy lay by the barrel for a shift with his cup beside him and his eyes open.

He argued that it was not his business. He argued that he was a Collar with a line on his arm and a thing in him that no page had a word for, and that the only way he came out of the other end of thirty-one days alive was to be nothing, sit against a wall, take his cup, be counted. He argued that he'd already killed a man and that once was a thing that had happened to you and twice was a thing you were.

He argued, and the old man from Marl sang to the floor, and Hask took another man's water on the thirteenth day, an old Collar this time, who did not try to keep it, and Jonah watched his hands do it and thought, he's going to do this all the way to the other end, and everybody in this hold knows it, and everybody in this hold is going to let him, because it's between him and the man he took it from.

And on the fourteenth day, when Hask stood up for the water and the line formed and Jonah was seventh in it, he found that the arguing had stopped some time ago without telling him, and that he had already decided, and that the deciding had happened in the same place the ring had gone into his arm.

He stepped out of the line.

He did it quietly. He walked up the hold past six bound men, past the barrel, to where Hask was standing with the cup in one hand and the steel in the other, waiting for the boy's replacement to come to him, and he stopped in front of the big Blank, close, and held out his bound hands, palms up, empty.

"Mine," he said.

The hold went still.

Hask looked at him. It took him a moment. He was a slow man, was Hask, and he had been the only thing in the hold for fourteen days and had stopped expecting anything to look back at him. He looked at Jonah's hands and Jonah's face and the line on Jonah's arm, and then he grinned, and it was not a bad grin, it was almost friendly, the grin of a man who has been handed a thing to do.

"Yeah?" he said.

"Yeah."

Hask put the cup down on the barrel and came at him with the steel.

He was not fast. That was the thing the feet had known and Jonah had not wanted to believe. He came in the way a big man comes in, straight, with the steel low and his weight on the front foot and his other hand out to grab, and it was what he'd done to a sixteen-year-old boy who'd never held anything, and it had worked, and so it was what he did.

The feet had already left.

Not far. There wasn't room; the hold was two paces wide between the sitting men. Left, and a little forward, off the line, the step Livia had named in the square, and Hask's steel went past his hip and Hask's grabbing hand closed on nothing and the big man's weight went on past the place where Jonah had been and Jonah's bound hands came up together, the way bound hands have to, and took Hask's knife wrist as it went by.

He turned it. He didn't know how. There was a way you turned a wrist, if you had a man's weight going past you and both your hands on it, and Varro had known the way and now Jonah's hands did, and Hask's arm went the way arms do not go, and there was a sound, and the steel came out of the slack fingers into Jonah's, between his palms, still bound, and Hask was on his knees on the deck with his mouth open and his arm at the wrong angle and his face, finally, looking at something.

Jonah put the steel into him.

He did it under the ear. He did not choose the place. He'd have said, later, that he had, that he'd picked the place where the neck seam was on a plated man because that was the place the feet knew, and it would have been a lie, and the old man would have heard it. The steel went in where his hands put it, and it went in easily, the way the fang had gone into Varro, like a hand into warm water, and Hask made a sound that was mostly air, and Jonah took the steel out and stepped back, and the big man went over sideways among the Blanks by the barrel, and one of them moved his legs out of the way.

That was all. Four seconds. Less.

Jonah stood in the middle of the hold with a piece of sharpened hull steel between his bound hands and a dead Blank at his feet and his heart going, and inside him, behind the heart, something turned over, small, like a lock, the way it had in the sand, and he felt his hands go steady, and hated them for it.

Nobody spoke.

He looked at the Blanks by the barrel. There were eight of them now and they were looking at him with the faces of men recalculating a sum, and none of them moved. He looked at the Collars along the walls. They were looking at him too, and their faces were different, and he did not want to know what was in them, so he didn't look long.

He looked at the cup on the barrel.

He put the steel down beside it. He picked up the cup with both hands, and filled it from the barrel, and walked back down the hold to where the old Collar sat, the one from the thirteenth day, and crouched, and held it out.

The old Collar looked at it. Then at him.

"It's yours," Jonah said. "It was always yours."

The old man took it. His hands were shaking. He drank, and Jonah went back to his place against the wall and sat down beside Pell, who had not moved through any of it, and who was looking at the floor, and who said, after a while, to the floor, "Four."

"Four," Jonah agreed.

"That's how it goes."

"Yeah."

Pell was quiet for a moment.

"You gave it back," the old man said.

"It was his."

"Nobody gives it back." Pell turned his head and looked at Jonah for the first time, properly, with his farmer's eyes. "I've been on the Limes sixty years. I've seen the sand. I've seen this. Nobody gives it back. You take the water, you keep the knife, you sit by the barrel. That's what the knife's for." He looked at Jonah's empty hands. "You left it on the barrel."

"I don't want it."

"Everybody wants it."

"Then everybody can have it," Jonah said. "I've got what I've got."

Pell looked at him a while longer. Then he turned back to the floor and, after a time, began to sing again, very quietly, a field song from Marl, and did not stop until the shift changed.

The crew came down on the count and looked at Hask and logged him and left. Between him and the man he took it from. Jonah watched them go up the ladder and thought about the sergeant on Anvil, who had said please, and about these four, who had not, and who did not need to, because the law had been said once, out loud, and after that it said itself.

---

He didn't sleep that shift. He sat against the wall and looked at his hands.

They were his hands. He'd had them all his life. And they had just broken a man's arm and put steel under his ear in four seconds without asking him, and had been pleased about it after, steady and warm and pleased, and it was Varro's pleasure, and it was in him now, and it was his.

He thought about that. He made himself.

The old man with the stick would say, later, that this was the night. Not the box, not the hold of the Kestrel. This one. The night Jonah had sat in a transport hold and looked at his own hands and understood that there was something in them that had come from a dead man and liked what it did, and that he could not take it out, and that he was going to have to decide, every time, whether to let it out or not, and that deciding was the only thing about the whole business that would ever be his.

He hadn't known that yet. He only knew that his hands were steady and he wished they were not.

Across the hold, the Thinstripe by the ramp was looking at him again.

He'd been looking since the water. He was an officer, or had been, and he had watched a bound Collar step out of a line and put a man down with a wrist-turn that they taught in garrison schools, and his hit face had gone through several things and settled on one, and the one was not fear.

It was recognition.

Jonah looked back at him. After a moment the Thinstripe nodded, once, slightly, the way one man nods to another across a room when they have both seen the same thing, and turned back to the ladder, and did not look at Jonah again for the rest of the voyage.

Jonah thought about that too. He put it with the rest.

There was a lot in there, now. His father's face at the door. Mara's, not looking away. A cup of the best water on Anvil. The quaestor's hands on a packet, and the fourth bell, and a sister asleep believing it. Ochoa, on his back, saying, you shouldn't have touched it. Varro, surprised. A sixteen-year-old boy from Marl who had said no once in a cracked voice and sat down carefully. Hask.

He'd stopped trying to put them in order. He just kept them.

---

On the thirty-first day the drive changed.

He felt it before the crew announced it. The note through the deck went down, and then the barge, the transport, whatever it was, began to turn, slowly, the way a barge turned when it was lining up on something, and the forty men who'd become thirty-eight sat up along the walls the way men do when a thing that has been a long way off stops being a long way off.

The crew came down for the last Hush. They did it and stood at the foot of the ladder with their rifles and looked at the hold, and the one who spoke said, "Metalla Seven. Intake in one hour. On the ramp you'll be read and you'll be cut and you'll be given a number, and after that the number's what you are. Anybody with anything on him, leave it on the deck now. They take it at the Throat and they don't ask nicely." He paused. "Thirty-one days. Wasn't so bad."

He went up.

Metalla Seven. Jonah had heard the name. Everybody on the Limes had heard the name, the way you hear the name of a thing that is under the ground. The mines. Ad metalla, the grey Blank had said, in the corridor under the quaestor's house, and had not said whose order, and now the order had a name.

Beside him, Pell had stopped singing.

"You know it," Jonah said.

"Everybody knows it." The old man's voice was flat. "My brother went. Thirty years back. A field, same as me. They don't come back, boy. It's not that kind of place. It's the kind of place where they dig, and what they dig at digs back, and when it's done with you they count you and that's the end of it." He looked at his bound hands. "Three years if you're clever. Two if you're strong. My brother was strong."

Jonah didn't say anything.

He was thinking about the thing under his ribs. It had been quiet for thirty-one days, lying down, watching, hungry. And in the last hour, as the drive changed and the transport turned, it had done something it had not done since the box.

It had sat up.

Not for him. Not for the crew, not for the Hush, not for Hask. It had sat up the way a dog sits up in a house when, far off, something it knows is coming down the road. It was pointed. He could feel it pointing, through the deck, through the hull, at whatever the transport was turning toward, and there was nothing in the pointing that was hunger, and nothing that was interest, and nothing he had a name for at all.

It was, if he had to say, something like the way you'd look at a door you'd once walked out of.

The drive cut. The ramp lights came on, red, over the doors. Thirty-eight men stood up along the walls, because that was what you did, and Jonah stood up with them, and the ramp began to come down, and there was a smell in the air before there was light, cold, mineral, wrong, like a cut in a rock that had never healed.

And the thing under his ribs, all at once and with everything it had, wanted to go home.
