# Chapter 19: The Riot

The Deep stopped giving slabs eleven days after the wall went loud, and the number Jonah had built for Rennick came apart the way a hull comes apart, all at once, from a crack nobody had looked at.

He'd known it would. He'd felt it the morning after, cutting at the face with plate stuck under his gloves: the slab had fought the steel. Not for him; it never fought him. For Ulli, beside him, and for Harrow, and for every deep-digger in the cut. The Vein had been bitten and had turned all of itself toward the bite, and where it had turned it had closed, the pale lines drawn tight the way skin draws tight over a wound, and a slab that had come off in an hour came off in three, and then in a shift, and then not at all.

The Deep gave shards. By the piece. Like the Shallows.

Rennick carried nine up the Throat on the twelfth day, by weight, and Tullus read it, and looked at him, and said, quietly, in front of the garrison hall, that the old count had said eleven by the piece and the new count said nine by weight and that one of the two counts was a lie, and that he would like to know which, and Rennick, who had had the voice since he was five, did not have it then.

The count went to fourteen. By the piece. Every decury. From tomorrow.

And Sabinus came down the Throat to remind the Deep.

---

"Fourteen," Bricks said. He said it once. He'd stopped saying things twice.

"Fourteen," Decimus read, off the list, in the voice for things that were not personal, and it was not personal, and his hand on the list was not steady, and Jonah saw that and put it with the rest.

The road did not say anything. Sixty men on the rock of the Shallows with their bowls, and Auger at the edge of the light with his arms folded, and nobody said anything, because the wall gave ten and had given ten for twenty years and fourteen was not a number, it was a strap, and everybody could count.

They made ten the first day. Decury six made eleven, because the foreman cut two shards and broke one, and Jonah stood at the post that dusk and chose Hollen, and gave him ten, and Hollen said nothing, and the decury beside them made nine and its foreman chose a boy who had come down with the last transport, and the boy screamed, and the decury beside that one made eight and there was no post for eight, there was the other thing, and Decimus did not come down for it, and in the morning there were fifty-nine on the road.

Sabinus did not come to the Shallows. Sabinus was in the Deep. But the road heard him. It heard him the way it had heard the wall go loud, through the rock, as a thing that had changed, and Ulli came up the road on the second night to carry a man down who had not made a shard, and stopped at the seventh chamber where Jonah was cutting, and said, without any weight:

"He's killed four."

"Who?"

"Diggers. At the face. For slabs that wouldn't come." The deep man's face did nothing. "Not the post. The fang. He walks the cut and he says, slab, and if the slab doesn't come he puts it through the seam at the neck, the way you'd cut a rope, and he says, next. Four. Harrow's stopped counting them. Harrow says it's a harvest." Ulli picked up his bucket. "I thought you'd want to know what's coming up the road."

Jonah stood at the wall with his chisel.

He counted. He did it the way the old man had said, all at once, without looking. Fifty-nine men on the road and a wall that gave ten and a number that said fourteen and a Centurion in the Deep cutting throats for slabs that would not come because a cut one had put his hand on the Vein and let a thing eat. He counted that too. He did not put it away. He counted Auger at the edge of the light, who had held the road nineteen years by making it run, and who was looking at a road that could not run, and he counted what a man like that did with that.

"He's going to do something," Jonah said to the old man that night, in the Cradle, with the stick coming at him from the dark and the plate finally, after nine days, gone back into his arms.

"Yes."

"You know what."

"I know Auger. I have known Auger nineteen years through a wall. He is a fair man who runs a road, and a road that cannot run is a road that will be decimated whatever he does, and he knows the arithmetic as well as I do, and a man who knows he is going to be counted whatever he does will try, once, to choose the count." The stick took him across the ribs and he went down and got up. "He will take the cage. Not to leave; nobody leaves. To hold the Shallows and the bottom of the Throat, so that nothing goes up and nothing comes down, and Tullus cannot harvest, and has to talk. That is what a riot is. It is a man with no cards putting the one he has on the table so that the other man has to look at it."

"It won't work."

"No. Tullus will send Sabinus down the Throat with the guard, and Sabinus will do what he is doing in the Deep, and then Tullus will line the road up and count it, and one in ten will go to the post and not come off it. That is what a decimation is. It is very old. They had it on the Old Ground; it is in the library the Roma ark carried. It is the most Roman thing in this Pit." The old man's face in the lamp was the stone. "And it will happen whether you are in it or not, and the only thing you get to choose is where you are standing when it does."

"Where should I stand?"

"Wherever the fewest men die," the Censor said. "That is the only answer I have ever had to that question and I did not learn it in time. Get up."

---

Auger came to the seventh chamber on the fourth night.

He came the way he did everything, slowly, without weight, an old broken man with a chisel he had forgotten he was holding, and he stood in the mouth of the chamber where Vetch had stood and looked at decury six at the wall, and at Jonah, and said, "Foreman. A word."

They stood in the road. Auger did not lower his voice; he had not needed to in nineteen years.

"Tomorrow. Change of guard, the fourth bell. There's four on the road and the cage is down and the young one's on it. We take the cage. We take the four. We hold the road and the bottom of the Throat, and Tullus talks or Tullus doesn't harvest." The broken face did not change. "I've counted. I don't need six. I need six to stay in the seventh chamber and not be on the road, because you've a foreman with a Stripe's wrist and a farmer with a garrison chisel and a north-side cutter who's had thirty on his back this week and hasn't made a sound, and I don't want any of you where Sabinus can see what you are. I want you behind a wall. That's the whole of what I'm asking."

"You're going to die."

"I've been dying nineteen years, cut one. I'd like to choose the week." Auger looked at him for a moment, in the way that had watched him break Bricks's wrist. "You've counted it. I can see it on you. You know it doesn't work and you know it's going to happen and you've been standing at that wall four nights working out where to be. I'm telling you where. Behind it. With your men. You've done a thing on this road nobody's done, you've made a decury that doesn't die, and I'd like one thing on this road to be left when it's over." He stopped. "And I'd like you to do one other thing."

"What?"

"The old guard. Decimus." Auger's voice did not change. "He's on the road at the fourth bell. He's always on the road; it's his count. And when we take the cage the young ones will come at us and he won't, he'll stand there with his rifle and say it's not personal, and one of mine will put a chisel in him, because he's a guard and it's a riot and that's what a riot is." The broken face looked at Jonah. "I've watched him twenty-five years. He brought you a bandage. I'd like it if he wasn't on the road. I can't do that. You've a wrist and you've a chamber with a wall in front of it. Do what you can."

He went back to the edge of the light.

Jonah stood in the road.

He'd counted it. Auger was right. He'd counted every way it went and every one of them had a road lined up in the morning and a Centurion walking down it with a fang, and the only number he could change was how many were on the road when the count came. Behind the wall. Six men behind a wall. And an old guard with a letter in his coat, who would stand on a road with a rifle and say it was not personal, and be right, and die of it.

He did not tell Decimus.

He would say that, later, when he told it, and the woman with the broad stripe would say, why not, and he would say: because Decimus would have gone up the Throat and told Tullus, because it was not personal, either way, and Tullus would have sent Sabinus down at the third bell instead of the fourth, and the road would have died a bell early with the cage still up. And she would say, you could have told him to be sick. To be off the road. And he would say, he wouldn't have been. He'd have said, it's my count. And I'd have told him, and he'd have known I knew, and he'd have had to decide, and he'd have decided the count. So I didn't make him.

That's not an excuse, she would say.

No, he would say. It's a number.

---

The fourth bell rang and the road went for the cage.

He was behind the wall. He'd told six at the dusk count, in the road's tongue, flat, the way Bricks would have: tomorrow we cut through the change of guard. Nobody goes to the road. Nobody goes to the bowl. Anybody asks, the foreman said fourteen and fourteen doesn't stop for a bell. And Bricks had looked at him with the small eyes that were not stupid and had said nothing, and Pell had said nothing, and Hollen with thirty on his back had said, "Right," and Corr had looked at the floor.

So they were at the wall when the road went, six men in the seventh chamber with chisels and the Vein breathing in front of them, and the sound came down the road and through the rock the way the wall going loud had come, and it was not one sound. It was sixty men who had decided, and a rifle, and another, and something that Jonah had never heard and knew at once, which was a cage coming down its cable too fast and hitting rock.

Then it was Sabinus.

He heard that too. Everyone did. It came down the Throat as a thing that had changed, the way Ulli had said, and it was the cage going back up, and coming down, and coming down, and each time it came down the sound on the road was different, and Jonah stood at the wall with the chisel and counted the cage and knew what was in it.

"They've got the four," Bricks said. He'd gone to the chamber mouth. "Auger's got the four. And the cage. And they've got." He stopped. "They've got Decimus. He's on his knees at the cage end. Rifle's gone. Auger's man's over him."

Jonah put the chisel down.

"Foreman."

"Stay at the wall."

"Foreman, Sabinus is on the road."

He knew. He'd heard the cage. He went to the chamber mouth and looked, once, down the road, and the road was the transport hold with the rail off and the prayer off and the dais off, sixty men and four guards and a cage, and Auger at the cage end with his arms not folded, and the young guard on the rock with a chisel in his neck, and Decimus on his knees with a Collar over him and the chisel up, and coming down the road from the cage, not hurrying, with six guards behind him in grey plate and a fang out on his right arm as long as a forearm, was a man of middle height with a narrow face and two white scars on his arm, and he was harvesting.

There was no other word. The old man had said it and Harrow had said it and Ulli had said it and Jonah had thought it was a word for the Deep, for slabs. It was this. Sabinus walked down the road at the pace a man walks when he is going somewhere, and men came at him from the walls with chisels, and he did not stop walking. He put the fang through the first one's throat as the man came in, without turning, and through the second one's belly on the backswing, and the third came in low and Sabinus stepped, once, half a step, and the third one's chisel went into the rock where his hip had been and the fang came down on the back of the man's neck like a man cutting a rope, and Sabinus said something, to nobody, that Jonah could not hear and knew.

Next.

Jonah stood in the chamber mouth and watched it and knew, with the whole of the count, that he would last two seconds.

Not the plate. The plate had come up fitted and right at the bottom of the Deep and had put a fang under a dying man's ribs, and the old man had hit it with a stick for nine nights and said ah. It was not the plate. It was the half step. Sabinus had stepped once, half a pace, on a low thrust, and he had not stepped left or right or back, he had stepped the way water steps, into the place the chisel was not, and the man was dead before his feet were done, and Jonah had done that step ninety nights on the rock of the Cradle with a pick haft finding every place he did it wrong, and had never once done it like that.

Two seconds. If he was lucky. The old man had been right about the two seconds.

"Foreman," Bricks said.

"Stay at the wall."

"He's going to."

He was. The Collar over Decimus had looked up the road at Sabinus coming and had made a decision that Jonah understood from forty paces, which was that if he was going to die on this road he was going to take a guard with him, and the chisel came down.

Jonah was already moving.

---

He did not use the fang. He wanted that understood, later, by the one person he told.

He went down the road with a chisel in his right hand and nothing on him, no plate, in the light, past sixty men and a Centurion, and he did it because the alternative was a fang in the light on a road with Sabinus on it, and Sabinus would not have been bored. He went the way the old man had put in his legs, the ugly way, the way that was nobody's, and he was fast, because he was twenty-four on a glass that had no line for him and the body around it had grown into the coat, and he reached the Collar over Decimus as the chisel came down and took the man's wrist.

Not the turn. The pull. He pulled the arm straight and stepped through and the Collar went past him onto the rock, and the chisel went into the rock beside Decimus's head instead of into Decimus, and the old guard looked up from his knees with his lined face gone white and his rifle gone and saw the foreman of six standing over him with a Collar's wrist in one hand and a chisel in the other, and said, "Four four seven one."

"Get up."

"It's my count."

"Get up, Decimus." He hauled him. He hauled him the way Ulli hauled the sick, under the arms, and the old guard came up off the rock and Jonah turned him toward the sleeping gallery, six paces, and pushed, and Decimus went, because a man pushed hard enough goes, and the Collar on the rock got up behind them with his chisel and Jonah turned and put his own in the man's thigh, high, in the meat, and pulled it out, and the man sat down, and would live, and would not follow.

"In," Jonah said. "Under the shelf. Stay there. It's not personal."

Decimus looked at him from the mouth of the gallery for one moment with the face that had waited in the tunnel.

Then he went in.

And Jonah turned around, and Sabinus was twenty paces off, and had stopped walking.

---

He had not stopped for Jonah. Jonah would understand that later, going over it on the rock of the Cradle with the old man silent across from him. Sabinus had stopped because Auger had come down the road to meet him, and Auger was the road, and a Centurion sent down a Throat to end a riot ends it by ending the man who made it, and everything else on the road was a slab that would come or would not.

Auger had his chisel. He had held it for nineteen years the way a man holds a thing he has forgotten, and he held it now like a thing he had just remembered, and he came down the road at Sabinus not fast, an old broken man who had been a Blank once at six and had put a Thinstripe on his back in a legal bout and been unstripped for winning, and Sabinus watched him come with the bored face, and Jonah, twenty paces off with a chisel and no plate, watched them both and thought, in the old tongue, because it was the tongue for slow things: he's going to make him hurry.

He did not know why he thought it. It was the old man's line. A wall that can shed is a man pretending, and a man pretending can be made to hurry. And Auger was not going to win, Auger could not win, Auger was a Collar with a chisel against a Centurion with a licence, and Auger came in the way Bricks had come in on the third night, low, short, a feint at the belly and the other hand for the throat, and Sabinus did the half step, the water step, into the place the chisel was not.

And Auger was there.

It was the only time in his life Jonah saw it. Auger had not feinted at the belly. He had feinted at the feint. He had watched Sabinus do the half step on three men and he had come in exactly so that the half step put the Centurion where Auger's other hand was, and the other hand had a second chisel in it, the ground-down point of Bricks's, and it went into the seam at Sabinus's armpit.

Not far. A finger. Plate was plate, and a Centurion's plate was two bands past anything a chisel was made for, and the point went a finger into the seam and stopped, and Sabinus made a sound, the first sound Jonah had heard him make, and his face stopped being bored.

He hurried.

The fang came round. It came round fast and wrong, not the rope cut, a wild backhand from a man who had felt steel in a seam for the first time in years, and it took Auger across the chest and opened him from shoulder to hip, and the old broken man went down on the rock of the road he had held for nineteen years, and did not get up, and Sabinus stood over him with a finger of chisel in his armpit and the bored face gone and something else on it that Jonah would keep, and would think about for two years on the rock of the Cradle, and would use.

Fear. Not of Auger. Of the seam.

"Next," Sabinus said, and pulled the chisel out of himself, and looked up the road.

Jonah was not on it.

He was in the dark. He'd gone the moment Auger's second hand came up; he'd gone sideways, into the cut between the sleeping gallery and the seventh chamber, where the lamps did not reach and the rock was whole dark, and he'd gone because the count said that a Centurion who had just been made to hurry would want, very badly, to put his fang through something, and the nearest something was a foreman with a chisel and a white line on his arm standing in the light.

And in the dark, two of the six guards came for him.

They'd seen him go. They were young and they were Tiro and they had grey plate and short fangs and they had watched their Centurion bleed, and they came into the cut after the Collar who'd run, two of them, with a lamp, and the lamp showed them the cut, and the cut was empty.

The plate came up.

Jonah did not bring it up. He'd stopped pretending he decided things a long time ago, and he'd stopped, since the bottom of the Deep, pretending that the plate was a thing he brought. It came up on him in the whole dark of the cut the way it had at the face, fitted, right, everywhere, and the crooked fang came out of the back of his right wrist, and the two young guards with the lamp looked at a place in the dark where a thing was standing that was darker than the dark, and one of them said, "What," and that was all he said.

Jonah put the fang through the throat seam. Not the way Varro had died, by accident, on instinct. The way the old man had put it in his hands with a stick: the seam is there, it is a finger wide, it is where the plate on the chest meets the plate on the jaw, and you do not aim for it, you know where it is and your hand goes there the way your hand goes to a bucket handle. The guard's plate came off him in a shiver and he sat down.

The second one had a fang out. He was quick, quicker than Vetch, and he came in low the way the garrison taught, and Jonah did the half step.

He did it. He would tell the old man that and the old man would say, no, you did a step, and it was half of one, and it was not the water, and Jonah would say, it was enough. It was enough. He stepped the way he had watched Sabinus step three times in the light, not left or right or back but into the place the fang was not, and it was ugly and it was late and the young guard's grey fang went past his hip close enough to skid on plate, and the crooked fang went into the seam at the armpit, all the way, not a finger, and the guard dropped the lamp.

It did not go out. It never did. It lay on the rock of the cut and threw its light up the wall and Jonah stood in it, in full plate, with two dead Blanks at his feet and a Centurion twenty paces off on a road that was screaming, and the thing under his ribs, fed, plated, unafraid, looked at the two cuttings in the two dead arms and leaned.

"No," Jonah said, aloud, in the old tongue. "Not here. He'd smell it."

It did not argue. It had never argued. But it did not stop leaning either, and he stood in the dark and held it off two dead men's loricae with the whole of the count, because a Centurion with a licence could feel a genius eat the way a man could feel a draught, and the road was twenty paces away, and Sabinus had just learned what fear was and would be looking for something to do about it.

He put the plate away.

It went. That was the thing he would keep from the cut. After nine days stuck on his arms and a week of not lying and waiting, it went, all at once, clean, the way it had gone in the Kestrel's hold, drawn in from everywhere to the middle and past the middle, because the middle was whole. He had told the old man. He had told Decimus to get under a shelf and told the road nothing and told himself the truth about all of it, and the seam was a scar now and not a tear, and the plate went in over it like water over a stone.

Something went over inside him. Not small. He stood in the whole dark with his hands empty and the fever starting from the middle and counted it, unus, duo, and thought, that's strain. That's the third time in a week. And then he picked up the lamp and put his hands in the nearest bucket, up to the wrist, in the shards, the way the rock had taught him on the ninth night of the haul, so that the last of the plate's dark went out of his skin under the Vein's dark, and walked out of the cut into the light with a bucket in his arms, a foreman carrying shards through a riot, and nobody looked at him twice, because there was nothing on the road to look at but Sabinus.

---

It was over by the sixth bell.

He'd known it would be. The road had taken the cage and held it for two bells and then Sabinus had come down it and it had stopped being a road and become the Deep with the slabs cut out, and by the sixth bell there were forty-one men on the rock of the Shallows who could stand and eighteen who could not, and Auger was one of the eighteen, and the cage went up with Sabinus in it and came down with Tullus.

He'd never seen the prefect. Nobody on the road had. Tullus was a name that came down the Throat as numbers, and the man who came down the cage at the sixth bell was fifty, and grey, and had a narrow stripe on his arm with one white line above it, and a face that had never made a licence, and he walked down the road with Rennick behind him and did not look at the dead.

"Line," Tullus said.

They lined. Forty-one men along the rock of the Shallows, the ones who could stand, with the ones who could not on the rock behind them, and decury six in the middle of the line with the foreman at one end of it and Pell at the other and Corr between, and Decimus, who had come out from under the shelf when the cage went up and had found his rifle and had been standing at the cage end with it since, with his lined face gone grey and the young guard's body at his feet, standing where a guard stood.

"Count them," Tullus said.

Rennick counted. He went down the line with his slate and his stripe and his voice that had gone since the count came apart, and he said the numbers, and the men said them back, and when he came to Jonah he stopped for a moment, with the slate, and looked at him, at the cut one who had been a purser's pilot and had shown him a count by weight, and his face did a thing, and he said, "Four four seven one," and went on.

"Forty-one," Rennick said at the end.

"One in ten." Tullus said it to the road. He said it the way Decimus said the count, in the voice for things that were not personal, and it was not, and Jonah understood, watching him, that the prefect had been trimmed once and had watched it done to himself in a hall with the guard watching and had decided that day what a number was. "Every tenth man. From the cage end. Rennick."

Rennick went down the line again.

He touched them. That was how it was done; the old man had said, it is very old, it is in the library, they touched them. The deputy prefect walked down forty-one men on a rock and touched every tenth one on the shoulder, and the touched ones stepped forward, and the first was a Deep man Jonah did not know, and the second was Hollen.

Hollen stepped forward with thirty on his back and looked at nothing.

The third was a boy from the last transport. The fourth was Bricks.

Bricks stepped forward with his one hand and his small eyes that were not stupid and looked at Jonah down the line, once, and Jonah looked back, and there was nothing in the foreman's wide face that asked for anything, and that was the worst of it.

Rennick came down the line. Nine. Ten. He touched a man Jonah did not know. Nineteen. Twenty. Hollen. Twenty-nine. Thirty. Bricks had been thirty. Thirty-one, thirty-two, Pell, thirty-three, Corr, thirty-four, thirty-five. He counted, the way he counted everything, all at once, without looking, and it came out, and it came out to four four seven one, and he had known since the first man stepped that it would, because he had put himself at the end of the decury when they lined, because that was where a foreman stood, and a foreman who could count had known where the tenth would fall and had stood in it.

Rennick stopped in front of him.

The deputy prefect's hand came up. It stopped. It stayed there, in the air, the width of a hand from Jonah's shoulder, and Rennick looked at him with the face of a man who had been afraid of Tullus for fifteen years and had just, on a rock at the bottom of the Throat, found something he was more afraid of, and Jonah did not know what it was, and then he did.

Tullus was looking at a slate.

Not Rennick's. Another one, that the prefect had taken out of his coat, and it was the thin dark slab that came out of the Chamber once a month, the numbers that kept the galleries from falling, and Tullus was reading it, at the bottom of the Throat, in front of forty-one men, with his grey face doing nothing, and then he looked up, and looked down the line at the cut one with the deputy's hand a finger from his shoulder, and said:

"Not that one."

Rennick's hand did not move.

"Not that one, Rennick. The galleries." Tullus put the slate back in his coat. "Next."

The hand moved. It went past Jonah's shoulder, to the man beside him, and touched it, and Corr stepped forward.

He stepped forward the way he had walked to the post. Without looking at the floor. He'd looked at the floor for three years and he did not look at it now; he stepped out of the line and stood with Hollen and Bricks and the Deep man and the boy, and he turned his head, once, and looked at Jonah, with the wall's dark on his face that had been on it for four years, and his mouth moved, and it was not a month.

Nothing personal, Corr said. Without sound.

Then the guards took the five of them up the road to the post at the cage end, and Sabinus came down the cage with his armpit bound and his face not bored, and it was not the post.

---

He told the old man all of it.

It took the first hour and the second, on the rock of the Cradle, in the old tongue, from the cage coming down too fast to the sound the road made when Sabinus put the fang through Corr, and the Censor sat across from him with the lamp and the stick on his knees and did not say anything until it was done, and did not say anything for a while after.

"The slate," Jonah said.

"Yes."

"Tullus read it. In the line. And he said not that one."

"Yes."

"That was you."

The old man looked at the lamp.

"Tullus came down the road at the fourth bell to my door with a key and a lamp," he said, "and stood where you stood on the night you opened it, and said, the Deep has stopped giving and there is a riot on the Shallows and I am going to count the road, and I need the galleries to hold through it, and he handed me a slate. And I wrote on it. I wrote the numbers that keep the galleries from falling, which are the only thing I have ever had to sell in this rock and which I have sold him once a month for twenty years for a lamp and a bowl. And at the bottom of the slate I wrote: four four seven one is the only man on the Shallows who can cut a slab, and the Deep has stopped, and if you count him you will be carrying nine up the Throat by the piece until the war ends. I did not write that you were mine. He does not know that you are. He knows that the galleries hold when I say they hold and that I do not lie about numbers, and he read it, and he counted the line, and he spent the one thing I had." The Censor's voice was the stone. "And the tenth man was Corr."

Jonah sat on the rock.

"You knew that."

"I knew that a tenth man is a tenth man. I did not know his name. I know it now; you have told me; I will write it." The old man did not look up. "You are going to say that I chose. You are right. I chose. I have chosen once before in my life with a slate in my hand and I have been in this rock twenty years for it, and tonight I chose again, and a man who liked you and said nothing personal went to the post instead of you, and I would do it again, and you will hate me for it, and that is correct."

"Why?"

"Because I have been wrong about the wall and wrong about the seed and wrong about the stone, and I am not wrong about this: you are the only thing in this Pit that can get out of it, and Corr was not, and I have one thing to sell and I sold it." He looked up. "That is what I am, boy. You have known since the first night and you have not wanted to know it, and now you have it on you like plate on a broken arm. I am a man who does arithmetic. I did it on the wall. I did it on the galleries. I did it on the road tonight and it came out to Corr. And I am going to tell you the rest of it, because you have earned it and because you will not come back to this rock if I do not, and the rest of it is this." He set the stick down. "I did not only do the arithmetic on the galleries. I drew them."

Jonah looked at him.

"Twenty-two years ago. Before the box. Before the Senate. I was Master of the College and forty years old and the Empire needed a mine that gave ten a day from the Shallows and slabs from the Deep and did not fall, and I sat at a table on Latium with a room of books larger than this Pit and I drew it. The Throat. The road. The seventh chamber. The Cradle, boy, I drew the Cradle, I put the sick against the thickest wall so it would finish them and called it a kindness in the margin. I drew this room. I drew the door you open with your hand. And they built it, and it worked, and I did the arithmetic on it in the tenth year and it said what I told you it said, and I went to the Senate, and they put me in the box I had drawn." He was quiet. "That is why it is correct that I die here. Not because I chose the stone. Because I made the rock. Every man on those slabs is on them because I drew the road he walked down. And I have counted every one, and I put myself first, and tonight I sold a man named Corr for a man named Vale, and I would do it again, and now you know what I am, and you can go."

Jonah stood up.

He stood in the Cradle that the old man had drawn, with his back to the wall the old man had put the sick against, and looked at the thin straight figure in the lamp with the white line across his arm and the stick on the rock beside him, and the place where he kept things was so full that he could not find the door of it.

"Corr's name," he said. "Corr of Anvil. North yards. Four years on the wall. He said a month."

"I will write it."

"Write it right."

"I have never written one wrong," the Censor said, "and I have written six hundred, and you know that, and it does not help, and it is the only thing I have." He picked up the lamp. "Go and carry your buckets. Or do not. I will be here. I have nowhere else to be; I drew it that way."

Jonah went out onto the Deep road.

He did not pick up the buckets. He stood in the dark between two lamps with the Vein warm through the rock beside him and the fever from the cut going through him, unus, duo, tres, and he said the names, all of them, out of order, the way he had them, and when he came to the end he said Corr, and Auger, and Hollen, and Bricks, and a boy from the last transport whose name he did not know, and a Deep man whose name he did not know, and he said, I'm sorry, I don't know it, and then he said the guard's name, the one he'd hauled under a shelf, Decimus Alba, of Etruria, and stopped.

He did not say the last one. He'd said it at the top of the list every night for two years, three words in the old tongue, tapped once, and he stood in the dark and could not, and did not, and picked up the buckets, and did not go back to the Cradle for six weeks.
