# Chapter 19: The Riot

The Deep stopped giving slabs eleven days after the wall went loud. The count Jonah had built for Rennick came apart all at once, from a crack nobody had looked at.

He'd known it would. He'd felt it the morning after, cutting at the face with plate still stuck under his gloves: the slab fought the steel. Not for him; it never fought him, not once. For Ulli beside him, for Harrow, for every deep-digger in the cut. The Vein had been bitten and had turned all of itself toward the bite. Where it had turned, it had closed, the pale lines drawn tight like skin over a wound. A slab that came off in an hour came off in three, then a shift, then not at all.

The Deep gave shards. By the piece. Like the Shallows.

Rennick carried nine up the Throat on the twelfth day, by weight. Tullus read it and looked at him. He said, quietly, in front of the garrison hall, that the old count had said eleven by the piece and the new count said nine by weight. One of the two was a lie, and he'd like to know which. Rennick, who'd had the voice since he was five, didn't have it then.

The count went to fourteen. By the piece. Every decury. From tomorrow.

And Sabinus came down the Throat to remind the Deep.

---

"Fourteen," Bricks said. Once. He'd stopped saying things twice.

"Fourteen," Decimus read off the list, flat, the voice for things that weren't personal. It wasn't personal. His hand on the list wasn't steady, and Jonah saw that and kept it.

The road didn't say anything. Sixty men on the rock of the Shallows with their bowls, and Auger at the edge of the light with his arms folded, and nobody said anything. The wall gave ten and had given ten for twenty years. Fourteen wasn't a number. It was a strap, and everybody could count.

They made ten the first day. Decury six made eleven; the foreman cut two shards and broke one. Jonah stood at the post that dusk and chose Hollen, gave him ten. Hollen said nothing. The decury beside them made nine, and its foreman chose a boy off the last transport, and the boy screamed. The decury beside that made eight. There was no post for eight. There was the other thing, and Decimus didn't come down for it. In the morning there were fifty-nine on the road.

Sabinus didn't come to the Shallows. Sabinus was in the Deep. The road heard him anyway, same as it had heard the wall go loud: something changed, felt through the rock. Ulli came up on the second night to carry down a man who hadn't made a shard. He stopped at the seventh chamber, where Jonah was cutting, and said, without weight:

"He's killed four."

"Who?"

"Diggers. At the face. For slabs that wouldn't come." His face did nothing. "Not the post. The fang. He walks the cut, says slab. If it doesn't come he puts it through the neck seam, like cutting a rope. Says next. Four. Harrow's stopped counting." He picked up his bucket. "Thought you'd want to know what's coming up the road."

Jonah stood at the wall with his chisel and counted. Fifty-nine men on the road. A wall that gave ten and a number that said fourteen. A Centurion cutting throats in the Deep because a cut one had put his hand on the Vein and let it eat. He counted that too, and didn't put it away. He counted Auger, at the edge of the light, who'd held the road nineteen years by making it run, looking now at a road that couldn't run anymore, and thought about what a man like that did with that.

---

"He's going to do something," Jonah told the old man that night, in the Cradle, the stick coming at him from the dark and the plate, after nine days, finally gone back into his arms.

"Yes."

"You know what."

"I know Auger. Nineteen years, through a wall. A fair man who runs a road. A road that can't run gets decimated whatever he does, and he knows the arithmetic same as I do. A man who's going to be counted whatever he does tries, once, to choose the count." The stick took him across the ribs. He went down and got up. "He'll take the cage. Not to leave; nobody leaves. To hold the Shallows and the bottom of the Throat, so nothing goes up and nothing comes down. Tullus can't harvest. Has to talk. That's what a riot is. A man with no cards, putting the one he has on the table so the other man has to look at it."

"It won't work."

"No. Tullus sends Sabinus down with the guard. Sabinus does what he's doing in the Deep. Then Tullus lines the road and counts it. One in ten goes to the post and doesn't come off it. A decimation. Very old. It's in the library the Roma ark carried. The most Roman thing in this Pit." His face in the lamp was stone. "It happens whether you're in it or not. The only thing you choose is where you're standing when it does."

"Where should I stand?"

"Wherever the fewest men die," the Censor said. "That's the only answer I've ever had, and I didn't learn it in time. Get up."

---

Auger came to the seventh chamber on the fourth night.

Slow, no weight to it, an old broken man with a chisel he'd forgotten he was holding. He looked at decury six at the wall, and at Jonah. "Foreman. A word."

They stood in the road. Auger didn't lower his voice; he hadn't needed to in nineteen years.

"Tomorrow. Change of guard, fourth bell. Four on the road, the cage down, the young one on it. We take the cage. We take the four. We hold the road and the bottom of the Throat, and Tullus talks or Tullus doesn't harvest." His face didn't change. "I've counted. I don't need six. I need six to stay in the seventh chamber and not be on the road. You've a foreman with a Stripe's wrist, a farmer with a garrison chisel, a north-side cutter who's had thirty on his back this week and hasn't made a sound. I don't want any of you where Sabinus can see what you are. I want you behind a wall. That's the whole of it."

"You're going to die."

"I've been dying nineteen years, cut one. I'd like to choose the week." He looked at Jonah the same way he'd looked at him the night he broke Bricks's wrist. "You've counted it. I can see it on you. You know it doesn't work and you know it's coming whatever you do. You've made a decury on this road that doesn't die. I'd like one thing left standing when it's over." He stopped. "And I'd like one other thing."

"What?"

"The old guard. Decimus." His voice didn't change. "He's on the road at the fourth bell. Always is; it's his count. When we take the cage the young ones will come at us, and he won't. He'll stand there with his rifle and say it's not personal. One of mine puts a chisel in him, because it's a riot and that's what a riot is." He looked at Jonah. "He brought you a bandage once. I'd like him off the road. I can't do that. You've a wrist and a chamber with a wall in front of it. Do what you can."

He went back to the edge of the light.

Jonah stood in the road. Auger was right. Every way he counted it, a road lined up in the morning with a Centurion walking down it. The only number he could change was how many were on the road when the count came. Six men behind a wall. And an old guard with a letter in his coat, who'd stand there and say it wasn't personal. He'd be right. He'd die of it.

He didn't tell Decimus.

He'd say that, later, and the woman with the broad stripe would ask why not. He'd say: Decimus would have gone up and told Tullus, because it wasn't personal either way. Tullus would have sent Sabinus down a bell early, and the road would have died with the cage still up. She'd say, you could have told him to be sick. He'd say, he wouldn't have been. He'd have said, it's my count. He'd have had to decide, and he'd have decided the count.

That's not an excuse, she'd say.

No, he'd say. It's a number.

---

The fourth bell rang, and the road went for the cage.

He was behind the wall. Six men in the seventh chamber, chisels out, the Vein breathing in front of them. Sound came down the road, through the rock, same as the wall going loud had come. Not one sound. Sixty men who'd decided. A rifle. Another. And something Jonah had never heard and knew at once: a cage dropping too fast and hitting rock.

Then it was Sabinus. Everyone heard that. It came down the Throat as a thing that had changed. The cage going up, and down, and down. Each time different.

"They've got the four," Bricks said. He'd gone to the mouth of the chamber. "Auger's got the four. And the cage." He stopped. "They've got Decimus. On his knees at the cage end. Rifle's gone. Auger's man's over him."

Jonah put the chisel down.

"Foreman."

"Stay at the wall."

"Foreman. Sabinus is on the road."

He knew. He went to the mouth and looked, once. Sixty men and four guards and a cage. Auger at the cage end, arms loose. A young guard on the rock with a chisel in his neck. Decimus on his knees, a Collar over him, chisel up. And coming down the road, not hurrying, a man of middle height, narrow face, two white scars on his arm. Six guards behind him in grey plate. A fang out on his right arm, a forearm long.

He was harvesting.

Sabinus walked at the pace of a man going somewhere. Men came at him from the walls. He didn't stop walking. The fang went through the first one's throat as he came in, without turning. Through the second one's belly on the backswing. The third came in low. Sabinus stepped, half a step, and the chisel hit rock where his hip had been. The fang came down on the man's neck like a rope cut.

Next.

Jonah watched from the mouth of the chamber and knew, with the whole count, that he'd last two seconds. Not the plate. It had come up fitted and right at the bottom of the Deep; the old man had hit it with a stick for nine nights and said ah. It was the step. Sabinus had stepped half a pace on a low thrust, not left or right or back, into the place the chisel wasn't. Jonah had done that step ninety nights on the rock and never once like that.

"Foreman," Bricks said.

"Stay at the wall."

"He's going to kill him."

He was. The Collar over Decimus had looked up the road at Sabinus coming and made a decision. If he was going to die, he'd take a guard with him. The chisel came down.

Jonah was already moving.

---

He didn't use the fang. He wanted that understood, later, by the one person he told.

He went down the road with a chisel in his right hand and nothing else on him, no plate, in the light, past sixty men and a Centurion. He did it because the other choice was a fang in the light with Sabinus on the road, and Sabinus wouldn't have been bored. He ran the ugly way, nobody's own, fast, twenty-four on a glass with no line for him and a body grown into the coat.

He took the Collar's wrist. Not the turn. The pull. He pulled the arm straight and stepped through, and the Collar went past him onto the rock. The chisel hit stone beside Decimus's head instead of Decimus. The old guard looked up, face gone white, rifle gone, and said, "Four four seven one."

"Get up."

"It's my count."

"Get up, Decimus." He hauled him under the arms, the same way Ulli hauled the sick. Six paces to the sleeping gallery. Pushed him in. The Collar got up behind them with his chisel. Jonah turned and put his own into the man's thigh, high, in the meat, and pulled it out. The man sat down. He'd live. He wouldn't follow.

"In," Jonah said. "Under the shelf. Stay there. It's not personal."

Decimus looked at him from the mouth of the gallery with the face that had waited in the tunnel once, for a boy on his knees.

Then he went in.

Jonah turned around. Sabinus was twenty paces off, and had stopped walking.

---

He hadn't stopped for Jonah. Auger had come to meet him.

Auger held his chisel like a man remembering it. He came in low, short, a feint at the belly and his other hand for the throat. Sabinus did the half step, into the place the chisel wasn't.

And Auger was there.

He hadn't feinted at the belly. He'd feinted at the feint. He'd watched Sabinus do the step on three men and came in exactly so it put the Centurion where his other hand was. That hand had a second chisel, the ground-down point of Bricks's, and it went into the seam at Sabinus's armpit.

A finger. Not far. Sabinus made a sound, the first Jonah had heard him make, and the bored face stopped being bored.

He hurried.

The fang came round wild, a backhand from a man who'd just felt steel in a seam for the first time in years. It took Auger from shoulder to hip. He went down on the road he'd held nineteen years, and didn't get up. Sabinus stood over him with a finger of chisel in his armpit, and something new on his face.

Fear. Not of Auger. Of the seam.

"Next," Sabinus said, and pulled the chisel out, and looked up the road.

Jonah wasn't on it.

He'd gone the moment Auger's second hand came up, sideways into the dark cut between the sleeping gallery and the seventh chamber, where the lamps didn't reach. A Centurion just made to hurry would want to put his fang through something, and the nearest something had been standing in the light with a chisel and a white line on his arm.

And in the dark, two of the six guards came for him.

---

They'd seen him go. Young, Tiro, grey plate, short fangs, and they'd watched their Centurion bleed. Two of them, with a lamp. The lamp found the cut, and the cut was empty.

The plate came up. He didn't bring it; he'd stopped pretending he brought it anywhere. Full, fitted, everywhere at once, out of the whole dark. The crooked fang came out of his wrist. One guard looked at a place darker than the dark and said, "What," and that was all.

The fang went through the throat seam. Not by accident, unlike Varro. The old man had drilled it into his hands with a stick: it's there, a finger wide, where the chest plate meets the jaw. You don't aim. Your hand goes there, same as it goes to a bucket handle. The plate came off him in a shiver, and he sat down.

The second one was quicker, came in low. Jonah did the half step. Late and ugly, half of Sabinus's. The grey fang skidded past his hip on plate. The crooked fang went into the armpit seam, to the hilt. The guard dropped the lamp.

It didn't go out. It lay on the rock and threw light up the wall. Jonah stood in it, full plate, two dead Blanks at his feet, a Centurion twenty paces off on a road that was screaming. The genius, fed, plated, unafraid, looked at the two cuttings in the two dead arms, and leaned.

"No," Jonah said, aloud, in the old tongue. "Not here. He'd smell it."

It didn't argue. It never argued. But it didn't stop leaning either. He held it off with the whole count. A Centurion with a licence could feel a genius eat the way a man feels a draught, and Sabinus had just learned fear and would want something to do about it.

He put the plate away.

It went, all at once, clean, same as it had in the Kestrel's hold, drawn in past the middle because the middle was whole. He'd told the old man the truth. He'd told Decimus to get under a shelf and told the road nothing and told himself the truth about all of it. The seam was a scar now, not a tear, and the plate went in over it like water over a stone.

Something went over inside him. Not small. He counted it, unus, duo, and thought: strain, third time this week. Then he picked up the lamp and put his hands in the nearest bucket, wrist-deep in shards, same as the rock had taught him the ninth night of the haul. The dark went out of his skin under the Vein's dark. He walked into the light with a bucket in his arms, a foreman carrying shards through a riot. Nobody looked at him twice. There was nothing on the road to look at but Sabinus.

---

It was over by the sixth bell.

The road had taken the cage and held it two bells. Then Sabinus came down it, and it stopped being a road and became the Deep with the slabs cut out. Forty-one men could stand. Eighteen couldn't. Auger was one of the eighteen. The cage went up with Sabinus in it and came down with Tullus.

He'd never seen the prefect. Nobody on the road had. Tullus was a name that came down the Throat as numbers. The man who stepped out of the cage was fifty, grey, a narrow stripe with one white line above it, a face that had never made a licence. He walked the road with Rennick behind him and didn't look at the dead.

"Line," Tullus said.

Forty-one men along the rock, the ones who could stand, the rest behind them. Decury six in the middle, the foreman at one end, Pell at the other, Corr between. Decimus had come out from under the shelf when the cage went up and found his rifle. He stood at the cage end with it, face gone grey, a dead guard at his feet.

"Count them," Tullus said.

Rennick went down the line with his slate and his voice that had gone since the count came apart. He came to Jonah and stopped, looked at him, the cut one who'd shown him a count by weight. His face did something. "Four four seven one," he said, and moved on.

"Forty-one," Rennick said, at the end.

"One in ten." Tullus said it to the road, in the voice for things that weren't personal, and it wasn't. He'd been trimmed once, and had decided that day what a number was. "Every tenth man. From the cage end. Rennick."

Rennick went down the line again. He touched them. That was how it was done; the old man had said, it's very old, it's in the library. He touched every tenth man on the shoulder, and the touched ones stepped forward. A Deep man Jonah didn't know. Hollen, with thirty on his back, who looked at nothing. A boy off the last transport. Bricks, with his one hand and his small eyes, who looked down the line at Jonah once, and got nothing back that asked for anything, which was the worst of it.

Rennick came down the line. Nine, ten, a man Jonah didn't know. Nineteen, twenty, Hollen. Twenty-nine, thirty, Bricks. Thirty-one, thirty-two, Pell, thirty-three, Corr, thirty-four, thirty-five. He'd known since the first man stepped that it would come to him. He'd put himself at the end of the decury when they lined, because that was where a foreman stood, and a foreman who could count had known where the tenth would fall.

Rennick stopped in front of him.

His hand came up, and stopped. The width of a hand from Jonah's shoulder. Rennick looked at him with the face of a man fifteen years afraid of Tullus, who'd just found something he was more afraid of. Jonah didn't know what it was, and then he did.

Tullus was reading a slate. Not Rennick's. Another one, from his coat, the thin dark slab that came out of the Chamber once a month, the numbers that kept the galleries from falling. He read it, in front of forty-one men, his grey face doing nothing. Then he looked up.

"Not that one."

Rennick's hand didn't move.

"Not that one, Rennick. The galleries." Tullus put the slate away. "Next."

The hand moved past Jonah's shoulder, to the man beside him, and touched it, and Corr stepped forward.

He stepped forward the same way he'd walked to the post before. Without looking at the floor. He turned his head once and looked at Jonah, the wall's dark on his face, and his mouth moved. Not a sound.

Nothing personal.

Then the guards took the five of them to the post at the cage end. Sabinus came down with his armpit bound, and his face not bored, and it wasn't the post.

---

He told the old man all of it. The first hour and the second, on the rock of the Cradle, from the cage dropping too fast to the sound the road made when Sabinus put the fang through Corr. The Censor sat across from him with the lamp and the stick on his knees. He didn't speak until it was done, and not for a while after.

"The slate," Jonah said.

"Yes."

"Tullus read it in the line. He said not that one."

"Yes."

"That was you."

The old man looked at the lamp.

"Tullus came down at the fourth bell, to my door, with a key and a lamp. Stood where you stood the night you opened it. Said, the Deep's stopped giving and there's a riot on the Shallows. I need the galleries to hold through it. He handed me a slate." His voice was even. "I wrote the numbers that keep the galleries from falling, which is the only thing I've ever had to sell in this rock, and have sold him once a month for twenty years, for a lamp and a bowl. At the bottom I wrote: four four seven one is the only man on the Shallows who can cut a slab, and the Deep's stopped. Count him and you carry nine up the Throat by the piece till the war ends. I didn't write that you were mine. He doesn't know that. He knows the galleries hold when I say they hold, and that I don't lie about numbers. He read it, counted the line, and spent the one thing I had." He was stone. "And the tenth man was Corr."

Jonah sat on the rock and said nothing for a while.

"You knew that."

"I knew a tenth man is a tenth man. I didn't know his name. I do now; you've told me; I'll write it." He didn't look up. "You're going to say I chose. You're right. I chose. I chose once before, with a slate in my hand, and I've been in this rock twenty years for it. Tonight I chose again, and a man who liked you and said nothing personal went to the post instead of you. I'd do it again. You'll hate me for it, and that's correct."

"Why?"

"Because I've been wrong about the wall, wrong about the seed, wrong about the stone, and I'm not wrong about this: you're the only thing in this Pit that can get out of it. Corr wasn't. I had one thing to sell, and I sold it." He looked up. "That's what I am, boy. You've known since the first night, and haven't wanted to. Now you have it on you like plate on a broken arm." He set the stick down. "I'll tell you the rest, because you've earned it, and because you won't come back if I don't. I didn't only do the arithmetic on the galleries. I drew them."

Jonah looked at him and waited.

"Twenty-two years ago. Before the box. Before the Senate. I was Master of the College, forty years old. The Empire needed a mine that gave ten a day from the Shallows and slabs from the Deep and did not fall. I sat at a table on Latium, in a room of books larger than this Pit, and I drew it. The Throat. The road. The seventh chamber. The Cradle, boy, I drew the Cradle. I put the sick against the thickest wall so it would finish them, and called it a kindness in the margin. I drew this room, and the door you open with your hand. They built it, and it worked. I did the arithmetic in the tenth year, and it said what I've told you it said. I went to the Senate, and they put me in the box I'd drawn." He was quiet. "That's why it's correct that I die here. Not because I chose the stone. Because I made the rock. Every man on those slabs is on them because I drew the road he walked down. Tonight I sold a man named Corr for a man named Vale, and I'd do it again. Now you know what I am, and you can go."

Jonah stood up. He stood with his back to the wall the old man had put the sick against, and looked at the thin straight figure in the lamp with the stick on the rock beside him. There was too much to carry, and no order to put it in.

"Corr's name," he said. "Corr of Anvil. North yards. Four years on the wall. He said a month."

"I'll write it."

"Write it right."

"I've never written one wrong," the Censor said, "and I've written six hundred, and you know that. It doesn't help, and it's the only thing I have." He picked up the lamp. "Go and carry your buckets. Or don't. I'll be here. I've nowhere else to be. I drew it that way."

Jonah went out onto the Deep road.

He didn't pick up the buckets. He stood in the dark between two lamps with the Vein warm through the rock beside him and the fever going through him, unus, duo, tres. He said the names, all of them, out of order, same as he always had them. Corr. Auger. Hollen. Bricks. A boy from the last transport whose name he didn't know. A Deep man whose name he didn't know, and he said, I'm sorry, I don't know it. Decimus Alba, of Etruria.

He didn't say the last one. He'd said it at the top of the list every night for two years, three words in the old tongue, tapped once. He stood in the dark and couldn't, and didn't. He picked up the buckets, and didn't go back to the Cradle for six weeks.
