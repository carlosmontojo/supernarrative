# Chapter 18: Deep

The first thing it took was the fat off his ribs, and he did not notice, because there had not been much.

The second thing was the muscle in his left forearm, above the ring. He noticed that, because he was on the haul with a bucket in each hand and his left hand let go.

It was the third year. He knew it by the count; he'd stopped needing the seasons. Twelve stood, by weight, and Rennick carried fifteen. The Deep gave slabs and the Shallows gave what it gave. Decury six had a foreman who had chosen Corr four times and Hollen three and Bricks once, for the one-handed count. He had chosen himself twice, when the arithmetic said so. He had stood at the post with the road watching and taken ten from Bricks's good hand and not made a sound. The old man had said, that is not the count, that is you wanting to be seen paying. Do not do it again. And he had not.

And the thing under his ribs had not eaten since Vetch.

It had grown. It grew on the haul and on the stick and on the wall. It grew on the door of the Vein held open in the Cradle for an hour every third night. It was twenty on the glass by the old man's guess and it was hungry with a size. He knew the size, and the size had been going the wrong way for a season.

"It is taking from you," the Censor said. He'd seen the bucket drop; he saw everything. "Your arm. It will be your legs next and then it will be the thing in your chest that you cannot spare. It has grown faster than the body around it and it needs to be fed. It has not been, because you have been on a road where the only meat is the wall and I have told you not to."

"Then let me."

"No."

"You let it take a mouthful in the Cradle."

"It took a mouthful in the Cradle while I was on the other side of a rock tapping at a dying man. I did not let anything. It learned from that mouthful that its mother could be bitten, and it has been leaning at every wall in this Pit since. If you put your hand on the Vein and let it eat on purpose it will learn that you will let it. It will not stop asking, and the wall will learn you faster than you learn it." The old man's face in the lamp was the stone. "It is finding out what shape you are, boy. Do not feed it the answer."

"It's eating my arm."

"Then eat more bread and drill less and let it be hungry. Hunger is a thing you know the size of. That is more than any man in the Empire can say about his genius, and it is the only thing that keeps yours yours." The stick came off his shoulder. "Get up."

Jonah got up.

He thought, lying on the shelf after, with his left hand aching and the thing under his ribs turned toward the rock wall of the gallery the way it always was: he's being a stone. He chose the stone on the sixth day, on that shelf, with the wall in him. He's been a stone for twenty years, and he's making me one.

He thought: he's afraid of it. He said so. He's afraid of what it does in a man and he's putting that on me with a stick and calling it a lesson.

He thought: I turned around. He didn't.

He was twenty-two. He would say that, later, when he told it, and the old man would not say anything, because the old man would be dead by then. Jonah would say it to a woman with a broad stripe on her arm who had never once lied to him. She would say, that's not an excuse, and he would say, no. It's a number. Like the ones on the glass.

---

The Deep took foremen for a week.

That was Rennick's doing, or the weight's. A foreman who had never cut a slab did not know what a slab was worth. A deputy who carried fifteen up the Throat on the Deep's slabs wanted every foreman on the road to know exactly what a slab was. That way, when the Shallows made nine by the piece, the foreman could say, and by weight, deputy, and carry the number. So the foremen went down. One at a time, for a week, to a deep decury, to cut at the slab face and learn it. Then come back up and count right.

Jonah's week came in the third year. He told the old man about it on the road, with the buckets, in the dark, and the old man said, "Good. Learn the cut. Do not touch the wall with your hand. Not once. The Deep is thick and it is loud and it has taken every man who ever put a hand on it. You have a thing in you that has been leaning at it for three years."

"I know."

"Say it."

"I won't touch the wall."

The old man looked at him in the dark for a moment, in the way that found out where the weight was. Jonah kept his face still and his weight where a man's weight was who meant it. The old man said, "Good," and went back into the rock.

It was the first time he had lied to the Censor. He knew it as he did it. He'd lied to his father at a door and to the quaestor with true things and to sixty men on a road with a fever. He had never once, in three years, lied to the old man, because the old man could hear it the way Priscus could, without a gift, from twenty years of listening through a wall. And because there had been nothing to lie about.

He lied now, and the old man did not hear it. Jonah walked down the Deep road with the buckets and felt something in his chest that he had felt once before, kneeling in his father's room with five vigiles in it.

A small clean tearing. Like a seam going.

He put it away.

---

The Deep was not the Shallows with more dark. He'd known that from the haul, from the road, from the Cradle, but he had not stood at the face. Standing at the face was another thing.

The slab galleries went off the Deep road the way the seventh chamber went off the Shallows, but they did not open out. They went in. They went in for two hundred paces and then they went down. At the bottom of the down there was the Vein, and it was not a wall. It was everything. It was the floor and the roof and the three sides. The gallery was a cut in it the way a shaft is a cut in a hull. The deep-diggers stood in the cut with steel and took it off the sides in slabs, and it breathed, all of it, around them. It was warm, and the pale lines in it ran everywhere at once. Jonah stood at the bottom of the down on the first day with a chisel in his hands and the thing under his ribs going through him like a tide. He thought, this is what Ulli carries the sick out of.

"Cut," said the deep foreman. His name was Harrow; Ulli had said he was fair and afraid. "Slab's a hand deep, two hands wide, long as your arm. Score it, three sides, then in under, and it comes. Don't touch it with the skin. Gloves."

There were gloves. He had not known that. Deep-diggers wore them, leather, worn through at every finger and patched with more leather, because the Vein at the face reached. A man who scored a slab with a bare hand on the steel was a man who had a hand on the Vein, and the Deep did not need any more men in the Cradle than it had.

He put the gloves on. He cut.

It came off for him. Of course it did. He'd known it would. He'd tried, for three days, to cut like a deep man, slowly, with the slab fighting the steel the way Harrow's did, and he could not make it fight, because it did not want to. The Vein opened for his chisel here the way it had in the Shallows, like a door. A slab a hand deep and an arm long came off the face for him in the time it took Ulli, beside him, to score one side. Ulli looked at it, and at him. He said nothing, and went back to his side.

Harrow noticed on the second day.

"You've cut before."

"Shallows. Three years."

"Not like that." The deep foreman looked at the slab at Jonah's feet. "That's a deep cut. That's twenty years. Where'd you learn it?"

"It comes off for me," Jonah said. "Corr says. It does that, before."

Harrow looked at him for a long moment, with the face of a man who was fair and afraid. Then he looked at the white line on Jonah's arm, and nodded slowly, the way Corr had nodded once, and went back down the gallery.

"He'll say a month," Ulli said, quietly, beside him.

"Everybody says a month."

"Not here. Here they say a week." Ulli scored his slab. "You told the old man you wouldn't touch it."

Jonah didn't answer.

"I sit at the end of the road. I hear what's said in the Cradle when the rock's open. I heard you say it." The deep man's voice was rough and low and did not carry. "I've carried forty men out of here. I know what a man looks like when he's decided a thing and hasn't told anyone. You've got it on you. You've had it on you since the haul dropped your hand."

"It's eating me, Ulli."

"I know. I've seen your arm." Ulli set the chisel down. He looked at the face, at the pale lines running everywhere, breathing, warm. He said, in the voice he used for the songs, "It sings names, cut one. I told you. And the old man says it's finding out your shape. You're going to put your hand on it tonight, when Harrow's up the road, and let the thing in you eat. Because you're hungry and you're twenty-two. You turned around once and you think that means you'll turn around again." He picked the chisel up. "I'm not going to stop you. I couldn't. I'm telling you I'll be at the end of the gallery. So somebody is."

---

He did it on the fourth night.

Harrow went up the road at the second bell, the way he did, to take the count to the cage. The deep decury sat down in the cut with their backs to the warm and their gloves off, and ate. Jonah sat with them. Then he got up and walked to the far end of the gallery, where the cut went deepest and the lamp did not reach. He took his glove off, and put his hand on the Vein.

He had thought about how.

Not the way he'd thought about Vetch, with arithmetic. The way he'd thought about the hook. He'd lain on the deep shelf three nights with the thing under his ribs turned toward the face like a plant toward a lamp. He had held the thought out to it the way he held out the names: not all of it. A mouthful. The way you took it in the Cradle. And then back. It had listened, the way it always listened, with that flat total attention. He had felt it understand, or felt it do the thing it did that was like understanding, and he had thought, all right. All right. It expects the names. It'll expect this.

He put his hand flat.

The wall was warm. It had always been warm. And the thing under his ribs, which had been leaning at it for three years, went. He did not stop it in the doorway this time. He did not say stop; he said, in the old tongue, because that was the tongue for slow things, eat.

It ate.

It was not like the Cradle. In the Cradle it had been going home and had turned and bitten on the way out, a child taking bread off a table. This was a thing that had been told it could. It went into the Vein through his palm the way it had gone into Varro's arm, reading, hungry. The Vein said come, the way it always said it, and the thing under his ribs said no, and ate.

He felt it take. He felt the Vein feel it. He felt something the size of the planetoid turn, all of it, toward a place on its own skin where a piece of itself was standing with its mouth open. It did not stop him, because it did not know how. It did not want to, because it was finding out what shape he was, and this was a shape.

The coat got bigger.

Not by a size he could almost name. By a size. He felt it go over inside him like a whole door, and then another. He stood at the bottom of the Deep with his hand flat on the thing that had made the Empire and felt his genius eat its mother on purpose. It was, he would tell the woman with the broad stripe years later, because she would ask and because he did not lie to her, the best thing he had ever felt.

That was the thing. Not the growing. The eating. He'd been hungry for three years with a size, and the size had been going the wrong way. Now it was going the right way, fast. Every piece of the wall that came through his palm was folded away, and the folding was being fed. Being fed was, after three years on one bowl a day and a stick, a thing he had forgotten had a feeling.

Enough, he thought.

It did not stop.

He thought it again, in the old tongue. Enough. Stop. And the thing under his ribs, which had stood in a doorway for stop a hundred times, which expected the names, which had made a hook because he asked, heard him, and went on eating. It was hungry with a size. It had just learned that the size could be filled and that he would let it. The old man had said that. The old man had said it will not stop asking, and it was not asking.

He took his hand off the wall.

It took everything he had. He would remember that. Not the eating; the taking off. His hand was on the Vein and the thing under his ribs was in the Vein. Pulling it away was like pulling a hand out of a hole in a hull with the vacuum on the other side, and he did it. He stood in the dark at the end of the gallery with his hand held against his chest and his heart going. The coat had gone big by four sizes, five, more than Vetch and Varro together, and the fever was coming up through him from the middle like nothing he'd ever counted.

He got as far as the lamp before it took him.

---

He came up out of it with a chisel coming at his face.

He did not know how long. He was on the floor of the cut, on his back, with the warm all around him and the lamp somewhere above and to the left. The fever was in every part of him, not from the middle out but everywhere at once, the way the plate had come on the derelict. His eyes were doubled. In both of them a man was standing over him with a chisel in both hands, raised, and the man was singing.

It was not Ulli.

He knew that with the part of him that knew things. It was a deep-digger, one of Harrow's, a thin man with a gone face whom Jonah had cut beside for four days and had not spoken to, because he did not speak. He sang, under his breath, to the face, the way Ulli did, except Ulli sang to keep the wall out and this man sang because the wall was in. He had been in the Cradle once, Ulli had said. He'd been carried out. He was the only one Ulli had ever carried out who was not dead. He'd been sent back to the face because Harrow was afraid and the Deep needed hands, and he'd been cutting there for a year with the Vein half in him, singing.

And the Vein was singing now. Jonah could hear it, with his hand off the wall, through the fever, the way he'd heard it in the Cradle with the old man's hand on his chest. Come. It was saying come, and it was saying it to the thing under his ribs, which had just eaten five sizes of it. It was loud, louder than it had ever been, because it had just been bitten and had turned all of itself toward the biting. The thin man with the chisel was standing in the loudest place in the Pit with the wall half in him. The wall was saying come, and he was doing what the wall said.

The chisel came down.

Jonah's hands came up. He did not bring them up. They came, the way his legs had left in the sand, and the plate came with them.

All of it.

Not the back of a hand. Not a hook. Not a thin thing from the palm for a lock. It came out of him the way it had in the Kestrel's hold, everywhere, dark glass over his arms and chest and face. It came out right this time, fitted, the way the old man had said a lorica should, because it had eaten and it was full. It was, for the first time since the box, not afraid of anything. The chisel came down on his forearm and skidded off it with the sound a fang made on plate. The thin man's face did a thing. The fang came out of the back of Jonah's right wrist, crooked, a hand long, and went into him.

He did not choose the seam. There was no seam; the man had no plate. He went in under the ribs, the way Hask had gone into a boy. The thin man sat down on the floor of the cut with the chisel still in his hands and his mouth open. The song stopped, and Jonah stood up.

He stood up in full plate at the bottom of the Deep with the wall singing and a man dying at his feet and the fever in every part of him. He felt the thing under his ribs, fed, full, plated, look out through his eyes at what it had done, and he felt himself look with it.

And it was good.

That was the thing he would keep, and would tell the woman with the broad stripe, and would tell nobody else. It was good. Not the killing. The standing. The plate on him, fitted, right, the fang out, the fever like heat from a stove. Five sizes of the wall folded away inside him, and the wall itself saying come and not being obeyed. He stood in it. He stood in it for a count of ten, or twenty, he did not know, with a dying man at his feet whom he had cut beside for four days and had not spoken to. He was not sorry, and he was not afraid. The decurion in his hands and the boy folded in with him looked out through his eyes at the bottom of the Pit. They had one opinion between them, and it was: yes. This. This is what it's for.

He nearly agreed.

He nearly agreed the way he'd nearly agreed in the transport hold, with Hask. He felt the yes come up through him from the echoes and the plate and the full thing under his ribs. It was warm. It was the warmest thing he'd felt since the eating. He stood in the Deep and let it come, and it was almost all the way up when he heard, from the end of the gallery, in a rough low voice that did not carry, somebody say his number.

"Four four seven one."

Ulli. At the end of the gallery, on his bucket, where he'd said he'd be, so that somebody was.

Jonah turned his head.

The deep man was looking at him. At the plate. At the dark glass over a Collar's arms and chest and face. At the crooked fang on the wrong side of the wrist with a deep-digger's blood on it. Ulli's face did not do the thing faces did. He had carried forty men out of the Cradle and had heard the rock tap. He had sat at the end of a road for three years listening to a thing that was not a man and a man that was. His face did nothing at all, and he said, quietly:

"Put it away. Harrow's on the road."

And the yes went out of Jonah like the drive cutting.

---

The plate would not go in.

He tried. He stood over the dead man with Ulli watching and he asked, the way he asked. The thing under his ribs heard him and drew in, the way it did, from everywhere, and it caught.

It caught on the seam.

He felt it. He'd felt the seam go twice, in his father's room and on the Deep road four nights ago telling the old man I won't touch the wall. Both times it had been a small thing, a tearing, and he had put it away. It was not a small thing now. The plate came in from his chest, his legs, his face. It came to the place in the middle of him where the tearing was. It could not get past it, because a thing that is drawing in draws in toward the middle, and the middle was torn. It stopped, and hung, half in. Dark glass over both his forearms, the fang still out, and it would not go.

"Ulli."

"I see it."

"It won't."

"I see." The deep man had come down the gallery. He stood a pace off and looked at the plate on Jonah's arms with the face that did nothing. "You lied to him."

"Yes."

"You told him you wouldn't touch it and then you touched it. And it ate. I heard the wall go loud. Everybody in the Deep heard it, they thought it was a fall." Ulli looked at the dead man. "Sorrel heard it. He'd been half in for a year and it went loud and he went the rest of the way. That's on the wall, not you. But the plate's on you." He looked back up. "The old man said it. Through the rock, the first month, when he was teaching you to hold it. I heard. He said a genius in a man who's lying to himself can't hold a shape. He said it's not a punishment, it's what happens. Like a hull with a crack in it. You can't pressurise it."

Jonah stood with the plate half on him and the fever in every part of him and looked at his hands.

"I'm not lying to myself."

"You lied to him. Same thing, for you. I've watched you three years." Ulli crouched and put his hands under Sorrel's arms. "Help me. He goes in the wall. Then you go up the gallery ahead of Harrow with your hands in the bucket, the way the old man taught you, and you keep them there. You cut with gloves on over plate and pray it holds. And tonight you go to the Cradle and you tell him."

"He'll know."

"He knew the minute you said it. He let you." The deep man lifted. "He wanted to see if you'd come back and say so. That's what he's been doing for three years, cut one. Seeing if you turn around."

---

He told him.

It was the eighth night of the week, the last. The plate was still on both his forearms under the gloves. There was a slab a hand deep in the bucket that he had cut in half the time it took Harrow, and a dead man's song in his head that he could not stop hearing, because the wall sang it now. Sorrel's shape was in the count, between a Blank who'd touched what he should not have and a boy from Marl who'd said no. He went into the Cradle with the buckets and put them down. The rock opened. The old man came out with the lamp and the stick. He looked at his face and then at his hands.

"Take the gloves off."

He took them off. The plate was there. Dark glass, fitted, right, on both forearms from wrist to elbow. It had been there for four days and nights and had not moved. The fang had gone in, that much he'd managed, but the plate had not.

The Censor looked at it for a long time.

"How much," he said.

"I don't know. Five sizes. More. It was more than Varro and Vetch."

"Twenty-four, then, if you were a cutting, and you are not. Twenty-four in one night." The old man's voice was the stone. "And a man dead, Ulli says, who was half in the wall already. The wall loud for an hour so that Harrow thought it was a fall. The plate stuck on you like a coat on a broken arm." He set the lamp down. "Tell me what you told me on the road, four nights before."

"I said I wouldn't touch it."

"And I said good, and went back into the rock. You walked down the road with that on you. It was on you, boy. I have listened to men lie through a wall for twenty years and you had the thing in your voice that they all have. I let you go, because I wanted to know what you would do with it." He picked up the stick. "Not the wall. I knew what you would do with the wall. What you would do with the lie."

"I came and told you."

"Four days after. With the plate stuck on you so that you had to. Would you have come if it had gone in?"

Jonah stood in the lamp with his plated arms at his sides and did not answer, because the old man could hear it, and because the answer was in the seam.

"No," the Censor said. "You would have cut your slab and come up and said the week was hard. I would have known, and you would have known I knew. We would have gone on with a thing between us that neither of us said. The plate would have come out on your hands in your sleep one night in front of Bricks, and that would have been the end of it." He looked at Jonah's arms. "That is the seam. Not a punishment. I have told you, and Ulli has told you, and now you have it on your arms so that you cannot put it away with the rest. It is what happens. The thing in you is a mind and it is joined to yours and it holds a shape by agreement. A man who has told a lie to the one person he has decided not to lie to has broken an agreement with himself, and the thing in him does not know what to hold onto. It hangs. It will hang until you are whole about it. You cannot be whole about a thing by saying you are sorry. You can only be whole by not lying, from now, and waiting."

"How long?"

"A week. A month. I do not know; I have never had one. I chose the stone." The old man lifted the stick. "So. You have eaten. You are twenty-four, near enough, and full. Your fever will be a bad one. The wall knows your shape a great deal better than it did, and it will not stop asking now. You will have to tell it no every night for the rest of your life, and that is what you bought for five sizes and a man named Sorrel." He set his feet. "And you have plate stuck on your arms and cannot take it off. That means that for the first time in three years you are going to fight me with it on. Get up."

"I'm standing."

"Then stand better," the Censor said, and the stick came out of the dark. It hit plate, and skidded. The old man said, "Ah," very quietly, as if he had been waiting a long time to hear that sound, and hit him again.
