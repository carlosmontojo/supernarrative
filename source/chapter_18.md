# Chapter 18: Deep

The first thing it took was the fat off his ribs. He didn't notice. There hadn't been much.

The second was the muscle in his left forearm, above the ring. He noticed that. He was hauling, a bucket in each hand, and his left hand let go.

Third year. He knew it by the count now, not the seasons. Twelve stood, by weight, and Rennick carried fifteen. The Deep gave slabs; the Shallows gave what it gave. Decury six had a foreman who'd chosen Corr four times, Hollen three, Bricks once, for the one-handed count. He'd chosen himself twice, when the arithmetic said so, and stood at the post with the road watching while ten came down on Bricks's good hand without a sound. The old man had said: that's not the count, that's you wanting to be seen paying. Don't do it again. He hadn't.

And the lorica hadn't eaten since Vetch.

It had grown anyway. On the haul, the stick, the wall. On the door of the Vein held open in the Cradle an hour every third night. Twenty on the glass, by the old man's guess, hungry with a size that had been going the wrong way for a season.

"It's taking from you," the Censor said. He'd seen the bucket drop; he saw everything. "Your arm. Your legs next, then what you can't spare. It's grown faster than the body around it, and it needs feeding. It hasn't been, because the only meat on this road is the wall, and I've told you not to."

The honest answer, the one he actually thought, was *tell that to my arm, which isn't feeling reasonable about it.* He gave the old man the shorter one instead.

"Then let me."

"No."

"You let it take a mouthful in the Cradle."

"I didn't let anything. It took a mouthful while I was tapping to a dying man on the other side of a rock. It learned its mother could be bitten, and it's leaned at every wall in this Pit since. Feed it on purpose and it learns you'll let it. It won't stop asking, and the wall will learn you faster than you learn it." His face in the lamp was stone. "It's finding out what shape you are, boy. Don't feed it the answer."

"It's eating my arm."

"Then eat more bread and drill less. Hunger you know the size of. No other man in the Empire can say that about his genius, and it's the only thing keeping yours yours." The stick came off his shoulder. "Get up."

Jonah got up.

He thought it later, on the shelf, his left hand aching: he's being a stone. Chose it on the sixth day, with the wall in him, and he's making one of me.

He's afraid of it. Said so himself. And he's putting that fear on me with a stick and calling it a lesson.

I turned around. He didn't.

He was twenty-two. He'd say that, later, to a woman with a broad stripe who never once lied to him. She'd say, that's not an excuse. He'd say, no. It's a number.

---

The Deep took foremen for a week.

Rennick's doing, or the weight's. A foreman who'd never cut a slab didn't know what one was worth, and a deputy who carried fifteen up the Throat on the Deep's slabs wanted every foreman to know exactly. So the foremen went down, one at a time, to a deep decury, to learn the cut, then come back up and count right.

Jonah's week came in the third year. He told the old man on the road, with the buckets, in the dark.

"Good. Learn the cut. Don't touch the wall with your hand. Not once. It's taken every man who ever put a hand on it, and you've something in you that's been leaning at it for three years."

"I know."

"Say it."

"I won't touch the wall."

The old man looked at him a moment, finding out where the weight was. Jonah kept his face still and his weight where a man's weight sits when he means it. "Good," the Censor said, and went back into the rock.

It was the first time he'd lied to the Censor. He knew it as he did it. He'd lied to his father at a door, and to the quaestor with true things, and to sixty men on a road with a fever. He'd never once lied to the old man, because the old man could hear it, same as Priscus could without a gift, after twenty years of listening through a wall. There had never been anything to lie about.

There was now, and the old man didn't hear it.

Jonah walked down the Deep road with the buckets and felt something in his chest he'd felt once before, kneeling in his father's room with five vigiles in it. A small clean tearing.

He put it away.

---

The Deep was not the Shallows with more dark. He'd known that from the haul and the road and the Cradle. Standing at the face was another thing.

The slab galleries went off the Deep road, same as the seventh chamber went off the Shallows, but they didn't open out. They went in for two hundred paces, then down. At the bottom the Vein was not a wall. It was everything: floor, roof, both sides, breathing, warm, pale lines running through it all at once. The deep-diggers stood inside the cut and took it off in slabs. Jonah stood at the bottom on the first day with a chisel in his hands and the lorica moving through him. This is what Ulli carries the sick out of, he thought.

"Cut," said the deep foreman. Harrow. Fair and afraid, Ulli had called him. "Slab's a hand deep, two hands wide, long as your arm. Score it, three sides, then in under, and it comes. Don't touch it with the skin. Gloves."

There were gloves, leather, worn through at every finger and patched. The Vein at the face reached. A man with a bare hand on the steel had a hand on the wall, and the Deep didn't need more men in the Cradle than it had.

He put the gloves on. He cut.

It came off for him. He'd tried, for three days, to cut slow, same as Harrow's slab fought the steel, and couldn't make it fight, because it didn't want to. A slab a hand deep and an arm long came off for him in the time it took Ulli, beside him, to score one side. Ulli looked at it, then at him. He said nothing, and went back to his side.

Harrow noticed on the second day.

"You've cut before."

"Shallows. Three years."

"Not like that." He looked at the slab. "That's a deep cut. Twenty years of one. Where'd you learn it?"

"It comes off for me. Corr taught me the angle three years ago, on a wall that fought him every day of his life and never once fought me. That's his, not mine," Jonah said.

Harrow looked at him a long moment, then at the white line on his arm, and nodded slowly, and went back down the gallery.

"He'll say a month," Ulli said, beside him.

"Everybody says a month."

"Not here. Here they say a week." Ulli scored his slab. "You told the old man you wouldn't touch it."

Jonah didn't answer.

"I sit at the end of the road. I hear what's said in the Cradle when the rock's open. I heard you say it." The deep man's voice was rough and low. "I've carried forty men out of here. I know what a man looks like when he's decided something and hasn't told anyone. You've had it on you since the haul dropped your hand."

"It's eating me, Ulli."

"I know. I've seen your arm." He set the chisel down and looked at the face, the pale lines running, breathing. "It sings names, cut one. I told you. The old man says it's finding out your shape. You're going to put your hand on it tonight, when Harrow's up the road, and let it eat. Because you're hungry, and you're twenty-two. You turned around once. You think that means you'll do it again." He picked the chisel back up. "I'm not going to stop you. I couldn't. I'm telling you I'll be at the end of the gallery. So somebody is."

---

He did it on the fourth night.

Harrow went up at the second bell, same as always. The deep decury sat down in the cut with their backs to the warm, gloves off, and ate. Jonah sat with them, then got up and walked to the far end, where the lamp didn't reach. He took his glove off and put his hand on the Vein.

He'd thought about how. Not with arithmetic, same as he'd thought about Vetch. Same as he'd thought about the hook. Three nights on the deep shelf, the lorica turned toward the face, he'd held the thought out to it, same as he held out the names: not all of it. A mouthful. Same as he'd taken it in the Cradle. Then back. It had listened, flat and total, same as it always listened. All right, he'd thought. It reads me every night and gets the truth. Tonight the truth is that I want to eat the wall.

He put his hand flat. The wall was warm; it had always been warm. The lorica, which had leaned at it three years, went. He didn't stop it in the doorway this time. He said, in the old tongue, because that was the tongue for slow things: eat.

It ate.

Not as it had in the Cradle, going home and biting on his way out. This was told it could. It went through his palm, same as it had gone into Varro's arm, reading, hungry. The Vein said come, same as it always did. The lorica said no, and ate.

He felt it take. He felt the Vein feel it, felt something the size of the planetoid turn, all of it, toward a place on its own skin where a piece of itself stood with its mouth open. It didn't stop him. It didn't know how. It didn't want to, either. It was finding out what shape he was, and this was a shape.

The coat got bigger. Not a size he could almost name. A size. He felt it go over inside him, whole, and then another. He stood at the bottom of the Deep with his hand flat on the wall that had made the Empire and felt his lorica eat its mother on purpose. It was, he'd tell the woman with the broad stripe years later, because she'd ask and because he didn't lie to her, the best thing he'd ever felt.

Not the growing. The eating. He'd been hungry three years with a size going the wrong way. Now it went the right way, fast. Every piece that came through his palm was folded away, and the folding was fed. Being fed was a feeling he'd forgotten existed, after three years on one bowl a day and a stick.

Enough, he thought.

It didn't stop.

He thought it again, in the old tongue. Enough. Stop. The lorica, which had stood in a doorway for stop a hundred times, which expected the names, which had made a hook because he asked, heard him and kept eating. It had just learned the size could be filled, and that he'd let it. The old man had said it wouldn't stop asking. It wasn't asking.

He took his hand off the wall.

It took everything he had. Not the eating; the taking off. His hand was on the Vein and the lorica was in the Vein, and pulling it away was pulling a hand out of a hull breach with the vacuum on the other side. He did it. He stood in the dark with his hand against his chest, his heart going, the coat gone big by four sizes, five, more than Vetch and Varro together, and the fever coming up through him from the middle like nothing he'd ever counted.

He got as far as the lamp before it took him.

---

He came up out of it with a chisel coming at his face.

He didn't know how long. He was on the floor of the cut, on his back, the warm all around him, the lamp somewhere above and to the left. The fever was everywhere at once, same as the plate had come on the derelict. His eyes were doubled. A man stood over him with a chisel raised in both hands, singing.

Not Ulli.

He knew that much. A deep-digger, thin, gone-faced, whom Jonah had cut beside four days and never spoken to, because the man didn't speak. Ulli sang to keep the wall out. This man sang under his breath because the wall was already in. He'd been carried out of the Cradle once, the only man Ulli had ever carried out who wasn't dead, and sent back to the face because Harrow was afraid and the Deep needed hands. A year at it, singing, with the Vein half in him.

And the Vein was singing now. Jonah could hear it through the fever, same as he'd heard it in the Cradle with the old man's hand on his chest. Come. It was saying it to the lorica, which had just eaten five sizes of it, and it was loud, louder than it had ever been, because it had just been bitten and turned all of itself toward the biting. The thin man stood in the loudest place in the Pit with the wall half in him, and the wall said come, and he was doing what it said.

The chisel came down.

Jonah's hands came up. He didn't bring them. They came on their own, same as his legs had left him in the sand, and the plate came with them.

All of it. Not a hand, not a hook, not a thin thing from the palm. It came out everywhere, dark glass over arms, chest, face, fitted this time, right, because it had eaten and it was full and afraid of nothing. The chisel hit his forearm and skidded, the sound a fang makes on plate. The thin man's face did something. The fang came out of Jonah's wrist, crooked, a hand long, and went into him.

There was no seam to find; the man had no plate. It went in under the ribs. The digger sat down on the floor with the chisel still in his hands, mouth open, and the song stopped, and Jonah stood up.

He stood in full plate at the bottom of the Deep, the wall singing, a man dying at his feet, the fever in every part of him. The lorica looked out through his eyes at what it had done, and he looked with it.

And it was good.

That was what he'd keep, and tell the woman with the broad stripe, and nobody else. Not the killing. The standing. The plate on him, fitted, right, the fang out, more heat coming off him than a body should make. He stood in it for a count of ten, or twenty, over a man he'd cut beside four days and never spoken to. He wasn't sorry. He wasn't afraid. The decurion in his hands and the boy folded in with him had one opinion, and it was yes. This. This is what it's for.

He nearly agreed.

He felt the yes come up through him, warm, warmer than anything since the eating, and let it come, and it had nearly reached the top when a rough voice at the end of the gallery said his number.

"Four four seven one."

Ulli. On his bucket, where he'd said he'd be, so somebody was.

Jonah turned his head.

The deep man looked at the plate, the dark glass over a Collar's arms and chest, the crooked fang with blood on it. His face did nothing at all. He'd carried forty men out of the Cradle and heard the rock tap. He said, quietly:

"Put it away. Harrow's on the road."

And the yes went out of Jonah the same way the drive cut: all at once, and gone.

---

The plate wouldn't go in.

He tried, standing over the dead man with Ulli watching, and asked, same as always. It heard him and drew in from everywhere, and caught.

It caught on the seam.

He'd felt the seam go twice before: in his father's room, and on the road four nights ago telling the old man I won't touch the wall. Small, both times. A tearing. He'd put it away. It wasn't small now. The plate came in from his chest, his legs, his face, toward the middle, where the tearing was. Whatever draws in draws toward the middle, and the middle was torn. It stopped and hung, half in, dark glass over both forearms, the fang still out.

"Ulli."

"I see it."

"It won't."

"I see." Ulli had come down the gallery. He stood a pace off, his face doing nothing. "You lied to him."

"Yes."

"You told him you wouldn't touch it, and you touched it. It ate. I heard the wall go loud. Everybody in the Deep heard it, thought it was a fall." He looked at the dead man. "Sorrel heard it too. Half in the wall a year, and it went loud, and he went the rest of it. That's on the wall, not you. But the plate's on you." He looked back up. "The old man said it to you through the rock, the first month, and I've heard him say it to me. A lorica in a man lying to himself can't hold a shape. It's not a punishment. It's what happens. Like a hull with a crack in it. You can't pressurise it."

Jonah looked at his hands, half plated, the fever going through him.

"I'm not lying to myself."

"You lied to him. Same thing, for you. I've watched you three years." Ulli crouched and got his hands under Sorrel's arms. "Help me. He goes in the wall. Then you go up the gallery ahead of Harrow with your hands in the bucket, same as the old man taught you, and keep them there. Cut with gloves on over plate and pray it holds. Tonight you go to the Cradle and tell him."

"He'll know."

"He knew the minute you said it. He let you." Ulli lifted. "He wanted to see if you'd come back and say so. That's what he's been doing three years, cut one. Seeing if you turn around."

---

He told him on the eighth night, the last of the week. The plate was still on both forearms under the gloves. There was a slab in the bucket he'd cut in half Harrow's time, and a dead man's song in his head he couldn't stop hearing, because the wall sang it now. Sorrel's shape was in the count between a Blank who'd touched what he shouldn't and a boy from Marl who'd said no.

He put the buckets down. The rock opened. The old man came out with the lamp and the stick, looked at his face, then his hands.

"Take the gloves off."

He did. The plate was there, dark glass, fitted, right, wrist to elbow, four days unmoved.

The Censor looked a long time.

"How much."

"I don't know. Five sizes. More than Varro and Vetch together."

"Twenty-four, then, if you were a cutting, which you're not. Twenty-four in one night." His voice was stone. "And a man dead, Ulli says, half in the wall already. The wall loud an hour, so Harrow thought it was a fall. The plate stuck on you and stayed stuck, same as a splint stays on a break." He set the lamp down. "Tell me what you told me on the road, four nights before."

"I said I wouldn't touch it."

"And I said good, and went back into the rock. You walked down that road with it on you. I've listened to men lie through a wall for twenty years, and you had it in your voice, same as they all do. I let you go, because I wanted to know what you'd do with it." He picked up the stick. "Not the wall. I knew what you'd do with the wall. What you'd do with the lie."

"I came and told you."

"Four days after. With the plate stuck on you so you had to. Would you have come if it had gone in?"

Jonah stood with his plated arms at his sides and didn't answer, because the old man could hear it, and because the answer was in the seam.

"No," the Censor said. "You'd have cut your slab, come up, said the week was hard. I'd have known, and you'd have known I knew. We'd have gone on with it between us, unsaid, until the plate came out on your hands in your sleep in front of Bricks one night. That would have been the end of it." He looked at Jonah's arms. "That's the seam. Not a punishment. I've told you, and Ulli's told you, and now you've got it on your arms so you can't put it away with the rest. It's what happens. You're joined to a mind, and it holds a shape by agreement. Lie to the one person you decided not to lie to, and it doesn't know what to hold onto. It hangs. You can't be whole about it by saying sorry. Only by not lying, from now, and waiting."

"How long?"

"A week. A month. I don't know; I've never had one. I chose the stone." He lifted the stick. "So. You've eaten. You're twenty-four, near enough, and full. Your fever will be a bad one. The wall knows your shape far better now, and it won't stop asking. You'll tell it no every night for the rest of your life, and that's what you bought for five sizes and a man named Sorrel." He set his feet. "And you've plate stuck on your arms and can't take it off. For the first time in three years you're going to fight me with it on. Get up."

"I'm standing."

"Then stand better."

The stick came out of the dark. It hit plate and skidded. The old man said, "Ah," very quietly. He had waited a long time to hear that sound. He hit him again.
