# Chapter 27: What You Ask of a Friend

On the fourteenth night Ulli was not on his bucket. Jonah found him in the Cradle, sitting against the wall with his eyes closed, singing to it.

He stopped in the mouth of the gallery. He had not seen Ulli in the Cradle since the third day of his own dying, seven years ago. Nobody was dying. The deep man sat against the warmest wall in the Pit with his knees up and his big hands hanging between them, and sang, low.

"Sit," Ulli said, without opening his eyes.

Jonah sat beside him, with his own back to the Vein.

"You said the fourteenth."

"I did. I've sat with it fourteen nights, till the wall's done. It's done. So ask."

Jonah said it flat, in the road's tongue: "Carry me. On the nineteenth. The harvest goes up on the twentieth. Harrow lids the last casks on the nineteenth and the Flamen seals them at dusk. I'll be in thirty-one from the sixth bell, held. It goes up the hopper cage on the twentieth with the rest. At the dusk count on the nineteenth, Decimus says four four seven one, and there's nobody. You say he went in. I carried him at the fourth bell, I sat with him, I took the blanket back. Decimus makes a mark."

"And the wall."

"The wall never took me. When the College reads the road in two years with the glass, it reads you. A man who carried a cut one to the Cradle and said the wall took him. Then it reads the wall. Six hundred and forty-three in it, and none of them's me." Jonah kept his voice level. "That's what I haven't counted. It doesn't come out. A man who lies to a Master's glass for a convict goes into the wall himself, or up the Throat to the slab. Either way he's in the count, and I put him there."

The deep man said nothing for a long time.

"You've got it wrong," he said at last. "You've counted it the way the old man would. Who owes, who fears, where the gap is. You've come out to a line that costs me, and you've stopped. You're a man who gives the bowl back. You don't know how to take one." He opened his eyes and looked at the roof. "I'm not on the slate, cut one. I never was. I'm the one who carries."

Jonah said, "I don't understand. Seven years I've counted casks and slabs and men, but I never once counted that. Why haven't I asked you why you're alive?"

"No. Seven years, and you've never once asked me why I'm alive. That's your answer."

Jonah looked at him.

"Two years," Ulli said. "That's the Deep. Two years if you're strong, and I've been in it nine. I came down the Throat at twenty-one for something I did on Marl. I'm not going to tell you what. It's the only thing I've got that's mine. Harrow put me at the face. The wall reached for me in the first month, and I sang."

"You sang."

"I'm Marl. We sing to a field so it knows we're there. My mother sang to the north forty every morning of her life. It never once made quota and she never once stopped. So the wall reached, and I sang, and it stopped."

"Why?"

"I don't know. The old man didn't know. I asked him through the rock, once, the only thing I ever asked. He tapped back that he'd read every page and there wasn't one for it." Ulli's face did nothing. "It reached for me, I sang, and it went still. Listening. Nine years it's been listening, and it's never once finished."

Behind Jonah's back the wall moved.

He felt it through his spine before he understood it. Warmth, and then more than warmth. The Vein leaning in the rock toward the man beside him, slow, the reach beginning. Ulli had stopped singing to talk. The plate came up on Jonah's palm where it lay on the floor without his asking. Ulli felt it too. He broke off, and hummed two lines of the field song under his breath, low, not looking. The rock went still. The reach stopped where it was, and waited.

"That," Ulli said. "That's why. Every time I shut my mouth for long enough."

Jonah put the plate away. His hand was shaking.

"So they made me the one who carries," Ulli said. "I was the only man in the Deep the wall wouldn't take. I carried forty-three. I sat with every one of them till the wall was done, and I sang, so they'd have something that wasn't the wall. It listened to me instead of finishing them fast. They went slow." He was quiet a moment. "Nine years. It's the only thing I've done in my life that was worth the doing. I did it in a room a man drew, and the man heard it through a wall and said so before he died. Most men get a number."

"Ulli."

"I'm not finished. You've counted what it costs me when the College reads the road. It costs me nothing. I'll not be on the road. The day the freighter's gone, the day after, I'm going to sit down in this room with my back to it, and I'm going to stop."

Jonah did not move.

"Singing. I'm going to stop singing. And it's going to do what it's wanted to do for nine years, and been too polite to do." Ulli looked at him then, for the first time. "That's not you. Don't put it on the slate. I've been dying nine years, cut one, the same as you have, except I meant it. I've known since the first month that this is how it ends, in this room. I've been choosing the day since. I've chosen it. The day after the harvest."

"No."

"Yes. A man who carried a cut one in and went in himself the next week is something the road understands. It's a story. It's not a question. The College reads a road with a story on it and writes the story down. The Master says, the wall is slow with some, and goes home."

"No."

"I've carried forty-three," Ulli said, in the voice Decimus used for a number. "I know what the wall does to a man who's ready, and I know what it does to one who isn't. I've watched you not be ready seven years and turn around three times. Going in's not for you. It's for me. I've been in this room longer than any man living. It's the only room I've got. I'd like to finish in it with somebody sitting there, and there's nobody, because the one who carries is me." He closed his eyes again. "So you'll do that. That's my price. Not a name on a slab. You'll sit with me. Before your freighter's a day out and you're in the dark and can't. You'll sit in this room with your back to it and you'll sing. A yard song, one of your names, the prayer to Mars if that's what you've got. I'll go, and you'll be there."

"I'll be in the cask."

"Not till the sixth bell of the nineteenth. It's the fourteenth. There's five days." His mouth did something that on another man would have been the start of a smile. "I can count too. The freighter loads on the twentieth. You're in the cask on the nineteenth. I go on the eighteenth. You sit with me on the eighteenth. You carry me to the count on the nineteenth, sick, and lay me down, and say to Decimus that the one who carries went. Then you go and get in your cask. On the dusk count there's two of us gone in a week, and Harrow says, that's the Deep, and nobody asks a Flamen anything."

Jonah sat with his back to the wall. He had nowhere to put it.

"That's not a price," Jonah said. "That's you dying so my count comes out even, and calling it a favour I'm doing you."

"It's me dying. The count's yours. I've told you what mine is. I've been choosing the day nine years, and I've chosen one that does a job for a man I carried out of this room once. I'd rather that than a day that does nothing, which is all the other days I had." He turned his head. "You give the bowl back. I've watched you do it seven years, and it's the reason I'm sitting here and not on my bucket. I'm asking you, once, to take one."

---

He took it.

"Your name," he said. "The whole of it. I've had Ulli for seven years and never the rest. I'm going to write it, and I want it right."

"Ullan," the deep man said. "Of Marl. The east fields, not the north forty; that's Pell's. Two l's. They always get it wrong. The Throat wrote it with one. Harrow says it with one. Nine years I've been Uli to every man on this road but you. You've been saying it with two and never knew, because you say a name as it sounds, and it sounds like two." He was quiet. "Write it with two."

"Ullan of Marl. The east fields. Two l's."

"That's it."

"And what you did. On Marl. That you're not going to tell me."

"No."

"Then I'll write that too. That there was a thing, and it was yours, and you kept it." Jonah put his hand flat on the rock of the floor. "That's what a count is. You say what's said, and you say you don't have it when you don't. He taught me that. It's the only thing he taught anyone he wasn't ashamed of."

Ulli said nothing. After a while he began to sing again. Jonah did not know the song and did not ask. By the third verse he understood that it was for a field at the end of the season, when the crop was in and the ground would lie fallow. Ulli had been singing it to himself since the winter.

---

He asked Tacitus that night, on the road, after the names. It was the first time he had asked it for something that was not for him.

Jonah said, in the old tongue, in the dark between the lamps, "He's going in. On the eighteenth. On purpose. He's going to sit down and stop singing, and the wall's going to do what it's wanted for nine years, slowly, because it's polite. I'm going to be sitting there." He put his hand on his chest. "I've felt it take a man. I felt it take the old man through my hand. I don't want to feel this one. I want to be there and not feel it. I don't know if that's something I can ask you."

It listened.

"You can hold me. You've shown me. Close on my heart and make me not a man for thirty days. I'm not asking that. I'm asking the other thing. Can you stand between. When it takes him. Can you be in the doorway and let me be in the room, and not let the room come through. So I can sit with him and sing and be there, without the count going through me. Just once."

It hesitated.

Then it did something new. It pressed, once, in the middle of him, where the seam had been and was now a scar. It was the nearest it had to a hand on his arm. A man beside you on a shelf in the dark does that, when you have said what cannot be answered.

It had no hand. It did that.

Jonah said, after a while, "All right. Thank you."

He went to the wall in the morning and cut his shard. Pell, beside him, did not look up and did not sing. At the change of shift he said, to the floor:

"Ullan's in the Cradle."

"Yes."

"He's not carrying anybody."

"No."

The old man from Marl cut his line for a long time. Then he set the chisel down against the wall and looked at his hands.

"Two l's."

"Yes."

"I've been saying it with one nine years. I'm Marl. I should have heard it." He picked the chisel up. "The east fields. They flood in the spring. That's why they sing the fallow song there, to tell the water the crop's in. His mother'd have sung it." He put the chisel to the Vein. "I'll sing it with him. On the day. If he'll have a north-forty man in his room."

"He'll have you."

"Then that's two of us, and you make three. Three's what a Marl field gets, at the end. The ones that made it, the ones that didn't, and the one that's going to lie fallow. That's how it goes."

He cut, and said nothing else. The road did not know anything yet. It would, in four days, and it would call it the Deep, and be right.
