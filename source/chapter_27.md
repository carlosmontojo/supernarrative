# Chapter 27: What You Ask of a Friend

On the fourteenth night Ulli was not on his bucket, and Jonah found him in the Cradle, sitting against the wall with his eyes closed, singing to it.

He stopped in the mouth of the gallery. He had not seen Ulli in the Cradle since the third day of his own dying, seven years ago, and he had thought, without ever quite thinking it, that Ulli did not go in unless there was a man to carry. There was no man. The Cradle had been empty since the winter, since the old man; the count was ten and nobody was dying, and the deep man sat against the warmest wall in the Pit with his knees up and his big hands hanging between them and sang, low, the song from the end of the road, the one Jonah did not know.

"Sit," Ulli said, without opening his eyes.

Jonah sat. Not across from him; beside him, with his own back to the Vein, the way they had sat seven years ago with the cold in the middle of Jonah and the rock tapping behind his head.

"You said the fourteenth."

"I did." The deep man's voice was rough and low and did not carry. "I've sat with it. Fourteen nights. I've sat with it the way I sit with the ones I carry, all the way through, till the wall's done. And it's done. So ask."

Jonah looked at the dark.

"Carry me," he said. "On the nineteenth. The harvest goes up on the twentieth and Harrow lids the last casks on the nineteenth and the Flamen seals them at dusk. I'll be in thirty-one before he lids it. I'll be in it from the sixth bell, held, and Harrow's men will lid it and carry it to the road's end and the Flamen'll put his rod on it, and it'll go up the hopper cage on the twentieth with the rest." He said it flat, in the road's tongue, the way he'd say a count. "And at the dusk count on the nineteenth, Decimus says four four seven one, and there's nobody. And you say he went in. You say, I carried him at the fourth bell, he'd been going seven years, he went, I sat with him, I took the blanket back. And Decimus makes a mark."

"And the wall."

"The wall never took me. Yes. When the College reads the road in two years with the glass and reads you, it reads a man who carried a cut one to the Cradle and said the wall took him, and it reads the wall, and the wall's got six hundred and forty-three in it and none of them's me." Jonah kept his voice level. "That's what I haven't counted. You said. I've sat with it fourteen nights too, Ulli, and it doesn't come out. There's nothing I can put on the other side of it. A man who lies to a Master's glass for a convict goes into the wall himself, or up the Throat to the slab, and either way he's in the count, and it's me that put him there."

The deep man did not say anything for a long time.

"You've got it wrong," he said at last. "The count. You've counted it like the old man would. Like it's a slate. Who owes, who fears, where the gap is. And you've come out to a thing that costs me, and you've stopped, because you're a man who gives the bowl back and you don't know how to take one." He opened his eyes and looked at the roof of the Cradle. "I'm not on the slate, cut one. I never was. I'm the one who carries."

"I don't understand."

"No. You don't. Seven years and you've never once asked me why I'm alive."

Jonah looked at him.

"Two years," Ulli said. "That's the Deep. Two years, if you're strong, and I've been in it nine. I came down the Throat at twenty-one for a thing I did on Marl that I'm not going to tell you, because it's the only thing I've got that's mine, and Harrow put me at the face, and the wall reached for me in the first month, the way it does, and I sang."

"You sang."

"I sang. I'm Marl. We sing to a field so it knows we're there. It's not a thing we think about; it's a thing we do, like breathing, my mother sang to the north forty every morning of her life and it never once made quota and she never once stopped. So the wall reached and I sang to it. And it stopped." The deep man's face did nothing. "I don't know why. The old man didn't know; I asked him through the rock, once, the only thing I ever asked, and he tapped back that he'd read every page and there wasn't one for it. But it stopped. It reached for me and I sang and it went still, the way your thing goes still under the glass, listening, and it's been listening nine years, and it's never once finished."

Jonah sat very still.

"And then they made me the one who carries," Ulli said. "Because I was the only man in the Deep the wall wouldn't take, so I was the only man who could sit in this room with it and not go in. And I carried. Forty-three. I sat with every one of them till the wall was done, and I sang, so they'd have something that wasn't the wall, and every time I sang the wall listened to me instead of finishing them fast, and they went slow, and some of them, the ones who could hear, heard something that wasn't come. That's what I did. Nine years." He was quiet a moment. "And it's the only thing I've done in my life that was worth the doing, and I did it in a room a man drew, and the man heard it through a wall and said so before he died. I've had that. Most men don't get that. Most men get a number."

"Ulli."

"I'm not finished." The deep man's voice did not change. "You've counted what it costs me when the College reads the road. It costs me nothing. I'll not be on the road. The day the freighter's gone, the day after, I'm going to sit down in this room with my back to it, and I'm going to stop."

Jonah did not move.

"Singing. I'm going to stop singing. And it's going to do what it's wanted to do for nine years and been too polite, listening, to do." Ulli looked at him then, for the first time, with the face that did nothing. "That's not you. Don't put it on the slate. I've been dying nine years, cut one, the same as you have, except I meant it, and I've known since the first month that this is how it ends, in this room, with my back to it, and I've been choosing the day since. I've chosen it. The day after the harvest. A man who carried a cut one in and went in himself the next week is a thing the road understands. It's a story. It's not a question. The College reads a road with a story on it and writes the story down, and the Master says, the wall is slow with some, and goes home."

"No."

"Yes." The deep man said it the way Decimus said a number. "I've carried forty-three. I know what the wall does to a man who's ready, and I know what it does to one who isn't, and I've watched you not be ready seven years and turn around three times, and it's not for you, going in. It's for me. I've been in this room longer than any man living. It's the only room I've got. I'd like to finish in it the way I've watched forty-three men finish, with somebody sitting there, and there's nobody, because the one who carries is me." He closed his eyes again. "So you'll do that. That's my price. Not a name on a slab. You'll sit with me. The day after. Before your freighter's a day out and you're in the dark and can't. You'll sit in this room with your back to it and you'll sing, and I don't care what, a yard song, one of your names, the prayer to Mars if that's what you've got, and I'll go, and you'll be there, and then you'll go up the Throat with that on you along with the rest."

"I'll be in the cask."

"Not till the sixth bell of the nineteenth. It's the fourteenth. There's five days." Ulli's mouth did something that on another man would have been the start of a smile. "I've counted, cut one. I can count too. The freighter loads on the twentieth. You're in the cask on the nineteenth. I go on the eighteenth. You sit with me on the eighteenth and you carry me to the count on the nineteenth, sick, and lay me down, and say to Decimus that the one who carries went, and then you go and get in your cask, and on the dusk count there's two of us gone in a week, and Harrow says, that's the Deep, that's how it goes, and nobody asks a Flamen anything."

Jonah sat with his back to the wall and could not find the door of the place where he kept things.

"That's not a price," he said. "That's you dying so the count comes out."

"It's me dying," Ulli agreed. "The count's yours. I've told you what mine is. I've been choosing the day nine years and I've chosen one that does a thing for a man I've carried out of this room once, and I'd rather that than a day that does nothing, which is all the other days I had." He turned his head. "You give the bowl back. It's the thing you do. I've watched you do it seven years and it's the reason I'm sitting here and not on my bucket. I'm asking you, once, to take one."

---

He took it.

He would tell that to the woman with the broad stripe, and she would not say anything for a long time, and then she would say, he chose, and Jonah would say, yes, and she would say, then it's his, and Jonah would say, it's on the slab in my hand. The deep man who sang. Marl. Nine years. He carried forty-three. And beside it, because the old man had said you write what is said: he asked me to sit.

"Your name," he said, in the Cradle, on the fourteenth night, with his back to the wall. "The whole of it. I've had Ulli for seven years and I've never had the rest, and I'm going to write it, and I want it right."

"Ullan," the deep man said. "Of Marl. The east fields, not the north forty; that's Pell's. Two l's." He said it flat. "They always get it wrong. The Throat wrote it with one. Harrow says it with one. Nine years I've been Uli to every man on this road but you, and you've been saying it with two and you never knew, because you say a thing the way it sounds and it sounds like two." He was quiet. "Write it with two."

"Ullan of Marl. The east fields. Two l's."

"That's it."

"And the thing you did. On Marl. That you're not going to tell me."

"No."

"Then I'll write that too. That there was a thing, and it was yours, and you kept it." Jonah put his hand flat on the rock of the floor. "That's what a count is. You say what's said and you say you don't have it when you don't. He taught me that. It's the only thing he taught anyone he wasn't ashamed of."

Ulli did not say anything.

After a while he began to sing. It was the song from the end of the road, the one that was not for Jonah, and Jonah sat beside him with his back to the wall and did not know it and did not ask, and understood, by the third verse, without knowing how, that it was a thing a man from Marl sang over a field at the end of the season, when the crop was in and the field was done and would lie fallow, and that Ulli had been singing it to himself since the winter.

---

He asked Tacitus that night, on the road, after the names, and it was the first time he had asked it a thing that was not for him.

"He's going in," he said, in the old tongue, in the dark between the lamps. "On the eighteenth. On purpose. He's going to sit down and stop singing and the wall's going to do what it's wanted to do for nine years, and it's going to be slow, because it's the wall and it's polite, and I'm going to be sitting there." He put his hand on his chest. "I've heard it take a man. I've felt it take the old man, through my hand, from the hole in. I don't want to feel this one. I want to be there and I don't want to feel it, and I don't know if that's a thing I can ask you."

It listened.

"You can hold me. You've shown me. You can close on my heart and make me not a man for thirty days. I'm not asking that. I'm asking the other thing. Can you." He stopped. He did not have the word, in either tongue. "Can you stand between. When it takes him. Can you be in the doorway and let me be in the room and not let the room come through. So I can sit with him and sing and be there and not have the count go through me the way it went through me in the Mouth. Just for that. Just once."

It did the doorway thing. The deciding.

And then it did a thing it had not done, and he stood in the dark and felt it and did not, for a long moment, understand it, because it was not settling and it was not listening and it was not the thing that was like a man deciding whether to go through a door. It was smaller than any of those and it was in the middle of him, in the place where the seam had been and was a scar, and it was, he thought, when he had it, the thing a man does beside you on a shelf in the dark when you have said a thing that cannot be answered and he puts his hand, once, on your arm.

It had no hand. It did that.

"All right," Jonah said, after a while. "All right. Thank you."

He went to the wall in the morning and cut his shard. It came off for him. Pell, beside him, did not look up, and did not sing, and at the change of shift said, to the floor, in the voice he used for things he had seen before:

"Ullan's in the Cradle."

"Yes."

"He's not carrying anybody."

"No."

The old man from Marl cut his line. He cut it for a long time, and then he set the chisel down against the wall the way he had set it down on the day the quota went to twelve, and looked at his hands, and said:

"Two l's."

"Yes."

"I've been saying it with one nine years. I'm Marl. I should have heard it." He picked the chisel up. "The east fields. They flood in the spring; that's why they sing the fallow song there, to tell the water the crop's in. His mother'd have sung it." He put the chisel to the Vein. "I'll sing it with him. On the day. If he'll have a north-forty man in his room."

"He'll have you."

"Then that's two of us," Pell said, "and you make three, and three's what a Marl field gets, at the end, the ones that made it and the ones that didn't and the one that's going to lie fallow. That's how it goes." And he cut, and did not say anything else, and at the dusk count Decimus said his number and he said it back, and the road did not know anything yet, and would, and would call it the Deep, and would be right.
