# Chapter 17: Decimus

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"You've a minute. Sit."

Jonah looked up from the buckets. Guards did not say sit. Guards said cut, and count, and post.

It was the end of the second year. The cage at night was a young man's post, given to the ones who had come down with the twelve and had not learned the road yet. Decimus had the dawn count and the dusk count and the road between. But tonight he was on the stool at the cage end with the rifle across his knees, the lamp above him, and a paper in his hand. He had not looked at the buckets.

Jonah sat. On the rock, against the cage frame. Far down the Deep road Ulli was singing to nobody.

"You read," Decimus said. "Bricks says. Says you read the deputy's slate upside down and told him what it said."

"I read."

He almost added *and count, and remember, none of which has done me any good yet.* He didn't. Decimus hadn't asked for a speech. He'd asked a fact.

"Common speech. Not the other."

"Common speech."

"Good." The old guard looked at the paper. Cheap grey garrison paper, folded small, folded and unfolded a great many times. "Came down with the last transport. I've read it enough I could say it to you. I'd like it read once by somebody who's not me, so I can hear it. Nobody up top reads. Nobody up top talks. Sabinus talks, and I'd sooner cut my own hand off than hand him this." He held it out. "It's from my daughter."

Jonah took it.

---

She was nine. Her name was Sula. She lived with her mother's sister on Etruria, in a foundry town, and she went to a school. The school had a garden. In the garden was a tree she had been given the care of, and it had not died, and she wanted him to know that. She had drawn it at the bottom of the page: a trunk, and more leaves than any tree Jonah had ever seen. Under it, in a very careful hand, MINE.

He read it out. All of it. It was not long. She hoped he was well. She hoped it was not too dark. She had learned the seven hills and wrote all seven, and two were spelled wrong. Jonah read them as she had spelled them, because they were hers. The aunt was well. She would be ten in the spring, and the aunt said he would be home the winter after. She had counted the winters. Two. She had drawn that too: two marks, scratched at the edge of the page. A wall count.

She said she loved him, at the end, where children put it. Jonah read it level and did not look up.

Decimus held out his hand. Jonah gave the paper back. The guard folded it along its folds and put it inside his coat, against his chest.

"Two winters," Decimus said.

"She counted."

"She's always counted. Before she could talk, her mother said. Fingers. Then stones. Then the days till I came." He looked at the lamp. "Her mother's dead. Four years. The College grew her a lung when the first one went, and she had two more years on it. Then the other went, and there's no growing two. That's what this is for." He touched the coat. "A guard's pay down here is three times Etruria's, because nobody wants it. It goes to the aunt. The aunt keeps her, and the school has a tree. That's the whole of why I'm on this stool. It's not complicated."

"Two years."

"Two and a bit. Twenty on Etruria, five here. At the end of the five they give you a paper and a house on Campania if you want one. I don't. I want the foundry town and the tree." He was quiet a moment. "So I'll finish the five. Whatever it costs."

Jonah sat with his hands on his knees and did not answer.

He was looking at the plate.

He sat three paces from a man of forty-five with a line on his arm and a Tiro cutting fused at seven, never once drilled past. Pale grey plate on the forearms, so old the man did not notice it. The genius looked at that plate and had an opinion. The opinion was that it was nothing. A chisel through the hip seam, same as Vetch, a count of twenty, folded away. The stool empty. Rennick would carry fifteen up the Throat and nobody the wiser, because Decimus had sat down on a post that was not his. Old guards went into the Deep and touched the wall. Decimus had said so himself.

He saw it all at once. The way he saw debris.

His right hand came up off his knee.

He had not lifted it. It went toward the chisel at his belt, and the genius went with it, hungry, leaning across three paces of rock at a cutting nobody would count. The two echoes in him, the decurion and the boy, had one opinion about guards on stools. Jonah put the hand down flat on the rock and held it there. He thought, in the old tongue, stop, as he had held the door of the wall in the Cradle.

It stopped. His hand stayed on the rock. He let the old man talk.

---

"I know about Vetch," Decimus said.

He said it to the lamp. Neither of them turned his head.

"He didn't walk into the Deep. He was twenty-two and a fool, and a fool walks where he's not put, that's true. But he didn't. He went into the seventh chamber at the end of a shift with two men in it and one shard, and he didn't come out. Next morning one of the two had a garrison chisel and the other had a fever." He shifted the rifle. "I said 'they do' to you at the count. I said it so it was said. I've been saying things so they're said for twenty-five years and I've never once said the thing I know. I'm not starting on a stool at the bottom of a rock with two winters to go."

"Why not?"

"Because it's not personal." Decimus looked at him, finally. "You've heard me say it. I say it to every man on the road. It's true, and it's the only way a man does this for twenty-five years and goes home. It's not personal when I count you. It's not personal when I hand a foreman the strap. It's not personal when a fool of a boy goes into a chamber with a fang out and doesn't come back."

He turned back to the lamp.

"And it wouldn't be personal if I told Sabinus, and Sabinus took you up the Throat and put you on the slab. Did to you what he did to a Marl officer I stood beside on the ramp your first day. I don't do personal. Either way. I count, and I go home. That's all."

The thing Jonah wanted to say was uglier: that a man who counted long enough eventually became one of the things counted, and stopped noticing the difference. He didn't say it. Decimus had brought him a letter to read, not a mirror to look in.

"That's not all," Jonah said.

"No." A pause. "No, it's not. But it's the part I can say on a stool."

Ulli's song came up the road, faint, and stopped, and started again.

"He was going to kill Pell, and Pell's sixty, slow, and never once raised a hand to anybody on this road," Jonah said.

"I know."

"In a quarter hour. He wasn't waiting. I've seen the ones that wait."

"I know that too." Decimus put a hand on the rifle. Not to lift it. To remind himself it was there. "I've been on that road three years with him. I've watched him make shards nobody could make and give his bowl back and break a foreman's hand. I've watched him turn around in the Cradle, which nobody does. I've heard him sing with Ulli some nights, when he thinks the road's asleep. He's sixty and he's Marl and he'll die on that wall. I'll count him, and it won't be personal." He stopped. "But I'd have liked it later. Later than a quarter hour with a fool's fang under his chin. So I said 'they do'. I'll say it again if I'm asked. That's the whole of what I've got to give a man, four four seven one. A thing said so it's said. It's not much. It's what a guard has."

Jonah looked at him.

He thought about the bandage. About the sergeant on Anvil who had said please, and the grey Blanks under the quaestor's house who had not needed to. About a man who brought a bandage down ten kilometres of rock because it was in his pocket, and who had just, in the only way he had, decided not to kill him.

"Why me?" he said. "The letter. Bricks reads."

"Bricks reads numbers." Decimus almost smiled. He had done it perhaps four times in three years. "You read the deputy's slate upside down and told him it was a Collar count. That's a man who reads what's under a thing. And you gave the bowl back. Third night. Bricks told me and I didn't believe him. I've watched you since. You gave Pell the chisel. You gave Corr ten hard so the road wouldn't think you were soft, then sat beside him and said nothing, which is what a man does when he's sorry and can't say so." He touched the coat. "You read the hills the way she spelled them. I wanted it read right, once. That's why."

---

They talked for an hour. It was the only hour in seven years that a man with a line on his arm sat with him and the line meant nothing.

Decimus talked about Etruria. Foundries that ran day and night and lit the sky so there was never a full dark. Legion depots where a Blank boy enlisted at sixteen and was a guard at twenty, with a cutting invested at one and drilled to seven and stopped, because seven was what a guard needed. Nine cost a trim at the end of every licence.

"I saw one," he said. "A trim. Tullus. His first year here, my first. He'd come off a real licence, the war before this, and he was fifty-two. The Flamen came down the Throat with the rod and put his thumb on him in the garrison hall with the whole guard watching. That's how they do it, so everyone sees. It took a day." His voice was flat. "I don't know what fifty-two feels like. I don't want to. I know what it looks like when a man loses it. I never asked for the nine after. I said, not worth the trim, and they laughed. I've been seven for twenty years, and I'm going home."

He talked about the Empire, and did not use the word. Blanks on Etruria said the Republic, same as the quaestor, without thinking. A town with a school in it. A College that grew lungs. Roads that ran. A peace that had held on the core worlds for three hundred and ninety years, so a foundry man's daughter could have the care of a tree. He said it as Tom had said the ladder. Not arguing. It was simply so.

"You'll think I'm a fool," he said.

"No."

"You will. You were a Collar, and you've been three years on a road where a wall grows plate for a legion out of men's backs. I'm sitting here with a rifle telling you about a school. I know what it sounds like." He looked at the rock overhead. "It's both. Nobody down here will tell you that and nobody up top will either. It's a school with a tree and it's a wall that eats Marl. It's the same thing. Twenty-five years and I've never found the place where one stops and the other starts. I stopped looking. A man who looks for that place doesn't go home." He was quiet. "You'll look. You've been looking since you turned around in the Cradle. I hope you find it. I hope it's somewhere I never have to stand."

Jonah looked at the old man and could not hate him. He had known since the bandage that he would not be able to. It was the heaviest thing he carried.

---

Decimus stood at the end of the hour. His knees made the sound all their knees made now. He put the rifle on his shoulder and looked down the Deep road, then at Jonah. Jonah understood, hearing what came next, that the letter had been the way to it and not the reason.

"If I don't finish the five," Decimus said.

Jonah waited.

"If something happens. On the road. In the Deep. It happens to guards, you've seen. Vetch." No weight on the name. "If it happens before the two winters, the garrison burns me on the surface and sends the aunt a paper. The paper has my number on it, because that's what a guard is up top, a number, same as you are down here. And nobody says it."

"Says what?"

"My name. The whole of it. The aunt gets the paper and says it to Sula, and Sula's ten and counting, and that's all there is." The old guard looked at him. "You count. Bricks says. You've a thing you do with names at night on the road. He's heard you. He thinks you're praying. I don't think you're praying."

"I'm not praying."

"Then whatever it is." The face in the lamp was the one from the tunnel, the one that had waited while a boy knelt. "If it happens. Say it. Once. Wherever you say the rest. So it's said by somebody who's not a paper."

"What is it?"

"Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. The foundry town. Sula's father." He said it plainly, in the voice for things that were not personal. It was the most personal thing anyone had said to Jonah in three years. They both knew it and neither said so. "That's all."

"Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father," Jonah said.

"Good." He shifted the rifle. "Now pick up your buckets. You've a road to walk and I've a count at dawn. You're late, four four seven one, and it's not personal, but you are."

Jonah picked up the buckets.

He walked the Deep road past the lamps that were too far apart. At the end of it Ulli opened his eyes on his bucket and closed them again. In the Cradle the rock opened and the old man came out with the lamp and the stick. He looked at Jonah's face and said, in the old tongue, "Who?"

"Decimus."

"Dead?"

"No. He asked me to say his name if he is."

The Censor stood with the stick on his shoulder a moment.

"Then you have it. Keep it with the rest. Not on the slabs. The slabs are for the ones who came down the Throat, and he came down it on a stool, which is not the same. I will not write a guard beside Pell's brother." He lifted the stick. "But keep it. You will be asked for it. Get up."

The stick came out of the dark before he had finished standing. It took him across the left forearm, hard, and he went down on the rock and got up. The second came for the knee. He stepped, late and ugly, and it caught his hip instead of the joint. The third he took on the back of the hand. The plate came up to meet it, and the old man grunted, and hit him again.

He said the names that night on the road, with the buckets, in the dark between the lamps. All of them, in the order he had them, out of order. At the end he said the new one. The genius, which an hour ago had wanted a guard's cutting off him through the hip seam in a count of twenty, listened to the name of the man on the stool. It added it to the names it expected, and did not wander.

"Decimus Alba," Jonah said, to the dark. "Of Etruria. Sula's father. Two winters."

He did not know, saying it, that he would say it again on the same road with the old man's stick broken and the old man dead and a Centurion's fang in his hand. That it would be the only thing holding the plate on him. He was glad, later, that he had not known. It had been, whatever came after, a good hour.
