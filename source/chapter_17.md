# Chapter 17: Decimus

The guard had a letter, and he had nobody to read it to, and that was how Jonah learned his other name.

It was the third year, or the end of the second; the seasons came down the Throat as numbers and the numbers had been twelve for so long that they had stopped meaning a season. Jonah was on the haul, as he always was, with two buckets and the Deep road ahead of him, and at the cage end of it, where the guard sat on a stool under a lamp with a rifle across his knees and watched the buckets come, the guard that night was Decimus.

He was not usually. The cage at night was a young man's post, a thing given to the ones who had come down with the twelve and had not yet learned what the road was, and Decimus had the dawn count and the dusk count and the road between, and Jonah saw him twice a day and said his number to him and had said it perhaps eight hundred times. But he was on the stool tonight, with the rifle and the lamp, and a paper in his hand, and when Jonah set the buckets down at the cage he did not look at them.

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"You've a minute. Sit."

Jonah looked at him. Guards did not say sit. Guards said cut, and count, and post.

He sat. On the rock, against the cage frame, with the buckets beside him and the old guard on his stool above with the paper, and for a while Decimus did not say anything, and the Deep road was quiet the way it was at that hour, and somewhere far down it Ulli was singing to nobody.

"You read," Decimus said. "Bricks said. Said you read the deputy's slate upside down once and told him what it said."

"I read."

"Common speech. Not the other."

"Common speech."

"Good." The old guard looked at the paper in his hand. It was the cheap grey paper that came down with the transports for the garrison, folded small, and it had been folded and unfolded a great many times. "This came down with the last one. I've read it. I've read it enough that I could say it to you, but I'd like it read, once, by somebody who's not me, so I can hear it. Nobody up top reads. Nobody up top talks. Sabinus talks, and I'd rather cut my own hand off than hand him this." He held it out. "It's from my daughter."

Jonah took it.

---

She was nine. Her name was Sula. She lived with her mother's sister on Etruria, in a town whose name Jonah did not know, near a foundry, and she went to a school, and the school had a garden, and in the garden there was a tree that she had been given the care of, and it had not died, and she wanted him to know that. She had drawn the tree. It was at the bottom of the page, in the same cheap grey, a thing with a trunk and a great many more leaves than any tree Jonah had ever seen, and under it she had written, in a hand that was very careful, MINE.

He read it out. All of it. It was not long. She hoped he was well. She hoped it was not too dark. She had learned the names of the seven hills and could say them and did, on the page, all seven, and two of them were spelled wrong, and Jonah read them the way she had spelled them and did not correct them, because it was hers. She said the aunt was well. She said the tree. She said she would be ten in the spring and that the aunt had said he would be home by the winter after, and she had counted the winters, and it was two, and she had drawn that too, two marks, at the edge of the page, like the marks a man makes on a wall.

She said she loved him. She said it at the end, the way children say it, as a thing that goes at the end, and Jonah read it in the voice he used for reading, level, and did not look up.

When he was done Decimus held out his hand, and Jonah gave the paper back, and the old guard folded it along the folds it already had and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where a man keeps a thing he is not going to lose.

"Two winters," Decimus said.

"She counted."

"She's always counted. She was counting before she could talk, her mother said. Fingers. Then stones. Then the days till I came." He looked at the lamp. "Her mother's dead. Four years. The College grew her a lung when the first one went and she had two more years on it, which she'd not have had, and then the other went and there's no growing two. That's what this is for." He touched the coat, over the paper. "The Pit. A guard's pay down here is three times what it is on Etruria, because nobody wants it, and it's paid to the aunt, and the aunt keeps her, and the school has a tree. That's the whole of why I'm on this stool. It's not complicated."

"Two years."

"Two and a bit. I've done twenty on Etruria and five here and at the end of the five they give you a paper that says you've served and a house on Campania if you want one, which I don't, I want the foundry town and the tree." The old guard was quiet a moment. "So I'll finish the five. Whatever it costs. That's what a paper's for."

Jonah sat against the cage with his hands on his knees and did not say anything.

He was thinking about the plate.

Not in the way he'd thought about it in his father's room, with five vigiles and two grey fangs. That had been a thing he could do. This was a thing he could see. He sat three paces from a man of forty-five with a line on his arm and a Tiro cutting fused at seven that he had never once, in twenty-five years, drilled past, a man with a rifle across his knees and pale grey plate on his forearms that he did not notice, and the thing under his ribs looked at that plate the way it looked at everything now, with an opinion, and the opinion was that it was nothing. That it would come off with a chisel through the hip seam the way Vetch's had, in a count of twenty, and be folded away, and the coat would get bigger by a size Jonah could name, and the stool would be empty, and Rennick would carry fifteen up the Throat, and nobody would be the wiser, because Decimus had sat down on a night post that was not his, and old guards went into the Deep and touched the wall, they did, Decimus had said so himself.

He could see it the way he saw debris. All at once. Without looking.

He put it away.

He did not put it away easily. That was the thing he would keep from this night, later, with the rest. It went into the place where he kept things and it did not want to go, because the thing under his ribs was hungry, always, with a size, and had not eaten since Vetch, and there was a genius three paces off that nobody would count, and the echoes in him, the decurion and the boy, were looking at a guard on a stool and had one opinion between them about guards on stools. He held it. He held it the way he'd held the door of the wall in the Cradle, and he thought, in the old tongue, stop, and it stopped, and he sat with his hands on his knees and let the old man talk.

---

"I know about Vetch," Decimus said.

He said it to the lamp. He had not turned his head. Jonah did not turn his either.

"I know he didn't walk into the Deep. He was twenty-two and he was a fool and a fool walks where he's not put, that's true, I've seen it, but he didn't. He went into the seventh chamber at the end of a shift with two men in it and one shard, and he didn't come out, and one of the two men had a garrison chisel the next morning and the other had a fever." The old guard shifted the rifle on his knees. "I said 'they do' to you at the count. I said it so it was said. I've been saying things so they're said for twenty-five years and I've never once said the other thing, the thing I know, and I'm not going to start on a stool at the bottom of a rock with two winters to go."

"Why not?"

"Because it's not personal." Decimus looked at him, finally. "You'll have heard me say that. I say it to every man on the road. I say it because it's true, and I say it because it's the only way a man does this for twenty-five years and goes home. It's not personal when I count you. It's not personal when I hand a foreman the strap. It's not personal when a fool of a boy goes into a chamber with a fang out and doesn't come back, and it wouldn't be personal if I told Sabinus, and Sabinus took you up the Throat, and put you on the slab, and did to you what he did to a Marl officer I stood beside on the ramp your first day and watched die screaming." He turned back to the lamp. "I don't do personal. Either way. I count, and I go home. That's all."

"That's not all."

"No." The old guard was quiet a moment. "No, it's not. But it's the part I can say on a stool."

The road was quiet. Ulli's song came up it, faint, and stopped, and started again.

"He was going to kill Pell," Jonah said.

"I know."

"In a quarter hour. He wasn't waiting. Pell said. I've seen the ones that wait and he wasn't one."

"I know that too." Decimus put a hand on the rifle. Not to lift it. The way a man puts a hand on a thing to remind himself it's there. "I've been on that road three years with him. I've watched him make shards nobody could make and give his bowl back and break a foreman's hand and turn around in the Cradle, which nobody does, and sing, some nights, with Ulli, when he thinks the road's asleep. He's sixty and he's Marl and he'll die on that wall and I'll count him, and it won't be personal." He stopped. "But I'd have liked it to be later. That's all. I'd have liked it later than a quarter hour with a fool's fang under his chin. So I said 'they do'. And I'll say it again if I'm asked. And that's the whole of what I've got to give a man, four four seven one, a thing said so it's said. It's not much. It's what a guard has."

Jonah looked at him.

He thought about the bandage. About the sergeant on Anvil, who had said please, and about the grey Blanks in the corridor under the quaestor's house who had not needed to. He thought about a man who brought a thing down ten kilometres of rock because it was in his pocket, and who sat on a stool with a letter against his chest and said it's not personal to a man he had just, quietly, in the only way he had, decided not to kill.

"Why me?" he said. "The letter. You could have had Bricks read it. Bricks reads."

"Bricks reads numbers." Decimus almost smiled. It was the thing from the tunnel on the first day, the thing he had done perhaps four times in three years. "You read the deputy's slate upside down and told him it was a Collar count. That's not numbers. That's a man who reads what's under a thing." He looked at the lamp. "And you gave the bowl back. On the third night. Bricks told me and I didn't believe him, and I've watched you since, and you've done it, one way and another, a dozen times, and you don't know you're doing it. You gave Pell the chisel. You gave Corr ten hard so the road wouldn't think you were soft and then you sat beside him and said nothing, which is what a man does when he's sorry and can't say so. You'd have read a child's letter right. You did. You read the hills the way she spelled them." He touched the coat. "I wanted it read right, once. That's why."

---

They talked for an hour. Jonah would try to remember all of it, later, and would get most of it, because he had learned by then to keep things, and because it was the only hour in seven years that a man with a line on his arm sat with him as if the line meant nothing.

Decimus talked about Etruria. About the foundries, which ran day and night and lit the sky over the town so that there was never a full dark, and about the legions' depots, where a Blank boy could enlist at sixteen and be a guard at twenty and have a cutting invested at one and drill it to seven and stop, because seven was what a guard needed and nine cost a trim at the end of every licence and a trim was not worth it, not for two grades, not for a man who was going home.

"I saw one," he said. "A trim. Tullus. His first year here, my first year. He'd come off a licence, a real one, the war before this, and he was fifty-two, and the Flamen came down the Throat with the rod and put his thumb on him in the garrison hall with the whole guard watching, because that's how they do it, so everyone sees, and it took a day." The old guard's voice was flat. "I don't know what fifty-two feels like. I don't want to. I know what it looks like when a man loses it. I've never asked for the nine since. I said, not worth the trim, and they laughed, and I've been seven for twenty years, and I'm going home."

He talked about the Empire. Not the word. He did not use the word; Blanks did not, on Etruria, they said the Republic the way the quaestor had, without thinking, and Jonah heard it and put it with the rest. He talked about a town with a school in it, and a College that grew lungs, and roads that ran, and a peace that had held on the core worlds for three hundred and ninety years so that a foundry man's daughter could have the care of a tree. He said it the way Tom had said the ladder. Not arguing. As a thing that was so.

"You'll think I'm a fool," he said.

"No."

"You will. You're a Collar, you were, and you've been on a road three years where a wall grows plate for a legion out of men's backs, and I'm sitting here with a rifle telling you about a school. I know what it sounds like." He looked at the rock overhead. "It's both. That's what nobody down here will tell you and nobody up top will either. It's a school with a tree and it's a wall that eats Marl. It's the same thing. I've been a guard twenty-five years and I've never once found the place where one stops and the other starts, and I stopped looking, because a man who looks for that place doesn't go home." He was quiet. "You'll look. I can see it on you. You've been looking since you turned around in the Cradle. I hope you find it. I hope it's somewhere I never have to stand."

Jonah sat with his back to the cage and looked at the old man, and could not hate him, and had known since the bandage that he would not be able to, and it was the heaviest thing in the place where he kept things.

---

Decimus stood up at the end of the hour. His knees made the sound that all their knees made now. He put the rifle on his shoulder and looked down the Deep road toward the lamps that were too far apart, and then at Jonah, and said the thing he had been on the stool to say, and Jonah understood, hearing it, that the letter had been the way to it and not the reason.

"If I don't finish the five," Decimus said.

Jonah waited.

"If something happens. On the road. In the Deep. Whatever it is. It happens to guards, you've seen. Vetch." He did not say the name with any weight. "If it happens to me before the two winters, the garrison'll burn me on the surface with the rest and send the aunt a paper, and the paper'll have my number on it, because that's what a guard is up top, a number, same as you are down here. And nobody'll say it."

"Say what?"

"My name. The whole of it. Up top they burn a guard and say the number, and on Etruria the aunt'll get the paper and she'll say it to Sula, and Sula'll be ten and counting, and that's all there'll be." The old guard looked at him. "You count. Bricks says. You've a thing you do, with the names, at night, on the road. He's heard you. He thinks you're praying. I don't think you're praying."

"I'm not praying."

"Then whatever it is." Decimus's face in the lamp was the face from the tunnel, the one that had waited while a boy knelt. "If it happens. Say it. Once. Wherever you say the rest. So it's said by somebody who's not a paper."

Jonah looked up at him for a long moment.

"What is it?"

"Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. The foundry town. Sula's father." The old man said it plainly, in the voice for things that were not personal, and it was the most personal thing anyone had said to Jonah in three years, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so. "That's all. That's the whole of it."

"Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father."

"Good." The guard shifted the rifle. "Now pick up your buckets. You've a road to walk and I've a count at dawn, and you're late, four four seven one, and it's not personal, but you are."

Jonah picked up the buckets.

He walked down the Deep road with them past the lamps that were too far apart, and at the end of it, on his bucket, Ulli opened his eyes and looked at him and closed them again, and in the Cradle the rock opened and the old man came out with the lamp and the stick and looked at Jonah's face and said, in the old tongue, "Who?"

"Decimus."

"Dead?"

"No. He asked me to say his name if he is."

The Censor stood with the stick on his shoulder for a moment.

"Then you have it," he said. "Put it where you keep things. Not on the slabs; the slabs are for the ones who come down the Throat, and he came down it on a stool, which is not the same, and I will not write a guard beside Pell's brother." He lifted the stick. "But keep it. You will be asked for it. Get up."

He got up. He'd been standing; the old man said get up whatever he was doing, it was the whole of what he said, and Jonah had stopped hearing it as anything but the start of the hour.

He said the names that night on the road, after, with the buckets, in the dark between the lamps, all of them, in the order he had them, out of order, and when he got to the end he said the new one, and the thing under his ribs, which had wanted an hour ago to take a guard's cutting off him through the hip seam in a count of twenty, listened to the name of the man on the stool and added it to the things it expected, and did not wander.

"Decimus Alba," Jonah said, to the dark. "Of Etruria. Sula's father. Two winters."

And he did not know, saying it, that he would say it again on the same road with the old man's stick broken and the old man dead and a Centurion's fang in his hand, and that it would be the only thing that held the plate on him, and he was glad, later, that he had not known. It would have made the hour on the stool a different hour. And it had been, whatever came after, a good one.
