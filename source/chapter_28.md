# Chapter 28: Harrow

A cask that said two hundred and forty on the slate had to weigh two hundred and forty on the dock, and Jonah weighed something, and the thing he weighed had to come out of thirty-one in slabs.

He did it on the sixteenth night, alone, without a lamp.

He had counted the loading gallery for six nights before he touched it. Harrow's men slept in the slab galleries with their gloves off and did not go past the turn after the last bell, because the casks were the Flamen's now and a man found beside a sealed cask at night was a man with a question on him. The Flamen himself slept up the road in the cut Tullus's men had made for him, with a lamp and a door, and did not come down before the fourth bell. Harrow slept in the gallery with his men. Jonah had lain on his shelf and put his hand flat on the rock and felt the Deep the way he had learned to feel it since the Mouth, boots and hearts through stone, and had counted eleven hearts in the slab galleries at the second bell every night for six nights, slow, asleep, and none past the turn.

So he went past the turn.

Thirty-one was in the third row. It was lidded but not sealed; Harrow's men lidded a cask when it was packed and the Flamen marked it on the day it went to the road's end, and thirty-one's day was the nineteenth, with the last eight. Jonah put both hands on the lid and felt for the groove and lifted, and the lid came up the way the Chamber door had come up, without any weight to it that his hands could feel, and he set it against the next cask and put his arm into the dark.

A hundred and twelve slabs, packed right. He took one out.

He held it in the whole dark of the gallery with the Vein breathing in the walls around him and the cask breathing in his hands, because it was the wall, and the slab was the wall, and the thing under his skin knew it for what it was before he had it clear of the groove. The hunger came up. It came up the way it had in the Mouth, all at once, not in his belly, in the doorway, and he stood with the slab in his two hands and let it come, because that was what he had come down for.

He ate.

Not the way he had eaten the shackle's teeth or Varro's fang. Slowly. He put the plate on his palms and let it go into the slab the way the wall had gone into the old man's sixteen, a bite at a time, and the slab went in through his hands and was not, and he felt it arrive in the middle of him and settle, and the settling did not fight him. That was new. It had fought him for a month since the Mouth, thirty-one to thirty-two, a day at a time, the way the old man had said it would, and he had stood in the line for Livia's glass with it fighting him and had said be nothing and it had been. It did not fight now. It took the slab and was quiet, the way a man is quiet after a meal who has been hungry for six years, and he stood in the dark and thought, with the part of him that counted, there's the top of thirty-two. That's the top of it. Anything past this is a grade I can't hide.

He ate eleven. He counted. He stopped at eleven because his hands had begun to shake in the wrong way, not the six years' way, the way they had shaken on the road with the fever after the Mouth, and because a slab that was in him was a slab that was not in a cask, and a cask with forty gone from it would sing wrong when Harrow knocked it, and Harrow knocked every cask.

The rest he carried.

Two at a time, up the gallery, to the cut in the far wall where the deep-diggers had stopped cutting when the knot came, and laid them against the rock in the dark, flat, in a row, the way the old man's had leaned against the Cradle. Thirty-one slabs. He would have to move them again; he knew that. He did not know yet where. He had counted six things and this was the seventh and it had not come out, and he laid the last slab down and stood up and put his hand on the rock to feel the gallery, and the gallery had twelve hearts in it.

He did not move.

Eleven in the slab galleries, slow, asleep. One at the turn. Not asleep. Not slow. Standing where a man stands when he has come round a corner and seen a thing and stopped, and the heart was going the way a heart goes in a man who is fair and afraid and has been afraid for nine years and has just found the thing he was afraid of.

"Harrow," Jonah said, into the dark.

The lamp came on.

---

The deep foreman had it shuttered. He opened it a crack and held it low, at his knee, so it would not carry up the road, and he stood at the turn with it and did not come nearer, and the light went across the floor of the gallery to the third row and to thirty-one with its lid off and its dark mouth open, and to the far cut, and to the row of slabs laid flat against the rock, and to Jonah, standing over them with the plate still on his hands to the wrist.

"Put that away," Harrow said. His voice was very quiet. "Whatever it is. Put it away, I don't want to see it."

Jonah put it away.

"I count the casks." The foreman did not move from the turn. "Every night since he came. I lie down with the men and I wait till they're under and I come and I knock the rows, because the Flamen's going to knock them too, on the day, that's what a rod's for, and if one sings wrong it's my packing and it's my neck, and I've packed nine harvests and I have never once had one sing wrong." He looked at thirty-one. "Fifteen nights it's sung right. I knocked it tonight and it sang hollow."

"Yes."

"And I thought, that's the Deep, that's a cask settling. And I went to bed. And I lay there." Harrow's lamp did not shake. That was the thing Jonah would remember; the man was afraid all the way through and the lamp did not shake. "And I thought about a foreman who likes the walk, who was on the haul and carried the seventh cask and asked me how long a freighter takes. And a deputy prefect who came down the road with a slate and stood in this gallery and wrote them light, and a Collar who carried it up. And I got up."

Jonah stood with his hands empty in the crack of the light.

"How much did you take?"

"Forty-two."

"Forty-two." Harrow said the number back the way Decimus said a number. "There's thirty-one there. Where's eleven?"

Jonah did not say anything.

"Where's eleven, four four seven one."

"In me."

The lamp did not shake. The foreman's face did what it did when the wall bit a man and he had to write it, and he looked at the row of slabs against the rock, and at the cask, and at Jonah, and Jonah felt his heart through the rock under his feet and it was going very fast and it was not going away.

"You're going up in it," Harrow said.

"Yes."

"On the twentieth. Sealed. In the dark. As matter." The foreman had it all at once, the way the old man had it, and Jonah watched him have it and did not stop him. "Seventy slabs round you, packed light, two forty on the slate in the deputy's own hand, and the Flamen puts his rod on the lid and it's shut, and the surface weighs it and it's two forty, and Rennick's written two forty, and nobody on this rock looks twice at a cask that weighs what the slate says." He breathed. "And you're not at the dusk count. And somebody says you went in the wall."

"Yes."

"Ulli."

"Ulli's going in himself. On the eighteenth."

Harrow closed his eyes.

He stood at the turn with the lamp at his knee and his eyes shut and Jonah watched the light on the rock and waited, because there was nothing on the slate for this, there had never been, it was the seventh thing and it had come looking for him the way the fourth had, and he could feel the shape of what it would take to make it come out and he did not want to. He could. The hunger said so. It was in the doorway with eleven slabs in it and it looked at a man with a heart going fast at twenty paces and it said, plainly, without words, that one. It would be quick. Harrow was Tiro, unstriped, a Collar with nine harvests and a girl somewhere he had not said yet, and Jonah was thirty-two and settled and could cross the gallery before the lamp hit the floor, and there would be twelve hearts in the Deep, and then eleven, and one of them would be a foreman who had gone in the wall in the night in a week when two other men did, and that was the Deep, that was how it goes.

He stood where he was. He put his hand on the rock, so that Tacitus would have something to hold that was not the man at the turn, and said, in his head, in the old tongue: no. Not that one. He carries the buckets with his gloves off. Not that one.

It settled. It did not like it. It settled.

"I've got a girl," Harrow said, with his eyes shut.

Jonah waited.

"On Marl. The south road, past Pell's forty. Ada. She was four when the Throat wrote me and she's thirteen and I've had one letter, in nine years, that my wife's sister wrote for her because my wife's dead, and it said she'd learned her letters and it said she was writing to a number." He opened his eyes. "That's what she's got of me. A number the Throat wrote wrong, that a letter goes to twice a year and comes back. I'm not a man who says no to a thing because it's dangerous, four four seven one. I said yes to a thing on Marl that was dangerous and it's why I'm here. I'm a man who says no to a thing because I've counted it, and I've counted this, standing here, and I can't see the end of it, and I want to."

"Then ask."

"When it fails." Harrow said it flat. "Not if. I've packed nine harvests and I've seen a lid go wrong on the road's end with the Flamen's rod on it four times, and every time it was nothing, it was a groove, it was a slab that shifted, and every time it took a man's hands in the cask to right it. When it fails. When thirty-one's on the road's end and the rod goes on it and the lid doesn't take the mark because there's a man in it and the mark's not for men. What happens to the foreman who packed it?"

Jonah did not say anything.

"I'm in it," Harrow said. "That's what happens. I'm in it either way. If you go up and it's found on the dock I packed it. If it fails here I packed it. If Tullus puts the rod on me and asks how a cut one got seventy slabs out of a cask I lidded I don't have a thing to say, because there isn't one; the only man who could have done it is me." He lowered the lamp until the light was a line on the floor between them. "So I'm not asking to be out. There's no out. I'm telling you that I'm in, and that from tonight I pack thirty-one, not you, because you don't know how to pack a cask so it sings right with a man in it and I do, and that on the nineteenth at the dusk count when Decimus reads your number and there's nothing, it's not Pell says he saw you go, because Pell's Shallows and Decimus knows it. It's me. The Deep foreman. I say, four four seven one went in the night, I carried him, he was a week from it, anybody could see. And Decimus makes a mark."

"And the rod."

"If the rod comes for me, I've a thing to say to it." Harrow's voice did not change. "I say I carried a dying man to the Cradle and he went in, because that's what happens on my road, and if the Flamen wants to read the wall for him he can read it, and I'll stand there while he does, and I'll not know why he's not in it. I'll not know. Don't tell me the rest. Don't tell me where the eleven went or how a lid comes off a cask without a mark on the groove or what that was on your hands. I've a girl on Marl and a rod's coming for me sooner or later, and when it does I want to have less than a man who's afraid can say." He breathed out. "That's what I'm asking. Less."

Jonah looked at him for a long time.

"There's a thing you'll not know," he said, at last, "and there's a thing you'll do. You'll not know about the eleven. You'll not know about my hands. You'll know that a cut one asked you to pack a cask light and that you did it, and why, and that you said a thing at the dusk count that was true as far as you'd seen it." He put his hand flat on the rock. "And the thing you'll do is this. The name. Ada Harrow, Marl, the south road past Pell's forty. Say it again."

Harrow said it again.

"She's writing to a number." Jonah did not take his hand off the rock. "When the freighter's a month out and I'm off it, there'll be a man on Marl. Not me; I'll not be on Marl. A man. He'll go to the south road and he'll say to a girl of thirteen that her father's foreman of the Deep on Tessera, and that he's alive, and that the number's the wrong one, and he'll tell her the right one, and he'll say a thing her father said, so she knows it's from him and not from the Throat." He looked at the lamp. "You'll tell me the thing. Tonight. And then that's the whole of what you've got, and it's more than a number."

The foreman stood at the turn with the lamp at his knee.

"Nobody's gone up the Throat," he said.

"No."

"And if you're on the dock and it opens."

"Then there's a man on the dock who says her father's alive, and he's got a knife in him, and he says it anyway." Jonah kept his voice level. "That's the count, Harrow. I've done it every way. That's the only line on it that's yours."

Harrow looked at the cask.

"Tell her," he said, "that the north forty never made quota. She'll know. It's a thing her mother said." He shut the lamp. "Put those slabs in twenty-nine and thirty. Six deep, on top, under the lid. They'll take it. Rennick's written them light and the surface won't care. I'll knock them in the morning and if they sing wrong I'll say the Deep shifted, which it does." His voice came from the dark now, going away. "The seventeenth. After the last bell. I'll pack thirty-one."

---

He packed it on the seventeenth.

Jonah stood in the gallery without a lamp and watched a man who had packed nine harvests pack a cask with a hole in it, and it was a craft, the way the yoke had been a craft, and the fang. Harrow did not build a hollow. He built a wall. Seventy slabs, on end, in a ring, leaning in, each one against the next so that the ring held itself the way an arch holds itself, and in the middle of the ring a space the width of a man's shoulders and the depth of the cask, and on top of the ring, flat, a course of slabs that closed it and left a gap at one side the width of a man.

"You go in through there," Harrow said. "Feet first. You lie down. I put the last two over the gap and they sit on the ring and they don't sit on you, because the ring's bearing, not the middle. Then the lid." He knocked the cask, once, with his knuckle, low down, and it made a sound, and he knocked twenty-nine, and it made the same sound. "It sings right. It'll sing right on the road's end. What it does on the dock I don't know and you're not going to tell me."

"No."

"There's a thing I've packed for nine years that I never thought about." The foreman stood with his hand on the lid. "The Vein doesn't reach for the casks. It reaches for a cutting in a man's arm at twenty paces and it reaches for a rod and it reaches for the slabs in a bucket, I've felt it, but it's never once reached for a cask, and the casks are it. I asked Auger why, my first year. He said, because they're it. Like a hand doesn't reach for the arm it's on." He took his hand off the lid. "Seventy slabs in a ring with a man in the middle. It'll not reach for the ring. I'd not want to be the thing in the middle."

"I've been the thing in the middle seven years."

"Yes," Harrow said. "That's what I've been afraid of. Since the day Auger put you at the face." He picked up the lamp. "I'll not come to the Cradle tomorrow. That's not mine."

---

The eighteenth night was the count's, and then it was theirs.

Decimus read the Deep at dusk and the road answered and Ulli said his number from his bucket the way he had said it for nine years, and then the road went to its shelves, and the deep man got up from the bucket and walked into the Cradle and sat down against the wall with his back to it, and Jonah came in behind him with the blanket from the sleeping gallery over his arm, and Pell came in behind Jonah, and there were three.

The old man from Marl had never been in the Cradle. Jonah watched him look at it, the cut room with the warm dark wall, and watched him understand that it was a room and that it was warm and that the thing they cut every day was two feet from his face and singing, and the old man's mouth did what it did when the quota went to twelve, and then he sat down on Ulli's other side with his back to the Vein, and did not say anything.

Jonah sat on the near side. He put the blanket over Ulli's knees. The deep man did not look at it.

"That's mine to take back," Ulli said.

"I'll take it."

"You'll take it in the morning to the shelf it came from and you'll fold it and you'll say to Decimus that the one who carries went in the night, and Harrow'll say it too, because he's the foreman, and it'll be so." He said it to the roof. "Two l's."

"Two l's. The east fields."

"Then that's done." He was quiet a while. The wall breathed against Jonah's back, and he felt it through the rock the way he had felt everything since the Mouth, and it was not reaching. It was listening. It had been listening to the man beside him for nine years and it was listening now, and it was polite, and it was waiting.

"Pell," the deep man said.

"Here."

"North forty."

"That's right."

"Never made quota."

"Not once in forty years." The old man's voice was steady. "My mother sang to it every morning and it never once."

"Neither did the east fields," Ulli said. "They flood. That's why the song." And he began.

It was the fallow song. Jonah did not know it and Pell did, and the old man came in on the second line, low, in a voice that had cut a line for six years and had sung a field for forty before that, and the two of them sang it, and it was not a good song; it was the kind of song that is not for anyone, that a man sings to a thing that cannot hear so that he will have said it. The crop's in. The field's done. Lie now. We'll come back. Jonah had that much of it by the second verse, the way he'd had the road's tongue, from the shape.

He put his hand on the rock.

He did not sing; he had no song, he had names, and he said them, under the singing, in the old tongue, the way he said them every night, so that there would be something in the room that was his. Tom Vale. Mara Vale. The Kestrel. Anvil. And he asked, the way he had asked on the road: stand between.

It stood between.

He felt it go into the doorway of him and stop there, and turn, and put its back to the room, and he did not have a better word for it than that. It was not the plate. It was not the holding. It was a thing with a name standing in the door of a man with its face to him and its back to a wall that was, at that moment, beginning to finish a thing it had been too polite to finish for nine years, and he felt the beginning of it, through the rock, through his hand, the way he had felt the old man go: the reach, the taking hold, the count starting. And then he did not feel it. It was there. He knew it was there. It was on the other side of a thing with a name, and the thing let none of it through, and he sat on the floor of the Cradle with his hand on the rock and said the names and was in the room, and was there, and the count did not go through him.

Ulli stopped singing on the fourth verse.

He did not stop the way a man stops. His voice went down into the song and did not come back up, and Pell went on alone, and the deep man sat with his back to the wall and his eyes shut and his big hands in his lap on the blanket, and his face did nothing, and Jonah watched it do nothing for a long time and understood that it was not doing nothing anymore. It had never done nothing. It had been a man holding still for nine years so that a thing behind him would not finish, and the man had let go, and the face was what a face is when the man has gone out of it and left it there, at peace, for the ones sitting with him to look at.

Pell sang it to the end. It was long. It had verses for the water and the seed and the ones who had cut and the ones who had not made it to the cutting, and at the end it had one verse for the one that would lie fallow, and the old man sang that verse to the wall, not to Ulli, with his chin up, the way a man from Marl sings to a field so it knows he was there.

Then it was quiet.

"He's gone," Pell said.

"Yes."

"I felt it. Through the rock. I've never felt it before." The old man looked at his hands. "That's what you've had. Seven years."

"Yes."

"Then I'll not ask what you are again," Pell said. "I've had the answer." And he got up, and put his hand on the deep man's shoulder once, and went out.

Jonah sat.

He sat till the fourth bell with his back to the wall beside a man the wall had taken, and the wall took him slowly, the way Ulli had said it would, because it was polite and it had listened nine years and it went carefully now, the way a hand goes over a thing it has wanted a long time, and Jonah felt none of it, because a thing with a name stood in his doorway with its back to the room. He felt the other thing instead. The hand on the arm. It did that twice, in the dark, at the second bell and the third, and he said, both times, out loud, "I know," and it was true.

At the fourth bell he took the blanket.

---

Decimus read the Deep at dawn and got to the end of it and said, "Two two nine oh," and nobody answered.

The old guard looked up from the list.

He looked down the road, past the lamps, to the end, where a bucket stood on its own beside the mouth of the Cradle with nobody on it, and then he looked at Jonah, who was standing in the line with a folded blanket under his arm and his hands shaking the wrong way, and then he looked at Harrow.

"The one who carries went in the night," Harrow said. He said it in the road's tongue, flat, the way he said a bite. "He sat down in the Cradle after the dusk count and he went. Four four seven one sat with him. I looked in at the second bell. It was slow."

Decimus did not make the mark.

He stood with the stylus over the slate and looked at the blanket under Jonah's arm, and at Jonah's hands, and at Jonah's face, for the length of a count, and Jonah stood in the line and let him look, and did not do anything with his face, because there was nothing on it that was not true. He had sat with a man till the fourth bell. His hands shook. He had eleven slabs of the wall in him and a grade at the top of the band that a glass would find, and a man he had known nine years was against the wall in a room a hundred yards away with his face at peace, and there was nothing on the slate for any of it, and Decimus looked at all of it and saw a man who was dying, because that was what it was.

"Nine years," the old guard said.

"Yes."

"He carried forty-three."

"Forty-four," Jonah said. "He carried me."

Decimus made the mark. He made it slowly. He put the stylus to the slate and Jonah heard the scratch of it in the quiet of the road, and heard, under it, through the rock, the old guard's heart, which he had never once listened for in seven years, and it was not going fast. It was going the way it had gone on the Kestrel's deck the first night, slow, steady, the heart of a man who had made marks on this road for seven years and was making one more.

"Four four seven one."

"Here."

"You look like the wall, boy." Decimus said it without weight. "You look like the old man looked the week before. I've made marks on this road seven years and I know the week before when I see it." He did not look up. "Have you a thing to say. I'll write it."

Jonah stood with the blanket under his arm.

"Ullan," he said. "Of Marl. The east fields. Two l's. Write it with two."

Decimus wrote it with two. He looked at what he had written, and looked at the number the Throat had given a man nine years ago, which was not a name, and he wrote the name beside the number in his own hand, small, the way he had once written a boy's name beside a number on a deck, because the boy had asked him to write it right.

"That's the Deep," Harrow said, to the road, to nobody. "That's how it goes."

And the road went to the wall.

---

Harrow found him at the sixth bell, in the loading gallery, standing beside thirty-one with the lid off and the ring of slabs inside it dark and holding, and the deep foreman had no lamp and did not say anything for a moment, and then said:

"Decimus said a thing to me. After."

"What."

"He said, four four seven one's for the Cradle. Tonight or tomorrow. He said, when he goes, don't carry him, there's nobody to carry now, let him walk in himself, he's earned that." Harrow's voice did not change. "I said I would. He made a mark. I don't know what the mark was."

Jonah looked at the cask.

He thought of the old guard on the deck of the Kestrel with the list, and on the Throat with the list, and at the Chamber with the key, and at the line with a hand on a finger, and at the dusk count for seven years with his number, and he thought, he knows. He's known since the harvest came down as a number. He counted it the way I did and he came out at the same place, and he's made a mark I can't read, and I'm never going to know what it was.

"Feet first," Harrow said.

He went in feet first.

The ring took him. It was cold, the way the Vein was not cold anywhere else, and it did not reach for him, because he was the thing in the middle and the ring was it and it did not reach for its own arm. He lay down in the dark with seventy slabs standing round him and looked up at the crack of the gallery's dark above the gap, and Harrow's hands came into it, and the last two slabs went over, and sat on the ring, and did not sit on him.

"Tell her the north forty," Harrow said, through the slabs.

"Never made quota."

"That's it."

The lid came down. He heard it find the groove. He heard the foreman's knuckle on the side of the cask, once, low, and the sound it made, and he did not know if it was the right sound, and then he heard nothing, because the cask was the wall and the wall did not carry sound, and he was in the whole dark, in the middle of the Vein, with his hands flat against the slabs on either side of him.

He said the names.

Then he said, in the old tongue, to the thing in the doorway, the thing he had asked on the floor of the Cradle and had not said when, and said now:

"Now."

It closed on his heart.
