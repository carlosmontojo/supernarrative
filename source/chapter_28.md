# Chapter 28: Harrow

Thirty-one was in the third row.

A cask that said two hundred and forty on the slate had to weigh two hundred and forty on the dock. Jonah weighed something. That weight had to come out of thirty-one in slabs, and he did it on the sixteenth night, alone, without a lamp.

He had counted the loading gallery for six nights first. Harrow's men slept in the slab galleries with their gloves off and did not go past the turn after the last bell. The Flamen slept up the road in a cut with a lamp and a door, and did not come down before the fourth bell. Every night at the second bell Jonah had put his hand on the rock and counted eleven hearts in the slab galleries, slow, asleep, and none past the turn.

So he went past the turn.

The cask was lidded but not sealed. Its day was the nineteenth, with the last eight. He put both hands on the lid, found the groove, and lifted. It came up with no weight his hands could feel. He set it against the next cask and put his arm into the dark.

A hundred and twelve slabs, packed right. He took one out.

The slab was the wall. The plate under his skin knew it before he had it clear of the groove, and the hunger came up all at once. He let it. That was what he had come for.

He ate. Slowly. He put the plate on his palms and let it go into the slab a bite at a time. The slab went in through his hands and was gone. He felt it settle in the middle of him, and the settling did not fight. That was new. It had fought him for a month since the Mouth, thirty-one to thirty-two, a day at a time. It took the slab now and was quiet. There's the top of thirty-two, he thought. Anything past this is a grade I can't hide.

He ate eleven. He stopped because his hands had begun to shake the wrong way, the fever way, not the six years' way. And because a cask with forty gone from it would sing wrong when Harrow knocked it, and Harrow knocked every cask.

The rest he carried. Two at a time, up the gallery, to the cut in the far wall where the deep-diggers had stopped cutting when the knot came. He laid them flat against the rock in a row. Thirty-one slabs. He would have to move them again, and did not know where yet. He laid the last one down, stood up, and put his hand on the rock.

The gallery had twelve hearts in it.

He did not move. Eleven in the slab galleries, slow, asleep. One at the turn. Not asleep. Not slow. A man who has come round a corner and seen something and stopped. A man who has been afraid for nine years and has just found what he was afraid of.

"Harrow," he said, into the dark.

The lamp came on.

---

The foreman had it shuttered. He opened it a crack and held it low, at his knee, so it would not carry up the road. He did not come nearer. The light went across the gallery floor to thirty-one with its lid off. Then to the far cut and the row of slabs. Then to Jonah, standing over them with the plate still on his hands to the wrist.

"Put that away," Harrow said, very quietly. "Whatever it is. I don't want to see it."

Jonah put it away.

"I count the casks. Every night since he came. I lie down with the men and wait till they're under, then I come and knock the rows, because the Flamen's going to knock them too, on the day. If one sings wrong it's my packing and my neck. Nine harvests, and I've never once had one sing wrong." He looked at thirty-one. "Fifteen nights it's sung right. Tonight it sang hollow."

"Yes."

"I thought, that's a cask settling, and I went to bed. And I lay there." The lamp did not shake. The man was afraid through to the bone and the lamp did not shake. "I thought about a foreman who likes the walk, who carried the seventh cask and asked me how long a freighter takes. And a deputy who came down with a slate and wrote them light, and a Collar who carried it up. And I got up."

Jonah stood with his hands empty in the crack of light.

"How much did you take?"

"Forty-two."

"There's thirty-one there. Where's eleven?"

Jonah said nothing.

"Where's eleven, four four seven one."

"In me."

The lamp did not shake. Harrow's heart, through the rock, was going very fast and was not going away.

"You're going up in it," Harrow said.

"Yes."

"On the twentieth. Sealed. In the dark. As matter." He had it all at once, and Jonah let him have it. "Seventy slabs round you, packed light, two forty on the slate in the deputy's own hand. The Flamen puts his rod on the lid and it's shut. The surface weighs it, and it's two forty, and Rennick's written two forty. Nobody on this rock looks twice at a cask that weighs what the slate says." He breathed. "And you're not at the dusk count. And somebody says you went in the wall."

"Yes."

"Ulli."

"Ulli's going in himself. On the eighteenth."

Harrow closed his eyes.

He stood at the turn with the lamp at his knee and his eyes shut, and Jonah waited, because there was nothing on the slate for this. The hunger had eleven slabs in it. It looked at a man with a heart going fast at twenty paces and said, plainly, without words: that one.

It would be quick. Harrow was Tiro, unstriped, nine harvests, a girl somewhere he had not said yet. Jonah was thirty-two and settled and could cross the gallery before the lamp hit the floor. Twelve hearts in the Deep, then eleven. A foreman gone into the wall in a week when two other men did. That was the Deep. That was how it goes.

He stayed where he was. He put his hand on the rock, so Tacitus would have something to hold that was not the man at the turn, and said in the old tongue: no. Not that one. He carries the buckets with his gloves off. Not that one.

It settled. It did not like it. It settled.

"I've got a girl," Harrow said, with his eyes shut.

Jonah waited.

"On Marl. The south road, past Pell's forty. Ada. She was four when the Throat wrote me and she's thirteen. I've had one letter in nine years. My wife's sister wrote it, because my wife's dead. It said she'd learned her letters, and it said she was writing to a number." He opened his eyes. "That's what she's got of me. A number the Throat wrote wrong, that a letter goes to twice a year and comes back. I'm not a man who says no to a job because it's dangerous. I said yes to something dangerous on Marl and it's why I'm here. I say no because I've counted it. I've counted this, standing here, and I can't see the end of it, and I want to."

"Then ask."

"When it fails. Not if. I've seen a lid go wrong at the road's end with the rod on it four times. Every time it was a groove, a slab that shifted. Every time it took a man's hands in the cask to right it." Harrow's voice was flat. "When thirty-one's at the road's end and the rod goes on it, and the lid doesn't take the mark because there's a man in it. What happens to the foreman who packed it?"

Jonah said nothing.

"I'm in it. That's what happens. If you go up and it's found on the dock, I packed it. If it fails here, I packed it. If Tullus puts the rod on me and asks how a cut one got seventy slabs out of a cask I lidded, I've nothing to say. The only man who could have done it is me." He lowered the lamp until the light was a line on the floor between them. "So I'm not asking to be out. There's no out. I'm telling you I'm in. From tonight I pack thirty-one, not you. You don't know how to pack a cask so it sings right with a man in it. I do. And on the nineteenth, when Decimus reads your number and there's nothing, it's not Pell who says he saw you go, because Pell's Shallows and Decimus knows it. It's me. The Deep foreman. Four four seven one went in the night. I carried him. He was a week from it, anybody could see."

"And the rod."

"If the rod comes for me, I've an answer for it. I carried a dying man to the Cradle and he went in, because that's what happens on my road. If the Flamen wants to read the wall for him, he can. I'll stand there while he does, and I'll not know why you're not in it." Harrow's voice did not change. "I'll not know. Don't tell me the rest. Not where the eleven went, not how a lid comes off a cask without a mark on the groove, not what that was on your hands. I've a girl on Marl and a rod's coming for me sooner or later. When it does I want to have less than a man who's afraid can say." He breathed out. "That's what I'm asking. Less."

Jonah looked at him for a long time.

Jonah said, "There's what you'll not know, and there's what you'll do. You'll not know about the eleven. You'll not know about my hands. You'll know that a cut one asked you to pack a cask light and you did it, and why. You'll know you said something at the dusk count that was true as far as you'd seen it." He put his hand flat on the rock. "And this is what you'll do. The name. Ada Harrow, Marl, the south road past Pell's forty. Say it again."

Harrow said it again.

"She's writing to a number. When the freighter's a month out and I'm off it, there'll be a man on Marl. Not me. A man. He'll go to the south road and say to a girl of thirteen that her father's foreman of the Deep on Tessera. That he's alive. That the number's the wrong one. He'll tell her the right one. And he'll say something her father said, so she knows it's from him and not from the Throat." He looked at the lamp. "You'll tell me what. Tonight. Then that's the whole of what you've got, and it's more than a number."

The foreman stood at the turn with the lamp at his knee.

"Nobody's gone up the Throat."

"No."

"And if you're on the dock and it opens."

Jonah said, "Then there's a man on the dock who says her father's alive, and he's got a knife in him, and he says it anyway." He kept his voice level. "That's the count, Harrow. I've done it every way there is to do it, and it comes out the same. That's the only line on it that's yours to carry, and I'm sorry it's there at all."

Harrow looked at the cask.

"Tell her the north forty never made quota. She'll know. Her mother used to say it." He shut the lamp. "Put those slabs in twenty-nine and thirty. Six deep, on top, under the lid. They'll take it. Rennick's written them light and the surface won't care. I'll knock them in the morning. If they sing wrong I'll say the Deep shifted, which it does." His voice came from the dark now, going away. "The seventeenth. After the last bell. I'll pack thirty-one."

---

He packed it on the seventeenth.

Jonah stood in the gallery without a lamp and watched a man who had packed nine harvests pack a cask with a hole in it. Harrow did not build a hollow. He built a wall. Seventy slabs on end, in a ring, leaning in, each one against the next, so the ring bore its own weight, the way an arch does. In the middle was a space the width of a man's shoulders and the depth of the cask. On top, flat, a course of slabs closed the ring and left a gap at one side the width of a man.

"You go in through there," Harrow said. "Feet first. You lie down. I put the last two over the gap. They sit on the ring, not on you, because the ring's bearing, not the middle. Then the lid." He knocked the cask once with his knuckle, low down. He knocked twenty-nine. Same sound. "It sings right. It'll sing right at the road's end. What it does on the dock I don't know, and you're not going to tell me."

"No."

"Nine years I've packed these and never thought about it." Harrow put his hand on the lid. "The Vein doesn't reach for the casks. It reaches for a cutting in a man's arm at twenty paces. It reaches for a rod. It reaches for the slabs in a bucket; I've felt it. But it's never once reached for a cask, and the casks are it. I asked Auger why, my first year. He said, because they're it. Like a hand doesn't reach for the arm it's on." He took his hand away. "Seventy slabs in a ring with a man in the middle. It'll not reach for the ring. I'd not want to be the thing in the middle."

"I've been the thing in the middle seven years," Jonah said. "It's not new work. It's just the first time anybody's built me a room for it."

"Yes," Harrow said. "That's what I've been afraid of, since the day Auger put you at the face." He picked up the lamp. "I'll not come to the Cradle tomorrow. That's not mine."

---

Decimus read the Deep at dusk on the eighteenth and the road answered. Ulli said his number from his bucket. Then the road went to its shelves. The deep man got up, walked into the Cradle, and sat down with his back to the wall. Jonah came in behind him with the blanket from the sleeping gallery over his arm. Pell came in behind Jonah, and there were three.

The old man from Marl had never been in the Cradle. He looked at the cut room and the warm dark wall. He understood that it was a room, and warm. The Vein he cut every day was two feet from his face. His mouth did what it did when the quota went to twelve. Then he sat down on Ulli's other side and said nothing.

Jonah sat on the near side and put the blanket over Ulli's knees.

"That's mine to take back," Ulli said.

"I'll take it."

"In the morning. To the shelf it came from, and fold it. Then you say to Decimus that the one who carries went in the night. Harrow'll say it too, because he's the foreman, and it'll be so." He said it to the roof. "Two l's."

"Two l's. The east fields."

"Then that's done."

The wall breathed against Jonah's back. He felt it through the rock, and it was not reaching. It was listening. It had listened to the man beside him for nine years, and it was polite, and it was waiting.

"Pell," the deep man said.

"Here."

"North forty."

"That's right."

"Never made quota."

"Not once in forty years. My mother sang to it every morning and it never once."

"Neither did the east fields," Ulli said. "They flood. That's why the song." And he began.

It was the fallow song. Pell came in on the second line, low, in a voice that had cut a line for six years and sung a field for forty before that. It was not a good song. It was the kind a man sings to something that cannot hear, so that he will have said it. The crop's in. The field's done. Lie now. We'll come back. Jonah had that much of it by the second verse.

He put his hand on the rock.

He did not sing. He had no song; he had names. He said them under the singing, in the old tongue. Tom Vale. Mara Vale. The Kestrel. Anvil. And he asked: stand between.

It stood between.

He felt it go into the doorway of him and stop there. It turned, and put its back to the room. The wall was beginning to finish what it had been too polite to finish for nine years. He felt the start of it through the rock: the reach, the taking hold, the count starting. Then he did not feel it. It was there. It was on the other side of Tacitus, and Tacitus let none of it through. He sat with his hand on the rock and said the names. He was in the room, and the count did not go through him.

Ulli stopped singing on the fourth verse.

Not the way a man stops. His voice went down into the song and did not come back up. Pell went on alone. The deep man sat with his eyes shut and his big hands in his lap on the blanket, and his face did nothing. Then Jonah understood that it had never done nothing. It had been a man holding still for nine years so that what was behind him would not finish. The man had let go. The face was at peace, for the ones sitting with him to look at.

Pell sang it to the end. It was long. It had verses for the water and the seed, for the ones who had cut and the ones who had not made it to the cutting. The last verse was for the one that would lie fallow. The old man sang that verse to the wall, not to Ulli, with his chin up.

Then it was quiet.

"He's gone," Pell said.

"Yes."

"I felt it. Through the rock. I've never felt it before." The old man looked at his hands. "That's what you've had. Seven years."

"Yes."

"Then I'll not ask what you are again," Pell said. "I've had the answer." He got up, put his hand on the deep man's shoulder once, and went out.

Jonah sat.

He sat till the fourth bell beside a man the wall had taken. The wall took him slowly, because it was polite and it had listened nine years. Jonah felt none of it, because Tacitus stood with its back to the room. He felt the other thing instead. The hand with no hand. It pressed, once, in the middle of him, at the second bell, and again at the third. Both times he said, out loud, "I know," and it was true.

At the fourth bell he took the blanket.

---

Decimus read the Deep at dawn and got to the end of it. He said, "Two two nine oh," and nobody answered.

The old guard looked up from the list. He looked down the road, past the lamps, to the end, where a bucket stood on its own beside the mouth of the Cradle with nobody on it. Then at Jonah, standing in the line with a folded blanket under his arm and his hands shaking the wrong way. Then at Harrow.

"The one who carries went in the night," Harrow said, flat, in the road's tongue. "He sat down in the Cradle after the dusk count and he went. Four four seven one sat with him. I looked in at the second bell. It was slow."

Decimus did not make the mark.

He stood with the stylus over the slate and looked at the blanket, at Jonah's hands, at Jonah's face, for the length of a count. Jonah let him look. There was nothing on his face that was not true. He had sat with a man till the fourth bell. His hands shook. He had eleven slabs of the wall in him and a grade at the top of the band. Decimus looked at all of it and saw a man who was dying, because that was what it was.

"Nine years," the old guard said.

"Yes."

"He carried forty-three."

"Forty-four," Jonah said. "He carried me."

Decimus made the mark. He made it slowly. Jonah heard the scratch of the stylus in the quiet of the road, and under it, through the rock, the old guard's heart. He had never once listened for it in seven years. It was going slow and steady, the heart of a man who had made marks on this road for seven years and was making one more.

"Four four seven one."

"Here."

"You look like the wall, boy." Decimus said it without weight. "You look like the old man looked the week before. I've made marks on this road seven years and I know the week before when I see it." He did not look up. "Have you a thing to say. I'll write it."

Jonah said, "Ullan. Of Marl. The east fields. Two l's. Write it with two."

Decimus wrote it with two. He looked at what he had written, and at the number the Throat had given a man nine years ago. He wrote the name beside the number in his own hand, small. He had once written a boy's name beside a number on a deck, because the boy had asked him to write it right.

"That's the Deep," Harrow said, to the road, to nobody. "That's how it goes."

And the road went to the wall.

---

Harrow found him at the sixth bell in the loading gallery, beside thirty-one with the lid off and the ring of slabs dark inside it. The foreman had no lamp.

"Decimus said something to me. After."

"What."

"He said, four four seven one's for the Cradle. Tonight or tomorrow. He said, when he goes, don't carry him. There's nobody to carry now. Let him walk in himself. He's earned that." Harrow's voice did not change. "I said I would. He made a mark. I don't know what the mark was."

Jonah looked at the cask.

He knows, he thought. He's known since the harvest came down as a number. He counted it as I did and came out at the same place. He's made a mark I can't read, and I'm never going to know what it was.

"Feet first," Harrow said.

He went in feet first.

The ring took him. It was cold, as the Vein was not cold anywhere else. It did not reach for him. He was in the middle, and the ring was it, and it did not reach for its own arm. He lay down with seventy slabs standing round him and looked up at the crack of dark above the gap. Harrow's hands came into it. The last two slabs went over, and sat on the ring, and did not sit on him.

"Tell her the north forty," Harrow said, through the slabs.

"Never made quota."

"That's it."

The lid came down. He heard it find the groove. He heard the foreman's knuckle on the side of the cask, once, low. He did not know if it was the right sound. Then he heard nothing, because the cask was the wall and the wall did not carry sound. He was in the whole dark with his hands flat against the slabs on either side.

He said the names.

Then he said, in the old tongue, to Tacitus, what he had not said on the floor of the Cradle, and said now:

"Now."

It closed on his heart.
