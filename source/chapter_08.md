# Chapter 8: The Pit

The first thing they took was the light.

Not on purpose. Nobody on Metalla Seven did anything on purpose that they could do by habit. The ramp came down on a dock cut into grey rock under a roof of grey rock, lit by lamps set too far apart, and the thirty-eight of them came down it into a cold that got into the teeth, and the light on the dock was the last light any of them would see that had not been bought by the shard.

Jonah didn't know that yet. He knew that the smell was worse off the ramp than on it, and that the thing under his ribs had not stopped pointing, and that it was pointing down.

There was a line. There was always a line. Grey Blanks with rifles on either side of it and, at the end, a table, and at the table a Flamen in the grey robe with a glass, and behind the Flamen a door in the rock that was not a door but a mouth, round, black, taller than three men, with a cage hanging in it on a cable that went down out of sight, and a wind coming up out of it that smelled of the cut.

"Strip," said the Blank at the head of the line. "All of it. On the deck. Then the table."

They stripped. Thirty-eight men in a cold that got into the teeth, with their clothes in piles at their feet and their bound hands held out in front of them because nobody had said to lower them, and Jonah stood among them with the ring under the skin of his arm and thought, that's the first thing, and it's already done, and they didn't even look.

The Thinstripe from the ramp was two men ahead of him. Aulus. His dead stripe stood out on his bare arm like a thing painted on, and his hit face had gone the colour of the rock, and he was standing very straight.

"Blanks to the left," the Flamen said, without looking up. "Peregrines to the right. Anyone with a mark on him, left. That's you, citizen."

That was to Jonah. The Flamen had looked up after all, at the line on Jonah's arm, and Jonah went left with the eight Blanks and Aulus, and watched the thirty Collars go right toward the cage and the wind, and saw Pell look back at him once, and then not.

---

The table on the left was a different table.

It had straps. That was the first thing he saw. It was a slab of the same dark glass as the one in the Flamens' house on Anvil, but it was set flat like a bed, and there were straps at the head and foot and one for each arm, and beside it, on a stand, a thing that was not a tool.

He had seen Flamens' tools. He had seen the jaws that had cut his collar, and the dish that had held the cutting, and the stylus. This was none of those. It was a rod of dark glass the length of a forearm with a cup at one end like the bell of a horn, and it lay on its stand and did not look like it had been made. It looked like it had been cut off something.

"Number," said the Blank at the table.

"He's a citizen," Jonah said, because Aulus was on the table and had not been asked. They'd put him there first. Two Blanks had taken his arms and walked him to it and laid him down and buckled him, and he had let them, straight-backed, with his jaw locked, and now the Flamen was standing over him with the glass rod in one hand, and the Blank with the list was asking Jonah for a number.

"He's four four seven oh," the Blank said. "Was. You're four four seven one. Say it."

"Four four seven one."

"Again."

"Four four seven one."

"Good. That's you now. Watch. It's easier if you've watched."

The Flamen laid the cup of the rod on the inside of Aulus's forearm, on the dead stripe.

Jonah watched.

He had not known what unstripping was. He had heard the word, on Anvil, the way you heard words for things that happened to Stripes in the City. He'd known it was a sentence, and that it was bad, and that men didn't come back from it, and he had put it with the other things that were nothing to do with him.

It was this. The Flamen laid the cup on the stripe and said a word, and the word was not the Hush and not the Lock. It was a third thing, in the old Latin, and it went into Aulus's arm and Jonah felt it go, felt it the way he'd felt the wind up the shaft, and it went in looking for the lorica and it found it, and it called.

The lorica came.

It came out through the stripe. It came the way plate came, dark and fitted and alive, but it did not come out to cover the man. It came out into the cup of the rod, drawn, all of it, from wherever it lived in him, up through the skin of his arm and out into the glass, and it took a long time, and Aulus screamed the whole of it.

He had not screamed for the Hush. He had not screamed on the ramp. He screamed now, on his back on the slab with his jaw finally unlocked, and it was not a scream of pain, though there was pain in it. It was the scream of a man having something torn out of him that had been in him for twenty years and had been the only thing in him that had ever obeyed. Jonah understood that. He understood it with the thing under his ribs, which had gone very still, and was watching too, and was not hungry, and was not pointing, and was, for the first time since he'd known it, afraid.

The lorica came out of Aulus for a count of about sixty. Then the Flamen lifted the rod, and the cup was full of something dark that moved, and he set the rod on its stand and put his hands on the glass slab on either side of the man, and looked down.

"Void," he said. "Clean."

Aulus was not screaming anymore. He was not doing anything anymore. His eyes were open and looking at the roof of grey rock, and on the inside of his forearm where the stripe had been there was a line of white, like a burn, like a thing rubbed off with a thumb, and his chest was not moving.

The Flamen looked at him for a moment longer, and then shrugged, a small movement, the shrug of a man whose sum has come out the way sums usually came out.

"Didn't take it," he said. "They mostly don't, that grade. Next."

---

They took Jonah's arms.

He let them. He'd been letting them since Anvil and it was almost a habit now, and habit was the only thing that got him onto the slab and lying down and buckled, with the cold of the glass all down his back and Aulus's warmth still in it, and the roof of the rock above him with its lamps too far apart. The straps went on. The Flamen came round to his left side, then looked at the list, then came round to his right, where the line was.

"Citizen," the Flamen said, bored. "Standard cutting, fused at nine, fresh. Should be easy. They mostly live, at one."

He laid the cup of the rod on the line.

Jonah had been thinking about this since the ramp. Since before. Since the crewman on the transport had said, on the ramp you'll be read and you'll be cut, and he'd thought, cut, and understood it, and had lain against the wall of the hold for the last hour arguing with a thing that did not argue. There was a cutting in his arm. There had been, for a moment on Anvil, before the thing under his ribs took it. And the thing under his ribs had put a piece of itself in the cutting's place, wearing the cutting, so that a glass would see one, and it had lain there for a month, and now a Flamen was going to call it out.

Not the piece. All of it. The word did not know about pieces. It went in and it called the lorica, and there was one lorica in Jonah, and it was the thing under his ribs, and it was the size of a man.

He had thought about that, and had not found an end to the thought, and was out of time.

The Flamen said the word.

It went in. He felt it go in the way he'd felt it go into Aulus, but from the inside, a call, in the old Latin, come, come out, come to the cup, and it went down through his arm looking for the thing to call, and it found the piece in his forearm, the finger of the whole that was wearing a dead cutting's coat, and it took hold, and it pulled.

And the piece went.

It went the way the cutting had gone into the glass on Anvil. It went the way a tooth goes. Jonah felt it come loose from the thing under his ribs, felt the place where it had been joined, and it was not a clean place, it was a tearing, a piece of a whole being pulled off by a word that had no idea what it was pulling, and the thing under his ribs made a sound.

Not a sound. He had no word for it. It was what a sound would have been if it had been made inside a man's bones by something that had never made a sound before and did not know how. It went through him like the ramp coming down. And the piece went up his arm and out through the line into the cup, dark, moving, wearing the coat of a Blank's cutting and three small loud thoughts, hold and strike and obey, and it went the whole way, all of it, and Jonah lay on the slab and screamed.

He did not decide to. He had meant to. He had lain in the hold and thought, if it comes to it, scream, they'll expect it, and he had not needed the plan. The scream came up out of the place where the piece had been torn off, and it was real, and it went on for as long as the piece took to go, which was less than sixty, because the piece was small, but not much less, and when it stopped he was lying on his back with his chest heaving and his eyes on the rock and the thing under his ribs pressed so flat against the bottom of him that he could not feel it at all.

The Flamen lifted the rod. He looked into the cup. He set it on its stand and laid his hands on the slab and looked down into the glass.

Jonah did not breathe. He'd thought about that too. He lay with his chest still and his eyes open and his mouth open and thought, void, void, void, and the thing under his ribs was so far down it was not there, it had gone flat the way it had gone flat under the glass on Anvil but further, into some place under the place, and he thought, they mostly don't, at that grade, and held his breath, and counted.

"Void," the Flamen said. He sounded almost surprised. "Clean. Well." He looked at Jonah's face. "You're still here. Breathe, then."

Jonah breathed.

It came in ragged and he let it. On the inside of his right forearm the line was gone. He turned his head and looked, and where the mark had been, the drawing, there was a line of white now, a burn, a thing rubbed off, and he knew, with the part of him that knew things, that it was a drawing too, that the thing under his ribs had drawn it in the last second before it went flat, the way it had drawn the mark, carefully, the way a child draws a house.

He lay on the glass and looked at the scar it had made for him, and something in his chest that was not the thing under his ribs turned over, and it was not hunger and it was not fear.

It had given him a piece of itself. It had not been asked. It had felt the word come and had known, the way it knew what a glass was, what the word wanted, and it had let a piece of itself go into the cup so that the rest could stay, and it had hurt, and it had made a sound, and now it was lying at the bottom of him not moving, and he did not know if it was because it was hiding or because it was hurt or because it was, in whatever way a thing like that could be, grieving.

"Four four seven one," the Blank with the list said. "Up. Shackle."

---

The shackle was the collar's ugly brother.

He'd worn a collar for nineteen years and had thought he knew what a thing around your neck was. The shackle went on the ankle. It was dark and heavy and it did not look like glass, it looked like iron, and when the Blank closed it round his left ankle it went tight on its own, without a click, and something in it woke up and looked at him.

It was a lorica. He'd known it would be. Everything the Empire put on a man was. But the collar had been a stupid loyal thing that knew how to hold and how to tell a Stripe where you were, and this knew something else. He felt it wake up and look at his ankle and decide, the way Hask had decided about a boy's water, that it owned it.

"Don't run," the Blank said, without any weight. "It burns. Don't dig where you're not put. It burns. Don't touch a guard. It burns. You'll work it out." He looked at the list. "Decury six. That's the Shallows. Cage."

Jonah went to the cage.

There were seven of the nine Blanks alive to go with him. Two had died on the slab, Aulus and a boy who had not made a sound. The seven of them stood in the cage with their new white scars and their new dark shackles and their bare bodies, and then the Collars from the other line came, thirty of them, and were put in with them, and they were forty all told, and nobody said anything.

Pell was beside him again. The old man had a shackle on and no scar, because there had been nothing in him to cut, and he looked at the white line on Jonah's arm for a long moment.

"That's the mark, is it," he said, to the floor of the cage.

"That's the mark."

"They cut it out of you."

"Yes."

"And you lived."

"So far."

Pell looked at him a little longer, and then turned his farmer's eyes to the cage door, which was closing, and the wind, which had gone from a breath to a push, and said, quietly, "My brother had one. A line. Got it for the field, before the field went. They'll have cut it out of him at that table." He was silent a moment. "I'd like to think he lived. Long enough to hate them for it."

The cage dropped.

---

It dropped for a long time.

He had never been underground. Anvil was underground, all of it, tenements and square cut into the rock under a dome, but it was underground the way a house was under a roof. This was different. The cage went down the Throat on its cable and the walls of the shaft went past, close enough to touch, grey rock and then darker rock and then rock with something in it, veins, thin, black, like the lines on a map, and the lamps went past too far apart and the wind came up past them harder as they went, cold, and the smell in the wind got stronger, until it was not a smell anymore, it was a taste, in the back of the throat, metal and salt and something under both that he did not have a word for.

Cut. That was the closest. The taste of something cut that had not been allowed to close.

The thing under his ribs was moving.

It had come up off the bottom somewhere in the first kilometre. Slowly. The way it had come up after the glass on Anvil, a piece at a time, and he had felt it come and had felt the place where the piece had been torn off, raw, and had not known what to do with that, and had put his hand flat on his chest, which did nothing, and had left it there. And as the cage went down it kept coming, and it was not coming toward him. It was coming toward the cage door. Toward the shaft. Toward the wind.

It was pointing again. It had pointed at the planetoid from the transport the way a dog points down a road, and it was pointing now down the shaft the way a dog points at a door with its whole body, straining, and Jonah stood in the cage with his hand on his chest and forty bare men around him and thought, quietly, no.

It did not stop.

It had never not stopped before. He had said no to it in the Kestrel's hold with Varro on the deck and it had gone still and not stopped wanting, but it had gone still. Now he said no and it did not hear him. Or it heard him and there was something louder. It went to the cage door and pressed there, inside him, the way a dog presses at a door, and the pressing was not hunger and it was not interest and it was not the pointing, either, not anymore.

It was homesick.

He'd have laughed, if he'd had it in him. He'd have said, to Mara, in the dark, it was homesick, and she'd have said, what does that mean, and he'd have said, I don't know, it's a thing from a box, what does anything it does mean. But he didn't have the laugh. He stood in the cage and felt the thing under his ribs strain toward a shaft in a rock a month from Anvil the way he had wanted, on the transport's ramp, to turn around and walk back up it, and he understood something he did not want to understand, which was that whatever was in him had been here before, and had been taken from here, and knew it.

The cage stopped.

---

The Shallows were a road.

That was what they looked like. A tunnel cut through the rock, wide enough for six men, tall enough for one, going away from the cage in both directions under lamps too far apart, and off it, every twenty paces, other tunnels, narrower, going off into the dark, and out of every one of the narrower tunnels came the sound of steel on rock. Not much of it. Slow. The sound men make when they have been making it for a long time.

The Blank at the cage door was old. Jonah noticed that first. He had a lined face and a line on his arm, a Blank, a guard, in the garrison grey with a rifle and a pale grey plate on his forearms that he did not seem to have deployed on purpose and did not seem to notice, and he had a list, and he looked at the forty of them the way Morrow had looked at the wrapped shape on the dock. Not kind. Not unkind. The look of a man who has seen the thing before and knows what it costs.

"Decury six," he said. "Six, seven, eight, nine, ten. Those numbers, step out. The rest go on down."

Jonah stepped out. Pell stepped out. Eight others, four Blanks with scars and four Collars without.

"I'm Decimus," the old guard said. "You'll see me at count, dawn and dusk. I'll say your number and you'll say it back and that's the whole of what I want from you, and if I get it I'll leave you alone, and if I don't I won't, and it won't be personal." He looked along the line of them. "You've been told about the shackle. You've been told about the quota. You haven't been told about the Vein, because they don't tell you that at the Throat, they let you see it. So. Come and see it."

He walked. They followed.

It was not far. Down the road, past six of the narrower tunnels with the sound coming out of them, to a seventh, and into it, single file, with Decimus's lamp ahead and the dark behind, and the tunnel went down, and turned, and the sound of steel on rock got louder and then stopped, because the men making it had heard them coming and had stopped to look, and then the tunnel opened out.

It was a room. A chamber, cut, the size of the Flamens' house on Anvil, with a dozen men in it holding steel bars, chisels, picks, and they were standing at the far wall, and the far wall was not rock.

Jonah stopped walking.

It was dark. It was dark the way the glass slab had been dark, with a depth in it, and it went from the floor to the roof and from one side of the chamber to the other, and it was not flat. It had a surface, the way skin has a surface, and there were lines in it, thin, paler, like the lines in the shaft walls but closer together, and where the men had been working there was a place, the size of a table, that had been cut into, and the cut was wet, and the wet was moving.

The wall breathed.

He watched it. Everyone watched it, the new ones; the men with the chisels had stopped watching a long time ago. It rose. It settled. It did it slowly, the way the cloth in the box on the derelict had, and he heard Pell make a sound beside him, and did not turn, because the thing under his ribs had gone from pressing at a door to something else, all at once, and he had to put both hands flat on the rock wall of the tunnel to stay standing.

It lunged.

There was no other word. It went from him toward the wall the way a man goes off a ledge, with everything, and the only reason it did not go was that it was in him and he was standing in a tunnel with his hands on the rock, and so it hit the inside of him instead, all of it, and he made a noise, and his knees went, and he was on them, on the rock, with his hands still flat on the wall and his head down, and the thing under his ribs pressed against the front of him toward the breathing wall with a force that made the pointing in the cage feel like a thought.

Mother.

He didn't think the word. He would swear that for years. It was not his word and it was not in his language and it was not, he would say, a word at all, it was what came up through him from the thing that was pressing, and it was the first thing it had ever said to him, if it was a saying, and it was that.

"That one," someone said.

It was one of the men with the chisels. He'd come over. He was standing above Jonah, looking down, a Collar, thin, with the chamber's dark on his face, and he was looking at Jonah on his knees with an expression that was not surprise.

"Vein wants that one already," the man said. "Look at him. First day." He turned his head toward Decimus. "That's a fast one, guard. That's a month. Put him on the far side, he'll get the rest of them going."

"He's a Blank," Decimus said. "Was. They cut him at the Throat. Look at his arm."

The man looked. Something in his face shifted.

"Cut one. Well." He crouched, and looked at Jonah's face, close, and his breath smelled of the wall. "They go fast, cut ones. Faster than a month. It knows there's a hole in you, see. Where the thing was. It wants to fill it." He straightened up. "Sorry, friend. Nothing personal. Three years if you're smart, two if you're strong. Cut ones, I've never seen one make a year."

He went back to the wall and picked up his chisel and put it to the dark, wet, breathing place, and began, slowly, to cut.

Jonah knelt on the rock with his hands flat on the tunnel wall and the thing under his ribs pressed toward the Vein with everything it had and its one word going through him over and over, and did not get up for a long time.

When he did, Decimus was still there. He had waited. He was holding the lamp and looking at Jonah with that look, the one that had seen the thing before.

"Four four seven one," the old guard said.

"Four four seven one."

"Good. Dawn count. Don't be late for it." He paused. "And don't listen to Corr. He says a month to all of them. It's just what he says."

"How long does he say for himself?"

Decimus looked at him for a moment. Then he did a thing Jonah had not seen a Stripe or a Blank or a guard do since the ramp on Anvil, which was to almost smile.

"He's been here four years," he said. "He says a month every morning. Pick up a chisel, four four seven one. Quota's a shard a day and you're already behind."

Jonah picked up a chisel.

He walked to the wall. He stood in front of it, in front of the dark breathing depth of it, with the cut place wet at his feet and the thing under his ribs going through him like a tide, and he put the chisel to it, where Corr had put his, and did not push.

The wall was warm.

It was warm the way the cloth had been warm through his torn glove, and when the chisel touched it something on the other side of it, far down, far in, turned, the way a head turns, toward the warm thing that had touched it, and looked.

He held the chisel there, and it looked at him, and the thing under his ribs looked back, and Jonah stood between them with steel in his hands and a white line on his arm and thought, very clearly, oh, so that's what you are.

Then he pushed, because the quota was a shard a day and he was already behind, and the wall let him.
