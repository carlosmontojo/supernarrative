# Chapter 8: The Pit

The ramp came down on a dock cut into grey rock, under a roof of grey rock, lit by lamps set too far apart. The cold got into the teeth. The thirty-eight of them walked down into it.

The smell was worse off the ramp than on it. Under Jonah's ribs it had not stopped pointing, and it was pointing down.

There was a line. There was always a line. Grey Blanks with rifles on both sides of it and a table at the end. At the table, a Flamen in the grey robe with a glass. Behind the Flamen, a round black mouth in the rock, taller than three men, with a cage hanging in it on a cable that went down out of sight. Wind came up out of it and smelled of the cut.

"Strip," said the Blank at the head of the line. "All of it. On the deck. Then the table."

They stripped. Thirty-eight men with their clothes in piles at their feet and their strapped hands held out in front of them, because nobody had said to lower them. Jonah stood among them with the ring under the skin of his arm. That's the first one. It's already done, and they didn't even look.

Aulus was two men ahead of him. His dead stripe stood out on his bare arm. His hit face had gone the colour of the rock. He was standing very straight.

"Blanks to the left," the Flamen said, without looking up. "Peregrines to the right. Anyone with a mark on him, left." He looked up. "That's you, citizen."

Jonah went left with the eight Blanks and Aulus. The thirty Collars went right, toward the cage and the wind. Pell looked back at him once, and then didn't.

---

The table on the left had straps.

It was a slab of the same dark glass as the one in the Flamens' house on Anvil, laid flat. Straps at head and foot, one for each arm. On a stand beside it lay a rod of dark glass the length of a forearm, with a flared cup at one end. It did not look made. It looked cut off something.

Two Blanks had Aulus by the arms. They walked him to the slab, laid him down, and buckled him. He let them, jaw locked.

"Number," said the Blank with the list.

What he wanted to say filled a paragraph about licences and a court that had never sat. What came out was short, aimed past the guard at the whole line.

"He's a citizen," he said.

"He's four four seven oh. Was. You're four four seven one. Say it."

"He hasn't been asked anything. Not his name. Not why," Jonah said.

"He's been asked everything he's going to be. Say it."

"Four four seven one."

"Again."

"Four four seven one."

"Good. That's you now. Watch. It's easier if you've watched."

The Flamen laid the cup of the rod on the inside of Aulus's forearm, on the dead stripe.

Jonah watched.

He had heard the word unstripping on Anvil. He'd known it was a sentence, and bad, and that men didn't come back from it. He had not known it was this.

The Flamen said a word. Not the Hush. Not the Lock. A third word in the old Latin. It went into Aulus's arm and Jonah felt it go. It went in looking for the lorica, and found it, and called.

The lorica came.

It came out through the stripe, dark and fitted and alive, but not to cover the man. It came out into the cup of the rod, all of it, drawn up through the skin of his arm and out into the glass. It took a long time. Aulus screamed the whole of it.

He had not screamed for the Hush. He had not screamed on the ramp. He screamed now, on his back, with his jaw finally unlocked. Twenty years that had been in him, the only part of him that had ever obeyed, torn out through his arm. Jonah understood that. So did the thing under his ribs. It had gone very still. Not hungry. Not pointing. For the first time since he'd known it, afraid.

Sixty counts. Then the Flamen lifted the rod. The cup was full of something dark that moved. He set it on its stand, put his hands on the glass either side of the man, and looked down.

"Void. Clean."

Aulus was not screaming anymore. He was not doing anything anymore. His eyes were open on the roof. On the inside of his forearm, where the stripe had been, was a line of white, a burn, rubbed off with a thumb. His chest did not move.

The Flamen looked at him a moment longer and shrugged.

"Didn't take it. They mostly don't, that grade. Next."

---

They took Jonah's arms. He let them. He'd been letting them since Anvil.

The glass was cold all down his back, with Aulus's warmth still in it. The straps went on. The Flamen came round to his left, checked the list, and came round to his right, where the line was.

"Citizen," the Flamen said, bored. "Standard cutting, fused at nine, fresh. Should be easy. They mostly live, at one."

He laid the cup of the rod on the line.

Jonah had been thinking about this since the crewman said, you'll be cut. There had been a cutting in his arm, for a moment on Anvil, before the thing under his ribs ate it and put a piece of itself in its place. The piece wore the cutting, so a glass would see one. Now a Flamen was going to call it out. Not the piece. The word did not know about pieces. It called the lorica. There was one lorica in Jonah, and it was the size of a man.

He had not found the end of that thought. He was out of time.

The Flamen said the word.

He felt it from the inside. Come, come out, come to the cup. It went down his arm looking for something to call. It found the piece in his forearm, the finger of the whole wearing a dead cutting's coat. It took hold and pulled.

And the piece went.

It tore loose. Jonah felt it come away from the rest, felt the joint go. Not a clean joint. A piece of a whole, pulled off by a word that had no idea what it was pulling.

Under his ribs, it made a sound.

Not a sound. He had no word for it. It was made inside his bones by something that had never made a sound before and did not know how. It went through him. And the piece went up his arm and out through the line into the cup, dark, moving, wearing a Blank's cutting: hold and strike and obey. All of it went. Jonah lay on the slab and screamed.

He'd planned to. Lying in the hold he'd thought, if it comes to it, scream, they'll expect it. He didn't need the plan. The scream came up out of the place where the piece had been torn off, and it was real. It lasted as long as the piece took to go, a little under sixty. When it stopped he was on his back with his chest heaving and his eyes on the rock, and under his ribs there was nothing he could feel at all.

The Flamen lifted the rod. Looked in the cup. Set it on its stand, laid his hands on the slab, and looked down into the glass.

Jonah did not breathe. Chest still, eyes open, mouth open. Void, void, void. It had gone flat under the glass on Anvil. This was further, some place under that place. They mostly don't, at that grade. He held his breath and counted.

"Void," the Flamen said. He sounded almost surprised. "Clean. Well." He looked at Jonah's face. "You're still here. Breathe, then."

Jonah breathed.

He turned his head. On the inside of his right forearm the line was gone. Where the drawing had been there was a line of white, a burn, rubbed off. He knew, with the part of him that knew things now, that it was a drawing too. It had drawn it in the last second before it went flat, carefully, as it had drawn the mark.

He lay on the glass and looked at the scar it had made for him. Something in his chest, his own, turned over. Not hunger. Not fear.

It had given up a piece of itself. Nobody asked it to. It had felt the word come and known what the word wanted, and let a piece go into the cup so the rest could stay. It had hurt. It had made a sound. Now it lay at the bottom of him, not moving. Hiding, or hurt, or grieving, if it could grieve.

"Four four seven one," the Blank with the list said. "Up. Shackle."

---

The shackle was the collar's ugly brother.

Dark, heavy, iron to look at. The Blank closed it round his left ankle and it went tight on its own, without a click. Something in it woke up and looked at him.

A lorica. Everything the Empire put on a man was. The collar had been stupid and loyal and knew how to hold and how to tell a Stripe where you were. This knew something else. It looked at his ankle and decided, as Hask had decided about a boy's water, that it owned it.

"Don't run," the Blank said. "It burns. Don't dig where you're not put. It burns. Don't touch a guard. It burns. You'll work it out." He checked the list. "Decury six. That's the Shallows. Cage."

"What's the Shallows?"

"Top road. Nearest the cage. You'll like it. Everybody likes it for a month." He looked past Jonah. "Next."

"A month," Jonah said. "That's specific, for a stranger."

"Everybody's a stranger the first day."

"Not for long, I hear."

"No," the Blank said. "Next."

Seven of the nine Blanks were alive to go in the cage. Two had died on the slab, Aulus and a boy who had not made a sound. The seven stood with their new white scars and new dark shackles and bare bodies. Then the thirty Collars were put in with them, and nobody said anything.

Pell was beside him again. Shackle on, no scar, because there had been nothing in him to cut. He looked at the white line on Jonah's arm for a long moment.

"That's the mark, is it."

"That's the mark."

"They cut it out of you."

"Yes."

"And you lived."

"So far."

"The Stripe didn't."

"No."

What he wanted to say was that the Stripe's face, at the end, had finally believed something true, and that it was the worst thing Jonah had seen since the box. What he said was smaller, because Pell's brother was already in the ground somewhere and did not need the company.

"His name was Aulus," Jonah said. "I don't know the rest of it."

Pell nodded slowly, like that mattered, because to him it did.

Pell looked at him a little longer. Then he turned to the cage door, which was closing, and the wind, which had gone from a breath to a push.

"My brother had one. A line. Got it for the field, before the field went. They'll have cut it out of him at that table." He was silent a moment. "I'd like to think he lived. Long enough to hate them for it."

"I hope he did," Jonah said.

Pell didn't answer that. Some hopes don't want an answer, only a witness.

The cage dropped.

---

It dropped for a long time.

He had never been underground. Anvil was cut into rock under a dome, but that was a roof over a town. This was different. The walls of the shaft went past close enough to touch. Grey rock, then darker rock, then rock with veins in it, thin and black. The lamps went past too far apart. The wind came up harder as they went, and the smell in it got stronger until it was not a smell anymore but a taste at the back of the throat. Metal, salt, and under both of them something cut that had not been allowed to close.

Under his ribs, it was moving.

It had come up off the bottom in the first kilometre, slowly, a piece at a time. He felt the raw place where the piece had been torn off. He put his hand flat on his chest, which did nothing, and left it there. It kept coming, and it was not coming toward him. It was coming toward the cage door. Toward the shaft. Toward the wind.

Pointing again. On the transport it had pointed down a road. Now it pointed at a door with its whole body, straining. Jonah stood in the cage with his hand on his chest and forty bare men around him and thought, no.

It did not stop.

It had always stopped before. He had said no in the Kestrel's hold with Varro on the deck, and it had gone still. Now he said no and it did not hear him, or heard him and heard something louder. It pressed at the cage door inside him. The pressing was not hunger, and not interest, and not the pointing anymore.

It was homesick.

He'd have laughed if he'd had it in him. Whatever was in him had been here before, and had been taken from here, and knew it.

"You're breathing hard," Pell said.

"It's the air."

"It's not the air."

The cage stopped.

---

The Shallows were a road.

A tunnel wide enough for six men and tall enough for one, going away from the cage in both directions under lamps too far apart. Every twenty paces, a narrower tunnel went off it into the dark, and out of every one came the sound of steel on rock. Slow. The sound men make when they have made it for a long time.

The Blank at the cage door was old. Lined face, a line on his arm, garrison grey, a rifle, a list. A pale grey plate lay on his forearms that he did not seem to notice. He looked at the forty of them without kindness or unkindness. A man who had seen it before and knew what it cost.

"Decury six," he said. "Six, seven, eight, nine, ten. Those numbers, step out. The rest go on down."

Jonah stepped out. Pell stepped out. Eight others, four Blanks with scars and four Collars without.

"I'm Decimus. You'll see me at count, dawn and dusk. I'll say your number and you'll say it back. That's the whole of what I want from you. If I get it I'll leave you alone. If I don't, I won't, and it won't be personal." He looked along the line. "You've been told about the shackle. You've been told about the quota. You haven't been told about the Vein, because they don't tell you at the Throat. They let you see it. So. Come and see it."

He walked. They followed.

Down the road, past six of the narrow tunnels, into the seventh. Single file, Decimus's lamp ahead, dark behind. The tunnel went down and turned. The sound of steel on rock got louder and then stopped, because the men making it had heard them coming.

The tunnel opened into a chamber the size of the Flamens' house on Anvil. A dozen men stood at the far wall with chisels and picks. The far wall was not rock.

Jonah stopped walking.

It was dark. Dark with depth in it, the dark of the glass slab. It went from floor to roof and from one side of the chamber to the other, and it was not flat. It had a surface. Skin has a surface. Thin pale lines ran through it, closer together than the lines in the shaft. Where the men had been working there was a place the size of a table that had been cut into. The cut was wet, and the wet was moving.

The wall breathed.

It rose. It settled. Slowly, as the cloth in the box on the derelict had. Pell made a sound beside him. Jonah did not turn. He had to put both hands flat on the tunnel wall to stay standing, because under his ribs the pressing had become something else, all at once.

It lunged.

It went for the wall with everything it had. The only reason it did not go was that it was in him, and he was standing in a tunnel with his hands on the rock. So it hit the inside of him instead. He made a noise. His knees went. He was on the rock on his knees with his hands still flat on the wall and his head down, and it pressed toward the breathing wall with a force that made the cage feel small.

Mother.

He didn't think the word. It was not his word and not in his language. It was not, he would say, a word at all. It came up through him from whatever was pressing. The first thing it had ever said to him, if it was a saying, and it was that.

"That one," someone said.

One of the men with chisels had come over. A Collar, thin, with the chamber's dark on his face. He looked down at Jonah on his knees without surprise.

"Vein wants that one already. Look at him. First day." He turned to Decimus. "That's a fast one, guard. That's a month. Put him on the far side, he'll get the rest of them going."

"He's a Blank," Decimus said. "Was. They cut him at the Throat. Look at his arm."

The man looked. Something in his face shifted.

"Cut one. Well." He crouched, close. His breath smelled of the wall. "They go fast, cut ones. Faster than a month. It knows there's a hole in you, see. Where the cutting was. It wants to fill it." He straightened. "Sorry, friend. Nothing personal. Three years if you're smart, two if you're strong. Cut ones, I've never seen one make a year."

"What's your name?" he asked.

The man blinked. Nobody had asked him that in longer than he could say. "Corr."

"Corr. Thank you for telling me straight," Jonah said.

Corr shrugged, still not looking away from the wall. "Don't thank me. I didn't do you a favour."

"You told me true. That's enough of one down here."

He went back to the wall, picked up his chisel, put it to the wet breathing place, and began, slowly, to cut.

Jonah knelt with his hands flat on the tunnel wall. Its one word went through him over and over. He did not get up for a long time.

When he did, Decimus was still there. He had waited. He stood holding the lamp with the look that had seen it before.

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"Good. Dawn count. Don't be late for it." He paused. "And don't listen to Corr. He says a month to all of them. It's just what he says."

"How long does he say for himself?" Jonah said.

Decimus looked at him. Then he did something Jonah had not seen a Stripe or a Blank or a guard do since the ramp on Anvil. He almost smiled.

"He's been here four years. He says a month every morning. Pick up a chisel, four four seven one. Quota's a shard a day and you're already behind."

Jonah picked up a chisel.

He walked to the wall and stood in front of it, the cut place wet at his feet, and under his ribs it went through him. He put the chisel to the wall, where Corr had put his, and did not push.

The wall was warm.

Warm as the cloth had been through his torn glove. When the chisel touched it, something on the other side, far down and far in, turned toward the warmth that had touched it, and looked.

He held the chisel there. It looked at him. Under his ribs, it looked back. Jonah stood between them with steel in his hands and a white line on his arm and thought, very clearly, oh, so that's what you are.

Then he pushed, because the quota was a shard a day and he was already behind. The wall let him.
