# Chapter 24: Tacitus

He said it in the Chamber, because it was the only room in the Pit that was quiet, and because the old man had drawn it.

It took him a week to get there. That was the settling. He'd known it would be long; he'd had five sizes at the face and it had put plate on his arms for nine days, and this was not five sizes, this was a piece of the first cut given by the wall's own hand, and the coat had gone over like a house of doors, and the body inside the coat had to grow into every one of them while it was on. He had the fever for six days. Not from the middle out; everywhere, evenly, all the time, the way the boredom had been on the rock at the dinner that was not there. He cut his shard. He said his number. He stood at the wall of the seventh chamber with the chisel and the Vein opening for him and sweated through his shirt, and the road looked at him the way it had looked at him for six years, and thought what it thought.

"You're going," Pell said, to the wall. He'd said it so many times it had stopped being a thing he said and become a thing he did, like the songs. "Sixth year. You've been going six years. I've never seen a man take six years to go."

"I'm not going."

"I know. I'm saying it so it's said." The old man from Marl cut his line. "You went down. Ulli said. Past the last lamp. He said the wall breathed out."

"It did."

"And you came back up with something."

Jonah looked at his hands on the chisel.

"A name," he said.

Pell did not ask whose. He had stopped asking things in the fourth year; he took what was said and put it in whatever place he kept things, which was, Jonah had come to think, a great deal like his own, and cut.

And the plate was wrong.

Not wrong the way it had been wrong on the derelict, everywhere and no shape. Wrong the way a coat is wrong that has been made for a bigger man. He'd learned the plate over six years, where it came out when asked and how fast and how it sat on the back of a hand or the whole of a forearm or in the shape of a Master's seal on the palm, and none of that was true anymore. He asked for the back of the hand and got the whole arm. He asked for the seal and got the seal and a hook. On the third night of the fever, lying on the shelf, he asked for stillness and the plate came up over his face in the dark the way it had in the tenements six years ago, and he lay under it with the taste of it in his mouth and Pell breathing above him and thought, I've lost it. I had it and it's gone and I've got a week to get it back before a Flamen comes down that Throat and asks for my arm.

He had not lost it. The old man had said. It settles faster, because it thinks, but into something slightly different every time, and after a big jump you will wake up and have to learn your own plate again.

So he learned it.

He did it the way he'd done everything, on a rock, in the dark, thirty times, and the thing under his ribs, which had been given a piece of the first cut and had not asked for it either, did it with him, interested, the flat total interest that he had stopped, since the Mouth, thinking of as flat. It was not flat. It had never been. It was the attention of a thing that had been alone in a box for two hundred years and had found something to look at, and had been looking, every hour, for six years, and had wanted, the whole time, one thing, and had not said, because it was polite.

By the sixth night he had the back of the hand. By the seventh he had the seal. On the eighth night he opened the Chamber door from the road with a fang from his palm that was thin as a pick and exactly as thin as he'd asked, and went in, and locked it behind him, and sat down on the floor in the dark in front of sixteen slabs he could not see and did not need to.

---

"I told you I'd find one," he said.

He said it in the old tongue. He said it to the doorway of himself, where the thing stood, the way it had stood since the road when he said sorry, still, listening differently. The Chamber was the whole dark; Tullus's men had not been down with a lamp since the month's end, and the month's end was tomorrow, and the shelf was empty with a stick across it and the slabs leaned on the rock and the room was as quiet as the old man had drawn it.

"I had to be somewhere you could hear it. He said that. He said, somewhere you can hear the answer, and I thought he meant me hearing yours, and he did, and he also meant this. A name's a thing you say once and then it's said. I wanted it said where it would stay." He put his hand flat on the floor, on the rock the old man had drawn. "This room's got six hundred and forty-two names in it and one at the top that I couldn't say for six weeks. It's the only room in this Pit that knows what a name is for. So."

The thing in the doorway listened.

"Ulli said, call it what it is. He said you're the quietest thing he's ever known. Seven years he's listened, to me and the rock and the wall, and you've never once said anything. Not a tap. Not a song. You went into me on a deck and read me and ate my collar and put a blade through a Stripe's throat and you've never said a word. You gave a piece of yourself to a rod and you made a sound, once, inside my bones, and that's the only sound you've ever made, and it wasn't a word either." He breathed. "I learned a word for that. On the sixtieth night, in the noise, before I knew what any of it meant. He said it and I heard it the way you hear your own name in a crowd, and I never had a use for it. I've got one now."

He said it.

One word. In the old tongue, with the ending right, the way the old man would have wanted it said, in a room the old man had drawn, to a thing the old man had hidden in a box and told him, on a shelf, with his heart catching on the second, was someone.

"Tacitus."

The Chamber did not do anything. The dark was the dark. The slabs leaned on the rock. Somewhere far up the Deep road the cage came down and he felt it through the floor, and somewhere further Ulli was singing, and the wall breathed, the way it did, warm through the rock at his back, saying come to every cutting in the Empire and hearing nothing.

And the thing in the doorway heard its name.

He felt it hear. He would never be able to say what that was like, and he would try, once, for the woman with the broad stripe, and would get as far as this: it was like saying a man's name across a road and watching him turn. Except that the man had never once in his life been called anything, and had not known that he could be, and had wanted it for six years the way the wall had wanted a shape for four hundred, without knowing what wanting was. It turned. Not in the doorway; it had turned in the doorway before, on the road, and looked. It turned all the way. It came out of the doorway and into the room of him, all of it, the way it had come in on the derelict, reading, and it went through him the way it had gone through him then, the arm, the shoulder, the old break in the wrist, the heart, and it stopped at the heart the way it always had, interested, and it stayed.

Not passing through. Staying. The way a man stays in a room he has just been told is his.

"Tacitus," Jonah said again, quietly, to be sure.

And the thing that had been the thing under his ribs for six years, and was now something that had a name, did what the old man had said it would do if it was treated as someone. It answered.

Not in words. It had none. But he felt it, in the place where it had stopped, and it was not hunger and not interest and not the want from the Mouth. It was the thing a man does when his name is said and he has been waiting to hear it and had not known that he was waiting. It settled. Into the coat, into the body, into the middle of him where the seam had been and was a scar now, into every part of him at once, and the plate under his skin, which had been a size too big for a week, stopped being a size too big. It fitted. He felt it fit. He sat on the floor of the Chamber in the dark and felt six years of a thing he had carried become, in the length of a breath, a thing he was with, and he put his hand on his chest, and it was there, and it was Tacitus, and it was staying.

"All right," he said. "All right. That's said."

He got up. He'd been sitting. It did not matter.

"Tacitus," he said, one more time, at the door, with his palm on the steel and the thin fang finding the pins. "You go at the top. Above him. He'd have wanted that; he put himself first so the rest would know he hadn't exempted himself, and you were in the box before he was in this room. Tacitus. Aulus Terentius Pharus. Then the rest." He turned the bar. "That's the count. It starts with you now."

The lock said the thing it said. He went out onto the road.

---

He said it there too, that night, with nothing in his hands, in the dark between two lamps. All of them. Six hundred and forty-two and one at the top, three words in the old tongue, and above the three words, one, with the ending right, and the thing that had a name heard it said on the road where the old man had wanted to be said, and expected it, and would expect it every night after, and did not wander, and would not, he thought, ever wander again, because it had somewhere to be.

Decimus was at the cage.

He was on the stool with the rifle across his knees, which was not his post, and had not been his post for six years, and he had been on it, Jonah had noticed, more nights than not since the winter, and Jonah had put that with the rest and not looked at it, because it was Decimus, and because looking at Decimus was the heaviest thing in the place where he kept things.

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"You've a fever."

"I've had it a week. It's going."

"It's going. You've said that six years." The old guard looked at him in the lamp. He looked for a long time, the way he had looked at the garrison chisel in Pell's hand, and at a cut that had closed too clean under a bandage, and at a man who had crossed a riot to put him under a shelf, and had said nothing about any of them, because it was not personal, either way. "Sit."

Jonah sat. On the rock, against the cage frame, the way he had four years ago with a letter.

"I signed," Decimus said.

He said it to the lamp.

"Another five. In the autumn, when the first five was up. I was at the surface with the paper in my hand that said I'd served, and the house on Campania if I wanted it, and the transport for Etruria was on the dock, and I signed." The lined face did nothing. "The aunt's dead. Two years. The school kept Sula on the fees I sent and the fees went up with the war, everything goes up with a war, and a guard's pay on Etruria is a third of what it is here, and there's a tree in a garden that she's got the care of, and I'm not going to be the reason it dies." He shifted the rifle. "So I signed. Five more. She'll be sixteen. She counted the winters and I've given her five more to count and I haven't written to tell her, because I don't know how to write that a man who said two winters signed for five. It's not personal. I've said that so many times I don't know what it means anymore. It's the only thing I've got that isn't."

Jonah sat with his back to the cage.

"You could have told me," he said.

"I'm telling you."

"Before. In the autumn."

"You'd have counted it." Decimus almost smiled. It was the thing from the tunnel; Jonah had seen it perhaps six times in six years and had kept every one. "You count everything. I've watched you. You'd have counted a guard with five more years on a rock and put it wherever you put things and it would have weighed, and I didn't want it to weigh on you, because you've enough, four four seven one, you've a road and a decury and whatever it is you carry that's not the wall, and I thought, he'll find out at the count, when I'm still here, and he'll say nothing, because he's learned that from me." The old guard looked down the Deep road. "And you did. You've said nothing about it since the autumn. I've watched you not say it. So I'm saying it, so it's said."

"Sula."

"Sixteen. Counting."

"When the five are up."

"Twenty-one." Decimus was quiet a moment. "She'll have stopped counting by then. That's what I lie awake on. Not the wall, not Sabinus, not the count. That she'll be twenty-one and have stopped, because a girl who counts five winters and then five more learns that the counting doesn't bring anybody home, and she'll be right." He put his hand on the rifle. "So. That's mine. I've told it. You can put it with the rest."

Jonah put it with the rest.

It weighed. The old guard had been right about that, and had been right that Jonah would say nothing, and was right, now, to say it so it was said, and Jonah sat against the cage frame and felt the weight go in with the other things and did not say anything, because there was nothing in six years of the old tongue and the road's tongue and the four voices of a dinner that was not there that would have been the right thing, and Decimus had taught him that, and it was the only thing Decimus had taught him that he wished he had not learned.

"There's a thing," Decimus said.

"What?"

"Tomorrow. The month's end. Tullus's men go down the road with the lamp and the key, the way they do." The old guard did not look at him. "They've been going down that road once a month for twenty-three years and coming back without the lamp and with a slate, and I've never asked what's behind that door, because it's not my count. And in the last month there's been no slate. Rennick says. The prefect's been up in the garrison hall every night with the old ones, doing the galleries by hand, and he can't, nobody can, it was always done for him, and the Deep's giving what it gives and the Shallows is ten and he doesn't know why, and he's afraid." Decimus turned his head. "And tomorrow they go down with the lamp, and I think, four four seven one, that whatever's behind that door is not going to be behind it. And I think you know that. And I think that when they come back up that road without a slate and with a face on them, the prefect is going to do the thing a frightened man does, which is count."

"Count what?"

"Everything. Everyone. With a glass." The old guard picked up his rifle. "He'll send for the College. Not the every-second-year College, the ones that come with a slab and four robes. He'll send for the ones that come when a thing's gone wrong, with scholars, to read the galleries and read whatever's in that room and read every man on this road to find out which of them knows what happened to a thing that was supposed to be locked in. I've seen it once. Twenty years ago on Etruria, when a Thinstripe's house lorica went missing from a shrine. They read the whole town." He stood up. His knees made the sound. "So whatever it is you do with your breath under the glass, and I don't know what it is, and I don't want to, I'd start doing it now. That's all. It's not personal."

"Decimus."

"What."

"Thank you."

The old guard looked at him from the stool with the rifle on his shoulder and the lamp on his lined face and the letter, Jonah knew, in the coat against his chest, four years old now, with two marks at the edge of the page that were six.

"Don't," he said. "It's a count. I'm telling you what's coming down the Throat because you're on my road and I'd like my road to be alive when it comes. That's the whole of what a guard has. I've said." He went down the road toward the Shallows, saying numbers, and Jonah sat against the cage and watched him go, and put his hand flat on his own chest, and the thing that had a name was there, and had heard all of it, and was, he thought, for the first time in six years, not listening to the wall at all.

It was listening to him.

---

They went down the road at the fourth bell with the lamp and the key, two of Tullus's men in the garrison grey, and Jonah stood at the wall of the seventh chamber with a chisel and felt them through the rock, through the plate, all the way down, past the Cradle, to the door.

He felt the key. He felt the door open, on hinges that had been oiled once a month for twenty-three years, without a sound. He felt the two men stand in the doorway of a room the size of two rooms with a lamp in one hand and a slate for the numbers in the other, and he felt the thing they did next, which was nothing, for the length of a hundred breaths.

Then he felt them run.

He cut his shard. It came off for him. He dropped it in the bucket and stood with his arms shaking, because a man the Vein was taking shook, and Pell beside him did not look up, and the road did not know anything yet, and would, by the dusk count, because everything came down the Throat as a number and this was going to come down as one.

It came down as three.

"Three days," Decimus read, at the dusk count, off the list, in the voice for things that were not personal. He read it twice. "Three days. Every man on the road to the Throat, by decury, for the glass. The College is coming down with scholars. The galleries are to be read and the." He stopped. He looked at the list. "And the Chamber. The Chamber is to be read. And every man on this road is to be read at the Throat, on the big slab, by a scholar, and any man who does not go up to be read is to be counted as having gone into the wall, and struck." He made a mark. "Prefect's order. Three days."

The road did not make the sound.

Jonah stood in it, at the end of decury six, with the fever gone and the plate fitted and a thing in him that had a name and was listening to him and not the wall, and thought: three days. And thought: a scholar. Not a robe with a wand; not four robes and a slab and six hours in a doorway. A scholar, who read galleries and rooms and slates for a living and would sit at the big slab at the Throat with every man in the Pit coming past one at a time, and would read the sixteen slabs first, because they would be what Tullus sent up, and would know, before Jonah's arm ever touched the glass, that a man had been counting in that room for twenty-three years in the old tongue and had written, on the last line of the last slab, in a hand that was not his own, his own name, and given to the wall he drew, and counted.

And below it, nothing. And above it, a seal.

He'd left the seal. He'd cut the name below the tree and below the seal and had not thought about the seal, because it was a shape, and he had drilled it thirty times a night for a year until it came from his palm before he finished asking, and it had stopped being a thing he saw. A Master's seal. In the margin of a slab of the Vein, in a stone room at the bottom of the Throat, in the hand of a man who had been Master and had been unstripped for it in the Forum, and a scholar would look at it and know what it was and know what it opened, and would look at the name written below it in a different hand, and would ask, in the right voice, whose hand.

"Three days," Pell said, to the floor. "That's how it goes."

"Yes."

"You'll do the thing with your breath."

"Yes."

"Six hours, Ulli says. You did it twice."

"Yes."

The old man from Marl looked up from the floor, which he did perhaps once a year, and looked at Jonah with his farmer's eyes, and said, in the voice for things he had seen before: "It's not six hours this time, cut one. It's a scholar. A scholar doesn't read your arm. A scholar reads the room you were in."

Jonah stood in the road.

Tacitus, in the doorway of him, heard the road say three days, and heard the man it was in think about a seal in a margin, and did not lean at the wall, and did not go still the way it went still under a glass, flat in a ditch, because it had not been told to yet.

It waited. The way it had waited on the deck of the Kestrel behind his heart, after Varro, a thing that had done something enormous and did not know if it was good.

Waiting to be asked.

"Three days," Jonah said, aloud, to nobody, in the old tongue. And then, because it had a name now, and because the old man had said that a mind asked nothing does not stay polite: "I'll need you to be nothing. For longer than six hours. I don't know how long. I'll ask you when. Will you?"

It did not answer. It had no words.

But it settled. Into the coat, into the middle, into every part of him, the way it had in the Chamber when he said the word, and he understood that this was what an answer felt like from a thing that had none, and that he was going to have to learn to hear it very quickly, because in three days a scholar was going to sit at a slab at the top of the Throat and read the room he had spent six years in, and he was going to stand in a line, and hold, and the scholar was going to look up from the glass at his face.

He did not know, yet, whose face would be looking back.
