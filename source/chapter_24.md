# Chapter 24: Tacitus

He said it in the Chamber, because it was the only room in the Pit that was quiet, and because the old man had drawn it.

Getting there took a week. He had known it would. Five sizes at the face had cost him nine days of plate on his arms. This was a piece of the first cut, given by the wall's own hand, and it was worse. The coat went over him door on door, stacking, and his body had to grow into every one of them while it was on. He had the fever for six days, even and constant. He cut his shard. He said his number. He stood at the wall of the seventh chamber with the chisel and sweated through his shirt while the Vein opened for him.

"You're going," Pell said, to the wall. He had said it so many times it had stopped being words. It was just what he did now, the same as the songs. "Sixth year. Nobody takes six years to go."

"I'm not going."

"I know. I'm saying it so it's said." The old man cut his line. "You went down. Ulli said. Past the last lamp. He said the wall breathed out."

"It did."

"And you came back up with something."

Jonah looked at his hands on the chisel.

"A name," he said.

Pell did not ask whose. He had stopped asking in the fourth year. He put it away and cut.

The plate was wrong.

On the derelict it had been wrong everywhere, with no shape at all. This was different. This coat was cut for a bigger man, and he had to grow into it. He had learned the plate over six years: how it sat on the back of a hand, or the whole of a forearm, or a Master's seal on his palm. None of that held anymore. He asked for the back of a hand and got the whole arm. He asked for the seal and got the seal and a hook.

On the third night, lying on the shelf with the fever on him, he asked for stillness. The plate came up over his face in the dark. It sealed his mouth and nose shut. He could not breathe. He clawed at it with both hands and felt nothing under his fingers, only his own skin gone slick. For three long seconds he thought he would die on a shelf, with Pell breathing above him, never having said the name at all. Then it thinned. He sucked air and lay shaking, the taste of the Vein in his mouth.

I've lost it, he thought. I've got a week to get it back before a Flamen comes down that Throat and asks for my arm.

He had not lost it. The old man had told him once: it settles faster because it thinks, but into something slightly different every time, and after a big change you wake up and have to learn your own plate again.

So he learned it. On a rock, in the dark, thirty times, same as everything else. Tacitus learned with him, watching, as it had for six years, not flat, never flat, the whole attention of something that had been alone in a box for two hundred years and had finally found someone to look at.

By the sixth night he had the back of the hand. By the seventh, the seal. On the eighth night he opened the Chamber door with a fang from his palm, thin as a pick, exactly as thin as he had asked. He went in and locked it behind him. He sat down on the floor in front of sixteen slabs he could not see and did not need to.

---

"I told you I'd find one," he said, in the old tongue, to the doorway of him, where it stood, listening differently than it ever had. The Chamber was whole dark. Tullus's men had not brought a lamp since month's end, and month's end was tomorrow. The shelf sat empty with a stick across it. The slabs leaned on the rock.

"I had to be somewhere you could hear it," he said. "He told me that once. Somewhere you can hear the answer. I thought he meant me hearing yours. He meant this too. A name's something you say once, and then it's said. I wanted it said where it would stay." He put his hand flat on the floor. "This room's got six hundred and forty-two names in it, and one at the top I couldn't say for six weeks. It's the only room in this Pit that knows what a name is for. So."

It listened.

"Ulli said call it what it is. Quietest thing he's ever known. Seven years he's listened, to me, the rock, the wall, and you've never once said anything. Not a tap. Not a song. You went into me on a deck and read me. You ate my collar and put a blade through a Stripe's throat. You gave a piece of yourself to a rod and made one sound, once, inside my bones. Wasn't a word either." He breathed. "I learned a word for what you are on the sixtieth night, in the noise, before I knew what any of it meant. Heard it the way you hear your own name in a crowd. Never had a use for it. I've got one now."

He said it.

One word, in the old tongue, the ending right, in a room the old man had drawn, to something the old man had hidden in a box and told him, on a shelf, was someone.

"Tacitus."

The Chamber did not do anything. The dark stayed dark. The slabs leaned on the rock. Somewhere up the road the cage came down; he felt it through the floor. Somewhere further Ulli was singing.

And it heard its name.

He felt it hear. He would try to describe that once, later, to a woman with a broad stripe. He would get only this far: it was like saying a man's name across a road and watching him turn. Except the man had never once been called anything, and had not known that he could be. It turned fully. It came out of the doorway and into the room of him, through the arm, the shoulder, the old break in the wrist, the heart. It stopped at the heart. It always did. And stayed.

"Tacitus," Jonah said again, quietly, to be sure.

It answered. Not in words; it had none. He felt it in the place where it had stopped, and it was not hunger, not interest, not the want from the Mouth. It settled into the coat, into the scar at the seam, into every part of him at once. The plate that had been a size too big for a week stopped being too big. It fit. He put his hand on his chest. It was Tacitus, and it was staying.

"All right," he said. "That's said."

He got up.

"Tacitus," he said again, at the door, palm on the steel, the fang finding the pins. "You go at the top. Above him. He put himself first so the rest would know he hadn't exempted himself, and you were in the box before he was in this room. Tacitus. Aulus Terentius Pharus. Then the rest." He turned the bar. "That's the count. It starts with you now."

The lock did what locks do. He went out onto the road.

---

He said it there too, that night, in the dark between two lamps. All of them: six hundred and forty-two, and one at the top, three words. It heard its name said on the road, where the old man had wanted, and it would expect that every night after. It would not wander again. It had somewhere to be.

Decimus was at the cage.

He sat the stool with the rifle across his knees, which had not been his post for six years. Jonah had noticed him there most nights since winter and had put it away without looking at it, because looking at Decimus was heavy work.

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"You've a fever."

"I've had it a week. It's going."

"You've said that six years." The old guard looked at him a long time, the same look he gave anything he had decided not to mention. "Sit."

Jonah sat against the cage frame, same as four years ago with a letter.

"I signed," Decimus said, to the lamp. "Another five. In the autumn, when the first five was up. I was at the surface with the paper that said I'd served, and the house on Campania if I wanted it. The transport for Etruria was on the dock, and I signed." His face did nothing. "The aunt's dead. Two years. Fees went up with the war, everything does. A guard's pay on Etruria is a third of what it is here. There's a tree in a garden Sula's got the care of, and I'm not going to be the reason it dies." He shifted the rifle. "So I signed. Five more. She'll be sixteen. I haven't written to tell her, because I don't know how to write that a man who said two winters signed for five. It's not personal. I've said that so many times I don't know what it means anymore. It's the only thing I've got that isn't."

"You could have told me," Jonah said. "Before. In the autumn."

"I'm telling you."

"You'd have counted it." Decimus almost smiled, the smile from the tunnel, which Jonah had seen perhaps six times in six years and kept every one. "You count everything. I didn't want it weighing on you. You've enough, four four seven one. I thought you'd find out at the count, when I'm still here, and say nothing, because you've learned that from me." He looked down the road. "And you did. Said nothing since the autumn. I've watched you not say it. So I'm saying it, so it's said."

"Sula."

"Sixteen. Counting."

"When the five are up."

"Twenty-one." He was quiet a moment. "She'll have stopped counting by then. That's what keeps me up. Not the wall, not Sabinus, not the count. A girl who counts five winters and then five more learns the counting doesn't bring anybody home. She'll be right." He put his hand on the rifle. "That's mine. I've told it. Put it with the rest."

Jonah put it with the rest. It weighed. Decimus had been right about that, and right that Jonah would say nothing.

"There's something," Decimus said.

"What?"

"Tomorrow. Month's end. Tullus's men go down with the lamp and the key. Twenty-three years, and they always come back without the lamp, with a slate instead. Last month there was no slate. Rennick says the prefect's been in the garrison hall every night doing the galleries by hand, and he can't. Nobody can; it was always done for him. He's afraid." Decimus turned his head. "Tomorrow they go down again, and I think whatever's behind that door won't be. I think you know that. And when they come up without a slate and a face on them, a frightened man's going to do what a frightened man does. Count."

"Count what?"

"Everything. Everyone. With a glass." He picked up his rifle. "He'll send for the College. Not the every-second-year College. The ones that come with scholars, when something's gone wrong, to read the galleries and the room and every man on this road, to find who knows what happened to whatever was locked in. Twenty years ago on Etruria, a Thinstripe's house lorica went missing from a shrine. They read the whole town." He stood, his knees cracking. "Whatever it is you do with your breath under a glass, I'd start doing it now. It's not personal."

"Decimus. Thank you."

He looked at Jonah from the stool, the lamp on his lined face, the letter Jonah knew was four years old against his own chest with two marks at the edge that were six.

"Don't," he said. "It's a count. I'm telling you what's coming because you're on my road and I'd like my road alive when it comes." He went down toward the Shallows, saying numbers.

Jonah sat against the cage and put his hand flat on his own chest. Tacitus was there. It had heard all of it. For the first time in six years, it was not listening to the wall at all.

It was listening to him.

---

They went down at the fourth bell, two of Tullus's men in garrison grey, lamp and key. Jonah stood at the wall of the seventh chamber with a chisel and felt them through the rock, past the Cradle, to the door.

He felt the key. He felt the door open on hinges oiled once a month for twenty-three years, without a sound. He felt the two men stand in the doorway with a lamp and a slate, and do nothing, for a hundred breaths.

Then he felt them run.

He cut his shard. It came off clean. He dropped it in the bucket and stood with his arms shaking. That was what the Vein did to a man it was taking. Pell did not look up. The road did not know yet. It would, by the dusk count, because everything came down the Throat as a number, and this was going to come down as one.

It came down as three.

"Three days," Decimus read at the dusk count, off the list, in the voice for things that were not personal. He read it twice. "Every man on the road to the Throat, by decury, for the glass. The College is coming down with scholars. The galleries are to be read, and." He stopped. "And the Chamber. Every man on this road is to be read at the Throat, on the big slab, by a scholar. Any man who does not go up to be read is to be counted as having gone into the wall, and struck." He made a mark. "Prefect's order. Three days."

The road did not make a sound.

Jonah stood at the end of decury six, fever gone, plate fitted, with Tacitus in him, named now, listening to him and not the wall. Three days. A scholar. Not a robe with a wand; not six hours in a doorway. Someone who read galleries and rooms for a living. Someone who would sit at the big slab while every man came past, one at a time. Who would read the sixteen slabs first, because they were what Tullus sent up. Who would know that a man had been writing in that room, in the old tongue, for twenty-three years. That on the last line, in a hand that was not his own, someone had written his own name and given it to the wall, and counted.

And above it, a seal.

He'd left the seal. Cut it below the tree, below the seal itself, and never thought about the seal, because it was a shape he'd drilled thirty times a night for a year until it came from his palm before he finished asking. A Master's seal, in the margin of a slab of the Vein, in the hand of a man who had been Master and been unstripped for it in the Forum. A scholar would know what it was. Would know what it opened. Would ask, in the right voice, whose hand.

"Three days," Pell said, to the floor. "That's how it goes."

"Yes."

"You'll do it with your breath."

"Yes."

"Six hours, Ulli says. You did it twice."

"Yes."

Pell looked up from the floor, which he did perhaps once a year, and said, in the voice for things he had seen before: "It's not six hours this time, cut one. It's a scholar. A scholar doesn't read your arm. A scholar reads the room you were in."

Tacitus heard the road say three days, and heard what Jonah was thinking about the seal in the margin. It did not lean at the wall. It waited, the way it had waited on the Kestrel's deck after Varro: it had done something enormous and did not know yet if it was good.

Waiting to be asked.

"Three days," Jonah said aloud, to nobody, in the old tongue. Then, because it had a name now, and a mind asked nothing does not stay polite: "I'll need you to be nothing. For longer than six hours. I don't know how long yet. Will you?"

It did not answer. It had no words.

But it settled, into the coat, into every part of him, same as in the Chamber when he said the word. He was going to have to learn to hear that very quickly. In three days a scholar would sit at a slab at the top of the Throat and read the room he had spent six years in.

He did not know yet whose face would be looking back.
