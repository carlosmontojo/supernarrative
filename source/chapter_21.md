# Chapter 21: The Sertorian Form

"A fang," the Censor said, "is a thing a man draws. That is the whole of what is wrong with it. Give me your hand."

Jonah gave it. Fourth year, and the old man had never asked for his hand before. He had asked for the plate, the fang, a hook, a pick, and beaten each of them out with a stick until they came when called. Now he turned the hand palm up in the lamplight and studied it.

"Every duellist in the Empire fights a man who is going to draw. Vibia waits for the draw and stops it for a heartbeat. Claudia walks into it and takes it on the plate. Fulvia circles it and cuts around it. Aemilia stands off and throws. Four answers to one question: where is his fang, and when is it coming out. Good answers, for four hundred years." He let go. "Sertoria asked a different question."

"What?"

"What if it is already out."

---

The house came first. It always did. You learned the history before you were let near the stick.

"Quintus Sertorius," the Censor said, with the stick across his knees. "A general in the Colony Wars, in the last decade before the Principate. On the side that lost. He did not lose. Say it back."

"He didn't lose."

"He won every battle he fought. So they sent him to the far edge of the settled worlds, to a rock nobody wanted, with a legion nobody wanted either. Halyard, Ferrum and Marl. He made a Senate on that rock. His own stripes, his own law. He held it eleven years against everything the first Princeps sent, and he was never once beaten in the field."

"So how did he die?"

"His friends killed him at dinner. A man who cannot be beaten in the field will never let the war end, and they wanted it ended. They put a fang in him over the fish course and sent his head to the Princeps in a box. The Princeps wept, it is written. Then he had every man at that dinner cut. The house was struck. The lorica went to the columbarium in the City with the other dead houses, and it has slept there three hundred and ninety years."

"You've seen it."

"I was Master. I have stood in the columbarium with the keys and looked at it in its niche. It is the only lorica in that room I have ever wanted to touch."

"Why?"

"Every house has a gift. Vibia has Verdict. Claudia has the Wall. Sertoria had Quiet. A glass cannot read it. Sight cannot see it. A Flamen with the rod in his hand and the lorica on the slab in front of him reads void. It is not void. It is sleeping, and nobody can find it to wake it." The pale eyes came up. "Your genius hides from a glass by holding its breath. Six hours, you told me. Then it breathes, and the glass sees a wobble. Quiet does not hold its breath. Quiet is not there. That is the gift, and it is going to be yours."

"How?"

"You are going to eat it. Then you are going to wear it, whole, as you wore a Blank's cutting on Anvil. You will walk into the Senate with the broad stripe of a dead house on your arm. No glass in the Empire will be able to say what you are."

"That's a long way from a rock in the Deep."

"That is the far end. The near end is the Form." The Censor picked up the stick. "Sertorius did not have what you have. He had a lorica, cut and made small. But the Flamens cut into it the one shape nobody had cut before or since: no shape at all. His fang came from wherever his plate was. He built a way of fighting on that. The book burned with the house. I read it once, in the College's vault, when I was thirty and thought I could be trusted with anything. Get up."

---

The first rule: there was no draw.

The old man beat that into him for a month. It was worse than the Fulvian step, because the step had been a dead man's habit and this was his own. Four years of the fang out of the back of the right wrist. Always the same place, crooked, a hand long. His body expected it there. The stick landed on the wrist every time it started to come.

Crack.

"You are drawing."

"I'm bringing it out."

"You are drawing. The shoulder turns. The weight goes onto the right foot. Then the fang. A Vibian sees the shoulder and stops you. A Claudian sees the foot and walks into it. You can put a blade anywhere on your body, and you have taught it to come out of one place with a tell a child could read." The stick came off. "Again. This time, do not bring it out."

"Then what?"

"Put your hand on me."

Jonah put his hand flat on the old man's chest.

"Now. Not from the wrist. From the hand. From where it already is."

He thought, in the old tongue: here.

The fang came out of his palm.

Not thin, as it had for the lock. A hand's length of dark glass, straight, out of the middle of his hand, and his hand was already flat on an old man's chest. It stopped a finger from the rag of the robe. The genius had learned in four years exactly what a finger was. The Censor looked down at it. He did not step back.

"Yes. That."

"It didn't."

"It did not draw. It was born where the hand was." The old man put his own hand over Jonah's and pushed the fang gently back into the palm. It went. "Your hand was on me before the blade existed. Every other Form asks where the fang is. Sertoria puts the hand on the seam and then has a fang. It is never seen coming. It is not there. Then a man has his hand on your throat, and then it is."

He stepped back and picked up the stick.

"Now. Your hand is a blade. Your elbow, your shoulder, your knee. The back of your neck, if a man is fool enough to put his throat there. You do not draw. You touch, and you decide. Come and touch me."

Jonah came.

He got nowhere. He went for the chest and the stick took his wrist. He went low and it took his knee. He came in under it and it was across his ribs before his hand was halfway there, and he was on the rock.

"Up."

He got up. He went for the shoulder. The old man was not where the shoulder had been. The stick hit the back of his neck.

"Up."

"You're seventy."

"I am aware of it. Up."

Down. Up. Down. On the thirtieth fall, lying on the rock with his ribs on fire, he understood the difference. He was not trying to reach a wrist anymore. He was trying to reach a man. Any part of him. The man was seventy. He had eaten one bowl a day for twenty-one years, and some nights now he coughed.

"You're teaching me this now," Jonah said from the floor, "because I'm going to touch you."

"Yes."

"Not tonight."

"No," the Censor said. "Up."

---

"Bearing," the Censor said, on a night in the fourth year when the first hour was done and Jonah was still on his feet. "Sit down. Not there. There. That is a couch. You are at dinner."

There was a rock the size of a bucket on the floor of the Cradle. The old man had rolled it there. Jonah sat on it.

"You are at dinner in the house of a man of the senatorial order. He has invited you because you are new in the City, with a broad stripe and a house name nobody has heard in four centuries. He wants to know what you are. Eleven others at the table. Two Broadstripes, three Thinstripes of the better sort, the rest wives and a Flamen. You are seated ninth, because your grade is unknown. Before the second course the host will read you with his house gift. It will be Pulse; half the great houses have it. You will lie to him." The old man sat on the floor across from the rock. "That is the fish course. Eat."

"There's nothing to eat."

"There is a fish on the rock. Campanian river fish, grilled, with a sauce. You eat it with the two-pronged fork, not the three; the three is for the meat. You eat it slowly. You do not look at the host while you eat. You look at the woman on your left and say something about the fish." The Censor folded his hands. "Say something about the fish."

Jonah looked at the rock.

"It's a good rock," he said.

The stick took him across the shin.

"It is a fish. It is a very good fish; the host's cook is famous for it. You will say so in the old tongue, in a sentence with a subordinate clause in it, because a Broadstripe never says anything straight when it can be said around a corner. You will say it to the woman on your left. She is the wife of the Thinstripe seated fourth and she is bored. You will be bored too. You have learned to stand like a man who has been beaten. Now learn to sit like a man who has never once been interested in anything."

"That's the whole of Bearing?"

"That is the whole of Bearing. Fish."

Three hours on a rock, eating a fish that was not there, saying things about it around corners to a woman who was a lamp. The old man was the host, the Flamen and the Thinstripe seated fourth, in three voices. The stick came for every straight sentence, every glance at the host, and every time Jonah's hands held on to the table.

He laughed at first. He could not help it. He was at the bottom of a Throat with a dead man's fish and an old man doing a bored wife's voice. The stick came. He laughed less.

"The sauce," said the wife, in the Censor's voice.

"The sauce, madam, which I am told is the cook's own, does not disgrace the fish."

"Better. The host is watching you."

"Let him. I'm eating."

Somewhere in the third hour he stopped laughing. He was saying something about the sauce, around a corner, to the lamp, and he was bored. Not pretending. He was a man who had eaten this fish at a hundred tables and had never once wondered where it came from. The old man stopped being the wife.

"There. You have stopped finding it funny."

"Yes."

"Then you have passed. Do not do it again where I can see; I will want to hit you." The Censor got up off the floor. His knees made a sound they had not made before. "A Collar thinks a Broadstripe is a man who has everything. He is not. He is a man who has never wanted anything, because it was there before he could want it. He is bored to his bones. Be bored beside him and he will believe anything else about you. Get up. Bring the fish."

---

Tremor came in the fifth year, at the wall.

Decury six was six again. Two transports had come down the Throat and Decimus had read the numbers. Jonah had told them, cut a shard, don't make me guess, and they had cut. He had chosen a man when the count was short, and not chosen soft. He had chosen himself once more, when the arithmetic said so.

And at the wall, with the chisel, with the Vein opening for him, he began to feel the road.

Not hear. Feel. The dark glass under his skin was in every part of him now, and it touched the rock wherever he stood. A guard's boots forty paces off, through the floor of the seventh chamber. The cage coming down. And on a night in the fifth year, Decimus, stopping outside the chamber mouth. Standing there. Ten breaths. Not coming in. Then going on.

Jonah did not know why. He cut his shard.

"That is Tremor," the Censor said, when he told him. "A gift. One of six that every duelling house has had for a century and never used. A lorica made small learns it in the tenth year of drill and forgets it in the eleventh. Yours has learned it in five, from standing on a rock, because it is not made small and it was interested in the rock." He did not seem pleased. "A small thing. You will use it every day of your life. Sabinus does not have it; the Claudian Form does not want it; a wall does not care what is coming. Keep it. Tell me when it is more than boots."

"Like what?"

"Like a heart," the Censor said. "Through a table."

---

He touched the old man in the spring of the fifth year, and it was the shoulder.

A year of trying. He had stopped counting falls. He was twenty-eight on a glass that could not read him, with a hand that was a blade. Every night the stick: wrist, knee, ribs. Every night closer, a finger, a hand. Every night the old man stepped, once, the water step Sabinus had done on the road, into the place the hand was not.

Jonah had watched it three hundred times. He had done half of it in a cut in the dark.

On a night in spring he did the other half.

Not the water step. That belonged to Sabinus, Auger and the Censor, and every time Jonah tried it, it came out half. He did what Auger had done. He watched the old man step, and he did not put his hand where the old man was. He put it where the old man was going.

The stick came across his ribs. It always did. He took it. His left hand, which had hung useless at his side for the whole of the year, was already on the old man's shoulder, because the shoulder had stepped into it. The fang came out of the palm and stopped a finger from the robe, at the seam where a plated man's shoulder meets his neck.

The old man stopped.

He stood very still with a stick across a Collar's ribs and a Collar's hand on his shoulder. He looked at the hand. Then at Jonah's face. The grief went across it, with the other thing under. This time the other thing was on top.

He laughed.

Not the one breath through the nose. The whole of it, out loud, in the Cradle, with the Vein breathing behind them. Cracked and high and going on. Jonah kept his hand on the shoulder. The Censor laughed until he coughed, and the cough went on longer than the laugh. When it stopped there was blood on his lip. He wiped it with the back of his hand, looked at it, and laughed again, shorter.

"Sertorius," he said. "That was Sertorius. The hand where the man is going. Not where he is. Do you understand what you did?"

"I put my hand where you'd step."

"And you did not know I would step there. You have never known. Three hundred times I have stepped there and you have never once been there. You did not decide. Your hand went. That is the Form. That is the whole of the book that burned. The hand goes where the man is going and then there is a blade. One day you are standing with your hand on a Centurion's shoulder and a fang in it, and he is saying oh." He wiped his lip again. "Take your hand off. You have won. I am seventy-three and coughing blood. I have been beaten by a barge pilot on a rock I drew, and I have never in my life been so pleased with anything."

Jonah took his hand off.

"The blood."

"Is the wall. It has been in the rock with me for twenty-two years. I am a stone, but a stone wears. I have a year. Perhaps less. I have known since the winter." He bent for the stick. He had dropped it. Jonah had never seen him drop it. "It changes nothing. It means you learn faster. Sit. Not the rock; the floor. I have to give you something tonight and it is not a Form."

---

"The keys," the Censor said. "They are not keys. They are three sentences in the old tongue that a Master says to the door of the columbarium on Latium, and a mark. The Master's own lorica makes the mark on a glass beside the door. I cannot give you the mark. My lorica has been in a cup in the College's vaults for twenty-two years."

"So I make it."

"So you make it. Your genius wore a Blank's cutting for a Flamen's glass. It will wear a Master's seal for a door. It is a shape. I will draw it on the slab and you will drill it as you drilled the hook."

"And inside?"

"Four hundred niches. Three hundred and eleven of them full. The dead houses: every lorica of every line that was struck, or died out, or was cut in a war. A house lorica without a house does not die. It waits. The College keeps them because one day a Princeps may want to give a dead house to a friend. It has happened, twice." His voice was even. "Sertoria is in the ninth row, third niche from the eastern wall. It is not marked. Quiet does not mark. Your genius will feel it, as it felt the wall: a place where something is that the glass cannot see. You will put your hand on it. It will do what it did to the cutting on Anvil and to the decurion in the hold. Except that this is three hundred and ninety years of a house that won every battle, with a gift in it that hides. It will be the largest meal your genius has ever had, and I do not know what it will do."

"You don't know."

"I have never known. I hid it in a box because I did not know. You will stand there in five years, or six, and decide, and I will not be there." He looked at the lamp. "Then you wear it. Not the cutting's coat. The stripe. Broad, on your arm, drawn, as it drew the line on Anvil. And you walk out of the columbarium as Gaius Sertorius, last of a house nobody remembers. A bastard branch from the far Limes that kept the name four centuries and has come to the City to claim the seat. The Senate will let you, because a house is a seat and a seat is a vote. There are men in that chamber who will pay a great deal for one more vote, and they will not ask where the far Limes is."

"Gaius."

"The most common name in the rolls. Nobody remembers a Gaius." The Censor almost smiled. "Nobody remembers a Jonah either. That is the point of both."

"And then?"

"Then you are a Broadstripe with a fang that is not there until it is, in a City full of men who burn seals. You find the four. You do what you have decided to do about them, and I have not asked what that is and I will not." He set the stick across his knees. "One day you find the Princeps, and you say, I know what the wall is. That is mine. That is the one thing I ask, and I have said I ask nothing, so I do not ask it. I say it. So it is said."

"So it's said."

The Censor coughed. It went on. When it stopped he looked at the blood on his hand and did not wipe it.

"The College comes down the Throat with the glass in the autumn of next year. I have counted it. The sixth year; they come every second. They came in the fourth. You held your breath for six hours. A young one in a clean robe frowned at his glass and wrote something, you told me."

"He wrote something. I didn't see what."

"It was nothing. You were seventeen and holding a Tiro. You are twenty-eight now, and thirty by the autumn. Thirty is not a Tiro. You will hold it six hours or you will not, and I will not be here to tap you through it." His hand closed on the stick. "So there is a year. In a year you will know the Form as you know your hand. You will make a Master's seal from your palm. You will have Tremor to the width of a heart. You will have the words. You will have sixteen slabs in your head, because I am going to make you read them until you can say every column. Then I am going to die, and you are going to go up the Throat."

"How?"

"I have not the faintest idea," the Censor said. "I drew the Pit. Nobody has ever left it. That is the one thing in this rock I did not draw, boy, and it is yours. Get up."

Jonah got up.

The stick came out of the dark before he was on his feet. He took it on the wrist. He took it on the knee. Down, up, down. On the fourth fall his left hand was on the old man's shoulder again, with the fang a finger off the seam.

The Censor laughed. Coughed. Wiped his mouth.

"Again," he said.
