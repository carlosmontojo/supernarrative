# Chapter 21: The Sertorian Form

"A fang," the Censor said, "is a thing a man draws. That is the whole of what is wrong with it. Give me your hand."

Jonah gave him his hand. It was the fourth year and the old man had not asked for his hand before; he had asked for the plate on the back of it, and the fang out of the wrist, and a hook, and a pick, and he had beaten every one of them with a stick until they came when asked. He had never taken the hand. He took it now, in the thread of lamp on the rock of the Cradle, and turned it palm up, and looked at it the way he had looked at the plate on the first night.

"Every duellist in the Empire," the old man said, "fights a man who is going to draw. That is what the four Forms are. Vibia waits for the draw and stops it for a heartbeat. Claudia walks into the draw and takes it on the plate. Fulvia circles the draw and cuts around it. Aemilia stands off from the draw and throws. Four answers to one question, which is: where is his fang, and when is it coming out. And they are good answers. They have been good answers for four hundred years." He let go of the hand. "Sertoria asked a different question."

"What?"

"What if it is already out."

---

He'd learned the house first. That was the old man's way; a thing had a name and the name had a history and you learned the history before you were let near the thing, the way you learned the endings before the tongue.

Quintus Sertorius had been a general in the Colony Wars, in the last decade before the Principate, on the side that lost. He had not lost. That was the thing the old man said three times in the first hour, in the old tongue, with the stick across his knees. He had won every battle he fought. He had been sent to the far edge of the settled worlds, to a rock the Roma ark's people had not wanted, with a legion the Roma ark's people had not wanted either, Halyard and Ferrum and Marl, and he had made a Senate there, of his own, on the rock, with its own stripes and its own law, and he had held it for eleven years against everything the first Princeps sent, and he had never once been beaten in the field.

"And then his friends killed him at dinner," the Censor said. "Because a man who cannot be beaten in the field is a man who will never let the war end, and they wanted it ended. They put a fang in him over the fish course and sent his head to the Princeps in a box, and the Princeps wept, it is written, and then had every man at that dinner cut, and the house of Sertorius was struck from the rolls, and its lorica, which had eleven years of the far edge in it and a gift nobody else has ever had, was taken to the City and put in the columbarium with the other dead houses, and it has slept there for three hundred and ninety years." The old man looked at the lamp. "I have seen it. I was Master. I have stood in the columbarium with the keys and looked at it in its niche, and it is the only lorica in that room that I have ever wanted to touch."

"Why?"

"Because of what it does. Every house has a gift. Vibia has Verdict. Claudia has the Wall. Sertoria had a thing the Flamens called Quiet, and what Quiet does is this: a glass cannot read it. Sight cannot see it. A Flamen with the rod in his hand and the lorica on the slab in front of him reads void, and it is not void, it is sleeping, and it has been sleeping for four centuries because nobody can find it to wake it." The pale eyes came up. "You have a thing in you that hides from a glass by holding its breath. It can hold its breath for six hours, you have told me, and then it must breathe, and when it breathes the glass sees a wobble. Quiet does not hold its breath. Quiet is not there. That is the gift, and it is in a niche in the City, and it is going to be yours."

"How?"

"You are going to eat it," the Censor said. "And then you are going to wear it, the way you wore a Blank's cutting on Anvil, except that you will wear it whole, and you will walk into the Senate of the Republic with the broad stripe of a dead house on your arm and a gift on you that means no glass in the Empire can say what you are." He picked up the stick. "But that is the far end of it. The near end is the Form. Sertorius did not have your thing. He had a lorica, cut, made small, with a shape in it like every other. But the shape the Flamens cut into it was the one thing nobody had thought to cut into a lorica before or since, which is no shape at all. His fang came from wherever his plate was. And he built a way of fighting on that, and it won every battle, and it is in a book that was burned when the house was struck, and I read the book, once, in the College's vault, when I was thirty and thought there was nothing in that vault I could not be trusted with. And I am going to give it to you now. Get up."

---

The first thing was that there was no draw.

The old man beat that into him for a month, the way he had beaten the Fulvian step out, and it was worse, because the Fulvian step had been a dead man's and this was his. He had drawn the fang out of the back of his wrist for four years. He'd drawn it in the Kestrel's hold and in the seventh chamber and at the face and in the cut, and every time it had come the same way, from the same place, crooked, a hand long, and his body had learned to expect it there the way his legs had learned to step left, and the Censor put the stick on the back of his right wrist every time it started to come.

"You are drawing."

"I'm bringing the fang out."

"You are drawing. You are reaching for the wrist with your whole body, the shoulder turns, the weight goes onto the right foot, and then the fang comes. A Vibian sees the shoulder turn and stops you. A Claudian sees the weight go onto the foot and walks into it. You have a thing that can put a blade anywhere on you and you have taught it to come out of one place with a tell a child could read." The stick came off the wrist. "Again. And this time, do not bring it out."

"Then what?"

"Put your hand on me."

Jonah put his hand on the old man's chest, flat, the way he had put it on the wall.

"Now. Not from the wrist. From the hand. From where it already is."

He thought, in the old tongue: here.

The fang came out of his palm.

Not thin, the way it had for the lock. A fang. A hand's length of dark glass, straight, out of the middle of his hand, where his hand was already flat on an old man's chest, and it stopped a finger from the rag of a robe because the thing under his ribs had learned in four years exactly what a finger was, and the Censor looked down at it, and did not step back, and said, very quietly:

"Yes. That."

"It didn't."

"It did not draw. It did not come from anywhere. It was born where the hand was." The old man put his own hand over Jonah's, over the fang, and pushed it gently back into the palm, and it went. "Your hand was on me before the blade existed. That is the Form. Every other Form asks where the fang is. Sertoria puts the hand where the seam is and then has a fang. The fang is never drawn. It is never seen coming. It is not there, and then a man has his hand on your throat, and then it is."

He stepped back and picked up the stick.

"Now. Your hand is a blade. Your elbow is a blade. Your shoulder, your knee, the back of your neck if a man is fool enough to put his throat there. You do not draw. You touch, and you decide. Come and touch me."

Jonah came.

He did not get near. The stick was there, the way it had been for four years, on the wrist, on the knee, across the ribs, and he went down, and got up, and went down, and the difference, the thing he understood on the rock on the thirtieth fall, was that he was not trying to reach a wrist anymore. He was trying to reach a man. Any part of him. And the old man, who had never let him reach anything in four years, was seventy, and had eaten one bowl a day for twenty-one of them, and coughed, some nights, now, in a way he had not before, and Jonah lay on the rock and thought, I am going to touch him. Not tonight. But I am, and he knows it, and that's why he's teaching me this now.

---

"Bearing," the Censor said, on a night in the fourth year when the first hour was done and Jonah was on his feet, which was most nights now. "Sit down. Not there. There. That is a couch. You are at dinner."

There was a rock. It was the size of a bucket and it sat on the floor of the Cradle where the old man had rolled it, and Jonah sat on it, and it was a couch.

"You are at dinner in the house of a man of the senatorial order who has invited you because you are new in the City and have a broad stripe and a house name nobody has heard in four centuries, and he wants to know what you are. There are eleven others at the table. Two are Broadstripes, three are Thinstripes of the better sort, the rest are wives and a Flamen. You are seated ninth, because your grade is unknown and a man of unknown grade is seated last but for the Flamen, and the host is going to read you with his house gift before the second course, and it will be Pulse, because half the great houses have it, and you will lie to him." The old man sat on the floor across from the rock. "That is the rock. That is the bucket. It is the fish course. Eat."

"There's nothing to eat."

"There is a fish on the rock. It is a Campanian river fish, grilled, with a sauce, and you will eat it with the two-pronged fork and not the three, because the three is for the meat, and you will eat it slowly, and you will not look at the host while you eat it, because a man of the senatorial order does not look at his host while eating, he looks at the woman on his left and says something about the fish." The Censor folded his hands. "Say something about the fish."

Jonah looked at the rock.

"It's a good rock," he said.

The stick took him across the shin.

"It is a fish. It is a very good fish; the host's cook is famous for it. And you will say so in the old tongue, in a sentence with a subordinate clause in it, because a Broadstripe never says a thing straight when it can be said around a corner, and you will say it to the woman on your left, who is the wife of the Thinstripe seated fourth and is bored, and you will be bored too. That is the whole of Bearing, boy. You have learned to stand like a man who has been beaten. Now learn to sit like a man who has never once been interested in anything."

It took three hours. Three hours on a rock, eating a fish that was not there, saying things about it around corners to a woman who was a lamp, and the old man across the floor being the host and the Flamen and the Thinstripe seated fourth, in three voices, and correcting him with the stick on every straight sentence and every look at the host and every time Jonah's hands did what a Collar's hands did at a table, which was hold on to it.

He laughed, at first. He couldn't help it. He was on a rock at the bottom of a Throat with a dead man's fish and an old man doing a bored wife's voice, and it was the most ridiculous thing he had done in five years, and he laughed, and the stick came, and he laughed less.

Somewhere in the third hour he stopped.

He would not be able to say when. He was saying a thing about the sauce, around a corner, in the old tongue, to the lamp, with the two-pronged fork that was his fingers, and he was bored. Not pretending. He was sitting on a rock being a man who had eaten this fish a hundred times at a hundred tables and would eat it a hundred more and had never once, in his life, wondered where it came from, and the boredom was in him the way the fever was, everywhere, evenly, and the old man across the floor stopped being the wife and was the Censor, and said:

"There. You have stopped finding it funny."

"Yes."

"Then you have passed. Do not do it again where I can see; I will want to hit you." The Censor got up off the floor, and his knees made a sound, which they had not before. "That is Bearing. A Collar thinks a Broadstripe is a man who has everything. He is not. He is a man who has never wanted anything, because it was there before he could want it, and he is bored, boy, he is bored to his bones, and if you can be bored beside him he will believe anything else about you. Get up. Bring the fish."

---

Tremor came in the galleries, in the fifth year, and it came because a man could not spend three hours a night on the rock of the Cradle without spending the other twenty-one somewhere.

He spent them at the wall. Decury six was six again; two transports had come down the Throat and Decimus had read the numbers and Jonah had said, cut a shard, don't make me guess, and they had cut, and he had chosen a man when the count was short and had not chosen soft, and had chosen himself once more, when the arithmetic said so, and the old man had not said anything about it this time, because it had. And at the wall, with the chisel, with the Vein opening for him like a door and the plate lying quiet under his skin the way it did now, fed on the stick and the slabs, he had begun to feel the road.

Not hear. Feel. Through the plate. Through the dark glass under his skin that was in every part of him now and had been since the face, and that touched, at every moment, the rock he stood on. He felt a guard's boots on the road forty paces off, through the floor of the seventh chamber, the way he'd felt the lock under the steel. He felt the cage come down. He felt, on a night in the fifth year, Decimus stop outside the chamber mouth and stand there for the length of ten breaths, not coming in, and then go on, and he did not know why and put it with the rest.

"That is Tremor," the Censor said, when he told him. "It is a gift. It is one of the six that every duelling house has had for a century and never used, because a lorica that was made small learns it in the tenth year of drill and forgets it in the eleventh. Yours has learned it in five, from standing on a rock, because it is not made small and it was interested in the rock." The old man did not seem pleased. "It is a small thing. You will use it every day of your life. Sabinus does not have it; the Claudian Form does not want it; a wall does not care what is coming. Keep it. Tell me when it is more than boots."

"Like what?"

"Like a heart," the Censor said. "Through a table."

---

He touched the old man in the fifth year, in the spring, and it was the shoulder.

He had been trying for a year. He'd stopped counting falls; he'd stopped counting nights; he had a thing in him that was twenty-eight on a glass that could not read it and a hand that was a blade and a way of standing that was nobody's, and every night for a year the stick had been there, on the wrist, the knee, the ribs, and every night he'd got closer, a finger, a hand, and every night the old man had stepped, once, the water step that Sabinus had done on the road, into the place the hand was not.

He'd watched it three hundred times. He'd done half of it in a cut in the dark. And on a night in the spring of the fifth year he did the other half.

Not the water step. That was not his; he'd tried it for a year and it was Sabinus's and Auger's and the Censor's, and every time he did it, it was half. He did the thing Auger had done. He watched the old man step, into the place the hand was not, the way the old man had stepped three hundred times, and he did not put his hand where the old man was. He put it where the old man was going.

The stick came across his ribs. It always did. And Jonah's left hand, which was nowhere near the stick, which had been at his side, useless, the whole of the year, was already on the old man's shoulder, because the old man's shoulder had stepped into it, and the thing under his ribs, which had learned in five years exactly what a finger was, put a fang out of the palm a finger from the rag of the robe, at the seam where a plated man's shoulder met his neck, and stopped.

The old man stopped.

He stood very still on the rock of the Cradle with a stick across a Collar's ribs and a Collar's hand on his shoulder with a blade in it that had not been drawn, and he looked at the hand, and then at Jonah's face, and the thing went across his own that Jonah had seen four times, the grief with the other thing under it, and this time the other thing was on top.

He laughed.

It was not the one breath through the nose. It was the whole of it, out loud, in the Cradle, with the Vein breathing behind them, an old man's laugh, cracked and high and going on, and Jonah stood with his hand on the shoulder and did not take it off, and the Censor laughed until he coughed, and the cough went on longer than the laugh, and when it stopped there was blood on the old man's lip, and he wiped it with the back of a duellist's hand and looked at it, and laughed again, shorter.

"Sertorius," he said. It was the first time he had said the name to Jonah's face. "That was Sertorius. The hand where the man is going. Not where he is. Do you understand what you did?"

"I put my hand where you'd step."

"You put your hand where I would step, and you did not know I would step there, you have never known, I have stepped there three hundred times and you have never once been there. You did not decide. Your hand went. That is the Form, boy, that is the whole of the book that burned. The hand goes where the man is going and then there is a blade. It is not a thing you draw. It is not a thing you decide. It is a thing you have, and one day you are standing with your hand on a Centurion's shoulder and a fang in it and he is saying oh." He wiped his lip again. "Take your hand off. You have won. I am seventy-three and coughing blood and I have been beaten by a barge pilot on a rock I drew, and I have never in my life been so pleased with anything."

Jonah took his hand off.

"The blood," he said.

"Is the wall. It has been in the rock with me for twenty-two years and I am a stone, but a stone wears. I have a year. Perhaps less. I have known since the winter." The old man picked up the stick; he'd dropped it, which Jonah had never seen. "It changes nothing. It means you learn faster. Sit. Not the rock; the floor. There is a thing I have to give you tonight and it is not a Form."

---

He gave him the keys.

They were not keys. They were words, in the old tongue, three sentences, that a Master of the College said to the door of the columbarium on Latium, and a mark that the Master's own lorica made on a glass beside the door, and the mark could not be given, because the Censor's lorica was in a cup somewhere in the College's vaults and had been for twenty-two years.

"So the mark is yours to make," the old man said. "Your thing can wear a Blank's cutting for a Flamen's glass. It will learn to wear a Master's seal for a door. It is a shape like any other. I will draw it for you on the slab and you will drill it the way you drilled the hook, and by the time you stand at that door it will come the way the hook comes."

"And inside?"

"Inside there are four hundred niches and three hundred and eleven of them are full, and they are the dead houses, every lorica of every line that was struck or died out or was cut in a war, and they sleep. A house lorica without a house does not die. It waits. The College keeps them because a genius with three hundred years in it is a thing you do not throw away, and because one day a Princeps may want to give a dead house to a friend, and it has happened, twice." The Censor's voice was even. "Sertoria is in the ninth row, third niche from the eastern wall. It is not marked. Quiet does not mark. You will know it because your thing will feel it, the way it felt the wall, as a place where something is that the glass cannot see. And you will put your hand on it. And it will do what it did to the cutting on Anvil and to the decurion in the hold, except that this is not a cutting. This is three hundred and ninety years of a house that won every battle, with a gift in it that hides, and your thing will eat it, and it will be the largest thing it has ever eaten, and I do not know what it will do."

"You don't know."

"I have never known. I hid it in a box because I did not know. I am telling you what is in the niche and what the words are, and you will stand there in five years, or six, and decide, and I will not be there." The old man looked at the lamp. "Then you will wear it. Not the cutting's coat. The stripe. Broad. On your arm, drawn, the way your thing drew the line on Anvil. And you will walk out of the columbarium as Gaius Sertorius, last of a house nobody remembers, a bastard branch from the far Limes that kept the name for four centuries and has come to the City to claim the seat, and the Senate will let you, because a house is a seat and a seat is a vote and there are men in that chamber who will pay a great deal for one more vote, and they will not ask where the far Limes is."

"Gaius."

"It is a name. It is the most common name in the rolls. Nobody remembers a Gaius." The Censor almost smiled. "Nobody remembers a Jonah either. That is the point of both."

Jonah sat on the floor of the Cradle.

"And then?" he said.

"And then you are a Broadstripe with a fang that is not there until it is, in a City full of men who burn seals, and you find the four, and you do what you have decided to do about them, and I have not asked what that is and I will not." The old man set the stick across his knees. "And you find the Princeps, one day, and you say, I know what the wall is. That is mine. That is the one thing I ask, and I have said I ask nothing, so I do not ask it. I say it. So it is said."

"So it's said."

The Censor coughed. It went on. When it stopped he looked at the blood on his hand and did not wipe it, and said, in the old tongue, with the endings right:

"The College comes down the Throat with the glass in the autumn of next year. I have counted it. It is the sixth year and they come every second and they came in the fourth and you held your breath for six hours and a young one with a clean robe frowned at his glass and wrote something, you told me, and it was nothing, because you were seventeen and holding a Tiro. You are twenty-eight now and you will be thirty by the autumn, and thirty is not a Tiro, and you will hold it for six hours or you will not, and I will not be here to tap you through it." He put his hand on the stick. "So there is a year. In a year you will know the Form the way you know the hand, and you will make a Master's seal with your palm the way you make a hook, and you will have Tremor to the width of a heart, and you will have the words, and you will have sixteen slabs in the place where you keep things, because I am going to make you read them until you can say every column, and then I am going to die, and you are going to go up the Throat."

"How?"

"I have not the faintest idea," the Censor said. "I drew the Pit. Nobody has ever left it. That is the one thing in this rock I did not draw, boy, and it is yours. Get up."

Jonah got up.

He'd been sitting. It did not matter. It was the whole of what the old man said, and he had a year of it left, and he stood on the rock with a fang that was not there and a hand that went where a man was going and a name that nobody remembered, and the stick came out of the dark, and he touched the old man's shoulder again, on the fourth fall, and the old man laughed, and coughed, and said, again.
