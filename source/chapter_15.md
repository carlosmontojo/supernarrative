# Chapter 15: Grade

"A number," the Censor said, "is a thing a Flamen writes on a glass so a man who can't count can feel he's counted something."

The stick came out of the dark before Jonah had finished sitting.

He got his forearm up. It caught him across the knuckles instead, and he swore and shook the hand out. The second cut came low. He stepped into it instead of back and took it on the hip instead of the knee. The third he blocked clean, stick on plate. The old man grunted and set the haft across his own knees.

"Better. That's the last one tonight." The Censor looked at him. "Sit down. Tonight there's no stick."

Jonah sat, knuckles stinging.

It was the hundred and thirtieth night, near enough; he'd stopped counting nights the way he'd stopped counting the dead, and kept them instead. The Cradle was warm. The lamp was turned to its thread. The old man had come out of the rock without the haft for once, and had sat. Out of his robe he took not a slab but a shard, a thumb of the Vein, dark, and set it on the rock between them with the lamp behind it so it threw a shadow.

"You've been read four times," the Censor said. "Once by a dock wand, three times by glass. You know what the glass says. Grade one. Void. Wobble. You don't know what it means, because nobody's ever needed to tell a Collar. Tonight I tell you. You won't like it. By the end you'll understand why I hid a box in a ship rather than open it." He touched the shard. "This is a piece of the wall. What's in you is a piece of the wall. The difference is that this one was cut off, and yours was cut off too, and yours came back with bread."

---

He drew it on the rock with a chisel point, in the old tongue, and Jonah read it as he went, because he could read it now.

"Every lorica in the Empire is read on five lines. The Flamens have Latin for them. I'll give you the Latin and the plain word. Use the plain word in your head and the Latin in your mouth, because that's what a Broadstripe does, and you're going to be one." The chisel scratched. "Virtus. Force. How hard the plated body strikes and lifts and holds. Celeritas. Speed. How fast it moves under the plate. Firmitas. Hold. How much it takes before it sheds. Acies. Sense. What the bearer sees and hears and feels through it." He stopped scratching. "And Ingenium. Wit. The genius itself. How much it can learn. How fast it settles once it's eaten. How clever its fang is."

"Five lines."

"Five lines. The number's the sum of them, near enough, and the number's the grade. That's what the glass says, what the law caps, and what men brag about on the floor." He looked at the shard. "A Blank's cutting is invested at one. A good one, drilled, reaches nine and stops. At nine a Flamen puts his thumb on the mark and says a word and fuses it. You felt that. On Anvil. The thumb."

"It came down on nothing."

"Yes. Nothing there to fuse. On a cutting, the word puts a ceiling in the genius, and it can't grow past it, ever. The man carries that ceiling in his arm the rest of his life." A line, scratched across the rock. "Nine. Tiro. A citizen. That's Decimus, who's seven, and was offered nine, and said it wasn't worth the trim. You'll understand why in a moment. Nine's a plate that stops a knife, a fall, a fang the length of a hand. That's what the Republic gives a citizen for his oath."

Another line, higher.

"Thirty-nine. Principal. The equestrian order. Vetch's captain, Rennick, Tullus, your decurion. Fused at investiture. That's a plate that stops a rifle, a fang a forearm long, and one man who kills ten Tiros without hurrying. Tullus is thirty-eight. He's been thirty-eight fifteen years. He'll die thirty-eight, one below the ceiling, and he knows it. It's why he's what he is."

Another line.

"Fifty-nine. A licence. A Thinstripe on military licence has his fuse lifted to fifty-nine for the length of it. He goes to the far Limes, kills things, comes back a Centurion. And when the licence ends," the voice didn't change, "a Flamen says a different word, and everything above thirty-nine burns out of him. Not taken. Burned. It's called a trim. It takes a day, and men scream through the whole of it. After, they're thirty-nine again, and they remember what fifty-two felt like, every hour, for the rest of their lives. Tullus was trimmed once. Sabinus twice. You've seen his arm."

"Two white lines."

"Above and below the stripe. Once for each licence, lifted and burned twice. He holds forty-five now, a third licence, for the war. When it ends he'll be trimmed a third time, and he knows exactly what that will feel like. There's nothing in this rock he wouldn't do to a Collar to put it off a day." The chisel went down. "That's a Centurion. That's what's standing between you and the Throat."

He looked at the lines.

"Above that there's no fuse. Above that's the broad stripe. Sixty's a Tribune, a Broadstripe heir on the day he takes the house lorica, and he's done nothing for it but be born. A fang a sword long. A genius three hundred years in the family with ten gifts learned in his great-grandfather's duels. Ninety's a Legate. Thirty men alive. Above ninety the record stops. Above a hundred and twenty there's one man. He holds the Wall, and nobody's seen him fight in forty years."

Jonah looked at the lines on the rock.

"So the Empire," he said slowly, "is a family heirloom."

"Yes. And you're the cloth they polish it with."

---

"Gifts." The old man drew a column and wrote beside it. "A gift is something the genius has learned that isn't plate and isn't fang. Three ways it grows, and you know two of them, because I've made you do both." He tapped the rock. "Drill. Bring the plate up on the back of one hand thirty times in the dark and the genius gets bigger, a little, and if you do it a year it might learn something. Nobody gets to Tribune on drill."

"Strain."

"The genius learns when its bearer's near death and doesn't die. That's why cadets are sent to the far Limes at twenty, and why the Ludi are fought with live plate. You've been near death five times since the derelict, by my count, and it's learned from every one. You told the rock what it felt like, and it felt like a lock going over. That's strain. It can't be faked. The genius knows."

"And the third."

The Censor was quiet a moment.

"Spolia. Spoils. What you did to your decurion in the hold, and to a guard in the seventh chamber. A genius eats another genius and keeps about a fifth of what it ate. A Thinstripe of thirty who spoils one of forty walks off it thirty-eight. Fastest way up there is, and it's the whole of the Law of Spoils, and why there's a floor." He looked at Jonah. "You didn't take a fifth. You took your decurion whole, and the guard, and a mouthful of the wall itself, which nobody's done, because nobody's had anything in them that wasn't made small."

"Made small."

"The Wit line. You felt the cutting on Anvil and called it stupid. That was one on the Wit line, and it would have been one its whole life, because the Flamens cut the Wit out of every piece before they give it away. A Blank's genius is one. A Thinstripe's is two, three. A great house's, ten gifts, is four, five at the most, because it was made small in the year one hundred, and nothing made small grows back." The pale eyes were steady. "A genius holds as many gifts as its Wit line, and one more. That's why a Blank has two, and a great house has ten and no more. It's had ten a century, and will have ten when the wall is gone."

He looked at the shard.

"Yours wasn't made small. I don't know what its Wit line is. I don't think it has one, not by the glass's measure. I think that when it went through your arm on the derelict and stopped at the old break in your wrist to look, it was doing something no lorica's done in four hundred years, which is think. I think it's been doing it every hour since. That's why it turned around at a door for forty names, and why it ate its mother. It's why the glass on Anvil said dark, and the glass in the quaestor's house said wobble, and the glass at the Throat said void. Every one of them was reading something it had no line for."

Jonah looked at his hands.

"What's my number?"

"I don't know."

"Guess."

The Censor almost smiled. "You've a decurion of twenty-four in you whole, a guard of eight, a Blank's cutting of one, a mouthful of the wall, a hundred and thirty nights of drill and five of strain. If you were a cutting I'd say eighteen and be nearly right. You're not a cutting. So I'll tell you what I told the rock: the number tells you how hard they hit. The gift tells you where. And the Form tells you when." He picked up the chisel. "You've no gifts yet. That's next."

---

"You have plate," the old man said. "Everybody has plate. You have a fang, out of the wrong arm, crooked, which I've not let you use because a man who reaches for the fang hasn't learned the hand. You have something you do with your breath that keeps a glass from seeing you. The College calls it Hush when a magistrate does it to a prisoner, and has no name for it when a man does it to himself. You invented it on a dock the first week, because nobody told you it couldn't be done." He set the chisel down, point toward Jonah. "And you have this. Not a gift. What the fifth Form was built for. I'll show you once, tonight, and not again till you can do the four."

He nodded at the chisel.

"Pick it up. Bring the fang out. Only the fang."

Jonah picked up the chisel left-handed and brought the fang out of the back of his right wrist.

It came crooked. It always did. A hand's length of dark glass, bent like a bad knife, out of the wrong side of the arm. The genius, never once asked for it before, brought it up with something close to eagerness, and held it, and waited.

"Every fang in the Empire comes out of the inside of the forearm, straight. That's the shape cut into every piece of the wall before the Flamens give it away. A Vibian fang's a spike. A Claudian's a cleaver. A Fulvian's a whip's length of edge. Shapes cut into the genius, same as the ceiling. It can no more make another shape than it can grow past nine." He looked at the crooked glass on the back of Jonah's wrist. "Yours wasn't cut. It came out of the wrong arm because nobody told it which arm, and crooked because nobody told it straight. It can come out anywhere, in any shape, and the only reason it hasn't is you've never asked." He paused. "Ask it for a hook."

"A what?"

"A hook. Something to hang a bucket on. You've carried a bucket in each hand down that road a hundred and thirty nights and your fingers are raw. Ask it."

Jonah looked at the fang.

He didn't know how to ask. He'd asked it for plate, for stillness, for the names it now expected, and it had learned all of that. He'd never asked it to be something different.

He thought, a hook. He thought of the bucket handle biting into his fingers, and what would take the handle instead of his hand. He held the thought out to the genius the way you'd hold out a bowl.

The fang moved.

It didn't go in and come out. It bent. He watched it on the back of his own wrist, curling slowly back toward the arm until it wasn't a point anymore. A hook, a hand long. It stopped, and held. The genius sat behind his heart with its flat total interest and looked at what it had made. He'd swear to this: it was pleased with itself.

Jonah looked at the hook on his wrist and laughed.

It wasn't the laugh from the Cradle. It was the other one, from the Deep road, the night the rock said LATE. The Censor heard it. Something crossed the old man's face in the lamp, the grief with something else under it.

"Yes," the Censor said, very quietly. "That's what I hid in a box."

---

"Now put it away, and come with me. I want to show you what a hook's for."

He got up and went to the back wall of the Cradle and didn't open it. He went past it, to the mouth of the gallery, and out onto the Deep road. Jonah followed. For a hundred and thirty nights he'd only seen the Censor inside the Cradle. Now the old man walked the open road, thin and straight in his rag of a robe, his shaved head catching the lamplight. Sixty men and a garrison believed this road ended in a sealed door with nothing behind it.

"Ulli," the old man said, in passing, to the shape on the bucket at the road's end.

Ulli opened his eyes. He looked at the old man walking past him for the first time in six years. He didn't get up, and didn't speak. He closed his eyes again, and his lips moved, and it was the song.

The steel door was two hundred paces on. No handle. No slot. A dead lamp beside it. The Censor stopped in front of it and put his hand flat on the steel.

"Every month Tullus's men come down this road with a lamp and a key. They hand me the lamp, take the slate I've written the numbers on, lock it, and go. Twenty years. I couldn't open it from the inside with a key, because the lock's on this side. I haven't stood on this side of it since the day they put me through." He took his hand away. "I built the tunnel instead. Took nine years."

He looked at Jonah.

"Open it."

"There's no keyhole."

"There's a keyhole. Under the plate, here, where the lock is. Tullus's men lift the plate with a tool and put in a key. You've no tool and no key." He stepped back. "You've something that can be any shape you ask it. Ask it for the one that fits."

Jonah put his right hand flat on the steel, where the old man had touched it.

He felt the lock. He hadn't known he could. The genius went out through his palm, reading, same as it had into Varro's arm. It found the steel: dead, nothing. Under the steel, a mechanism, pins and a bar. It went through the shape of it all at once, same as the transport in the rings, and knew where the furniture was.

The fang came out of his palm.

Not his wrist. His palm. Thin, thinner than a chisel point. It went into the seam of the plate over the lock and lifted it, and under, into the keyhole. He felt it find the first pin and lift it, then the second, then the third. He felt the bar, a hinge that only went one way. He thought, turn, and it turned.

The door moved.

It didn't open. It moved a finger's width in its frame, with a sound that went down the Deep road like a bell. Jonah pulled the fang back into his hand so fast it hurt. He stood with his heart going and looked down the road toward Ulli, the buckets, the lamps.

Nothing came.

"A finger's width," the Censor said, beside him. "In a hundred and thirty nights. I've been a finger's width from this road for twenty years." He put his hand on the door and pushed. It swung in, on hinges oiled by Tullus's men every month for two decades, and behind it was the Chamber.

---

It was smaller than the Cradle.

That was the first thing Jonah saw, in the light of the old man's lamp: a cut in the rock the size of two rooms, a shelf for sleeping, a slab for everything else. Leaning against the rock in rows, sixteen slabs of the Vein cut thin and dark, each covered edge to edge in a small even hand. A bowl. A bucket. In the far corner, low, the mouth of the tunnel, and a chisel worn to half its length propped beside it. Nothing else.

"Twenty years," the old man said. He stood in the doorway of his own cell. "I've tapped to dying men through that wall. Written on those. Dug with that. Once a month a Thinstripe has stood where you're standing and handed me a lamp." He went to the slabs and touched the nearest one. "I was Master of the College of Flamens. Four hundred men under me. A house on Latium with a room for my books larger than this Pit. I'm going to die here, and it's correct that I should. Look at this room and understand that, so when you leave it you don't carry me with you like a debt."

Jonah stood in the doorway.

"Why is it correct?"

The Censor didn't answer that. He turned from the slabs and looked at Jonah in the lamp, considering, finding out where the weight was.

"Because I built the box," he said, "and I'm in it. That's enough for tonight. You've opened a door with your hand that Tullus's men need a tool and a key for. You've made a hook out of a fang because I asked. You understand what a fuse is, why a man would refuse a licence, and why another would kill for one. That's the fourth of it, and the fifth. Go and carry your buckets." He came to the door. "Close this behind you. With the hand. I want to hear it lock from the inside, once, so I know the sound."

Jonah went out onto the road.

He put his hand on the steel and asked. The fang found the pins, found the bar, and turned it back. The door rang the same note. The old man, on the other side, in the dark, said nothing at all.

He picked up the buckets. His fingers were raw. He thought about a fang that could be a hook, and a thumb coming down on a mark and finding nothing to fuse. He thought about the number the old man didn't know and the glass couldn't say. It was a ceiling that wasn't there.

He'd thought he was carrying something, since the derelict. Something out of a box.

He was wrong. It was a door that had never been shut, in a house where every other door had been. He was standing on the wrong side of a great many doors, and he'd just learned he could open them with his hand.

He walked down the road with the buckets and didn't need the hook, because his fingers were raw and he wanted them to be. Somewhere behind him an old man sat down on a shelf and listened to a lock he'd built, and didn't sleep.
