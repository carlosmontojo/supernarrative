# Chapter 15: Grade

"A number," the Censor said, "is a thing a Flamen writes on a glass so that a man who cannot count can feel he has counted something. Sit down. Tonight there is no stick."

Jonah sat down.

It was the hundred and thirtieth night, or near it; he'd stopped counting nights the way he'd stopped counting the dead, and kept them instead. The Cradle was warm. The lamp was turned to its thread. And the old man had come out of the rock without the haft for the first time since the first night, and had sat, and had taken out of his rag of a robe not a slab but a shard, a thumb of the Vein, dark, and had set it on the rock between them with the lamp behind it so that it threw a shadow.

"You have been read four times," the Censor said. "Once by a dock wand, three times by glass. You know what the glass says. Grade one. Void. Wobble. You do not know what it means, because nobody has ever told you, because nobody has ever needed to tell a Collar. So. Tonight I tell you. You will not like it. You will like it less the more of it you understand, and by the end you will understand why I hid a box in a ship rather than open it." He touched the shard. "This is a piece of the wall. What is in you is a piece of the wall. The difference between them is that this one has been cut off and yours has been cut off and yours came back with bread."

---

He drew it on the rock with the point of a chisel, the way he wrote the slabs, in the old tongue, and Jonah read it as he went, because he could read it now.

"Every lorica in the Empire is read on five lines," the old man said. "The Flamens have Latin for them. I will give you the Latin and the plain word and you will use the plain word in your head and the Latin in your mouth, because that is what a Broadstripe does and you are going to be one." The chisel scratched. "Virtus. Force. How hard the plated body strikes and lifts and holds. Celeritas. Speed. How fast the plate comes and the body moves under it. Firmitas. Hold. How much the plate takes before it sheds. Acies. Sense. What the bearer sees and hears and feels through it, including other loricae. And Ingenium." He stopped scratching. "Wit. The genius itself. How much it can learn. How many things it can do. How fast it settles when it has eaten. How clever its fang is."

"Five lines."

"Five lines, and the number is the sum of them, near enough, and the number is the grade, and the grade is what the glass says and what the law caps and what men brag about on the floor." The Censor looked at the shard. "A Blank's cutting is invested at one. A good one, drilled, reaches nine and stops, because at nine the Flamen who invested him put his thumb on the mark and said a word and fused it. You felt that. On Anvil. The thumb."

"It came down on nothing."

"Yes. Because there was nothing there to fuse. But on a cutting, the word puts a ceiling in the genius, and the genius cannot grow past it, ever, and the man carries that ceiling in his arm for the rest of his life, and it is nine." The chisel scratched a line across the rock. "Nine. Tiro. That is a citizen. That is Decimus, who is seven and was offered nine and said it was not worth the trim, and I will tell you what a trim is in a moment and you will understand why he said it. Nine is a plate that stops a knife and a fall and a fang the length of a hand. That is what the Republic gives a citizen for his oath."

Another line, higher.

"Thirty-nine. Principal. That is the equestrian order. Vetch's captain, Rennick, Tullus, your decurion. Fused at thirty-nine, at investiture, by a Flamen's thumb, and it is a plate that stops a rifle, and a fang a forearm long, and one of them kills ten Tiros without hurrying. Tullus is thirty-eight. He has been thirty-eight for fifteen years. He will die thirty-eight, one below the ceiling, and he knows it, and it is why he is what he is."

Another line.

"Fifty-nine. That is a licence. A Thinstripe on military licence has his fuse lifted to fifty-nine for the length of the licence, and he goes to the far Limes and kills things and comes back a Centurion, and when the licence ends," the old man's voice did not change, "a Flamen puts the thumb on the mark and says a different word, and everything above thirty-nine is burned out of him. Not taken. Burned. It is called a trim. It takes a day and men scream through the whole of it and afterward they are thirty-nine again and they remember, every hour of every day, what fifty-two felt like. Tullus was trimmed once. Sabinus twice. You saw his arm."

"Two white lines."

"Above and below the stripe. That is where the thumb goes. Once for each licence. He has had it lifted twice and burned twice and he holds forty-five now on a third, for the war, and when the war ends he will be trimmed a third time, and he knows exactly what it will feel like, and there is nothing in this rock he would not do to a Collar to put it off a day." The Censor set the chisel down. "That is a Centurion. That is what is standing between you and the Throat."

He looked at the rock, at the lines.

"And above that there is no fuse," he said. "Above that there is the broad stripe. Sixty is a Tribune. That is a Broadstripe heir on the day he takes the house lorica, and he has done nothing for it but be born, and it is a plate that fights in vacuum and a fang a sword long and a genius that has been in his family for three hundred years and has ten gifts in it that it learned in duels his great-grandfather fought. Ninety is a Legate. Thirty men alive. The heads of the great houses. Above ninety the record stops, and above a hundred and twenty there is one man, and he holds the Wall, and nobody has seen him fight in forty years."

Jonah looked at the lines on the rock.

"So the Empire," he said slowly, "is a family heirloom."

"Yes," the Censor said. "And you are the cloth they polish it with."

---

"Gifts," the old man said.

He'd drawn a new thing on the rock, a column, and was writing beside it.

"A gift is a thing the genius has learned to do that is not plate and not fang. It learns it one of three ways, and the three ways are the three ways it grows, and you know two of them already because I have made you do them." He tapped the rock. "Drill. You bring the plate up on the back of one hand thirty times in the dark and the genius gets, by a little, bigger, and if you do it for a year it may learn a thing. That is one. Nobody gets to Tribune on drill."

"Strain."

"Strain. The genius learns when its bearer is near death and does not die. That is why Broadstripe cadets are sent to the far Limes at twenty, and why the Ludi are fought with live plate, and why a man who has never been in a real fight is never above thirty however long he drills. You have been near death, by my count, five times since the derelict, and your genius has learned from every one of them, and you told the rock what it felt like and it felt like a lock going over. That is strain. It cannot be faked. The genius knows."

"And the third."

The Censor was quiet for a moment.

"Spolia," he said. "Spoils. What you did to your decurion in a hold and to a guard in the seventh chamber. A genius eats another genius and takes, in the ordinary way, about a fifth of what it ate. A Thinstripe of thirty who spoils a Thinstripe of forty on the floor walks off it thirty-eight. That is the fastest way up there is, and it is the whole of the Law of Spoils, and it is the reason there is a floor." He looked at Jonah. "You did not take a fifth. You took your decurion whole. And the guard. And a mouthful of the wall itself, which nobody has ever done, because nobody has ever had a thing in them that was not made small."

"Made small."

"The Wit line. Ingenium. You felt the cutting on Anvil; you called it stupid. It was one on the Wit line, and it would have been one for its whole life, because the Flamens cut the Wit out of every piece before they give it to a man. A Blank's genius is one. A Thinstripe's is two, perhaps three. A Broadstripe's house genius, three hundred years old, ten gifts, is four, five at the most, because it was made small in the year one hundred and nothing that is made small grows back." The old man's pale eyes were very steady. "And a genius holds as many gifts as its Wit line, and one more. That is why a Blank has two. That is why a great house has ten and no more, and has had ten for a century, and will have ten when the wall is gone."

He looked at the shard on the rock.

"Yours was not made small," he said. "I do not know what its Wit line is. I do not think it has one, in the sense the glass means. I think that when it went through your arm on the derelict and stopped at the old break in your wrist to look, it was doing a thing that no lorica in the Empire has done in four hundred years, which is think, and I think it has been doing it every hour since, and I think that is why it turned around at a door for forty names, and why it ate its mother, and why the glass on Anvil said dark and the glass in the quaestor's house said wobble and the glass at the Throat said void and every one of them was reading a thing it had no line for."

Jonah looked at his hands.

"What's my number?" he said.

"I do not know."

"Guess."

The Censor almost smiled. "You have a decurion of twenty-four in you whole, and a guard of eight, and a Blank's cutting of one, and a mouthful of the wall, and a hundred and thirty nights of drill and five of strain. If you were a cutting I would say eighteen and I would be nearly right. You are not a cutting. I will tell you what I told the rock and it is the only honest answer: the number tells you how hard they hit. The gift tells you where. And the Form tells you when." He picked up the chisel. "And you have no gifts yet. That is the next thing."

---

"You have plate," the old man said. "Everybody has plate. You have a fang, which comes out of the wrong arm, crooked, and which I have not let you use in a hundred and thirty nights because a man who reaches for the fang has not learned the hand. You have a thing you do with your breath that keeps a glass from seeing you, which the College calls Hush when a magistrate does it to a prisoner and has no name for when a man does it to himself, and which you invented on a dock in the first week because nobody told you it could not be done." He set the chisel on the rock, point toward Jonah. "And you have this. Which is not a gift. Which is the thing the fifth Form was built for, and which I am going to show you tonight, once, and then not again until you can do the four."

He nodded at the chisel.

"Pick it up. Bring the fang out. Only the fang."

Jonah picked up the chisel in his left hand and brought the fang out of the back of his right wrist.

It came crooked. It always did. A length of dark glass a hand long, bent like a bad knife, from the wrong side of the arm, and the thing under his ribs, which had not been asked for it in a hundred and thirty nights, brought it with something that was almost eagerness and held it there and waited.

"Every fang in the Empire," the Censor said, "comes out of the inside of the forearm, straight, because that is the shape the Flamens cut into every piece of the wall before they give it away. A Vibian fang is a spike. A Claudian is a cleaver. A Fulvian is a whip's length of edge. They are shapes. They were cut into the genius the way the ceiling was, and the genius can no more make another shape than it can grow past nine." He looked at the crooked glass on the back of Jonah's wrist. "Yours was not cut. It came out of the wrong arm because nobody told it which arm. It came out crooked because nobody told it straight. It can come out of anywhere you like, in any shape you like, and the only reason it has not is that you have never asked." He paused. "Ask it for a hook."

"A what?"

"A hook. Something to hang a bucket on. You have carried a bucket in each hand down that road for a hundred and thirty nights and your fingers are raw. Ask it for a hook."

Jonah looked at the fang.

He didn't know how to ask. He'd asked it for plate on the back of his hand and it had learned to give that before he finished asking; he'd asked it to be still and it had learned that; he'd said names to it and it had learned to expect them. He had never asked it to be a different thing than it was.

He thought, a hook. He thought it the way he thought in the old tongue now, slowly, on purpose. He thought of the bucket handle, the way it bit into his fingers on the Deep road, and of what a thing would look like that took the handle instead, and he held the thought out to the thing under his ribs the way you'd hold out a bowl.

The fang moved.

It did not go in and come out. It bent. He watched it, on the back of his own wrist, in the thread of lamplight, a length of dark glass that had been a bad knife, and it bent, slowly, the way the ring had gone under his skin, the point curling back toward the arm until it was not a point, it was a curve, a hook, a hand long, and it stopped, and held, and the thing under his ribs, which had done it, sat behind his heart with that flat total interest and looked at what it had done and was, he would swear to this, pleased with itself.

Jonah looked at the hook on his wrist and laughed.

It was not the laugh from the Cradle. It was the other one, the one from the Deep road when the rock said LATE, and the Censor, across from him, heard it, and something went across the old man's face in the lamp that was the thing from the first night, the grief with the other thing under it.

"Yes," the Censor said, very quietly. "That. That is what I hid in a box."

---

"Now put it away," the old man said, "and come with me, because I want to show you what a hook is for."

He got up. He went to the rock at the back of the Cradle and did not open it; he went past it, to the mouth of the gallery, and out onto the Deep road, and Jonah followed him, and it was the first time in a hundred and thirty nights he had seen the Censor outside the Cradle, on the road, under the lamps, thin and straight in his rag of a robe with his shaved head catching the light, walking down a road that sixty men and a garrison believed had a sealed door at the end of it and nothing behind it.

"Ulli," the old man said, in passing, to the shape on the bucket at the road's end.

Ulli opened his eyes. He looked at the old man walking past him for the first time in six years, and did not get up, and did not speak, and closed his eyes again, and his lips moved, and it was the song.

The steel door was two hundred paces on.

It was as Jonah had seen it on the first night of the haul. Flush in the rock. No handle. No slot. A lamp beside it, unlit. The Censor stopped in front of it and put his hand flat on the steel and said, "Every month Tullus's men come down this road with a lamp and a key and open this, and hand me the lamp, and take the slate I have written the numbers on, and lock it, and go. Twenty years. I could not open it from the inside if I had a key, because the lock is on this side, and I have not stood on this side of it since the day they put me through." He took his hand away. "I built the tunnel instead. It took nine years. There was nothing else to do with the years."

He looked at Jonah.

"Open it."

Jonah looked at the door.

"There's no keyhole."

"There is a keyhole. It is under the plate, here, where the lock is. Tullus's men lift the plate with a tool and put in a key. You have no tool and no key." The old man stepped back. "You have a thing that can be any shape you ask it. Ask it for the one that fits."

Jonah put his right hand flat on the steel where the old man had touched it.

He felt the lock. He had not known he could. The thing under his ribs went out through his palm the way it had gone into Varro's arm, reading, and it found the steel, and the steel was steel, dead, nothing, and under the steel there was a shape, a mechanism, a thing of pins and a bar, and the thing under his ribs went through the shape the way it had gone through the legion transport in the rings, all of it at once, and knew where the furniture was.

The fang came out of his palm.

Not his wrist. His palm. It came out of the middle of his hand, where he had it flat on the steel, and it was not a fang and it was not a hook; it was thin, thinner than a chisel point, and it went into the seam of the plate that covered the lock and lifted it, and went under, and into the keyhole, and he felt it go, felt it find the first pin and lift it and the second and the third, felt the bar the way he'd felt Varro's wrist, a hinge that only went one way, and he thought, turn, and it turned.

The door moved.

It did not open. It moved, a finger's width, in its frame, with a sound that went down the Deep road like a bell, and Jonah pulled the fang back into his hand so fast that it hurt and stood there with his heart going and looked down the road toward Ulli and the buckets and the lamps.

Nothing came.

"A finger's width," the Censor said, beside him. "In a hundred and thirty nights. I have been a finger's width from this road for twenty years." He put his hand on the door and pushed, and it swung in, on hinges that had been oiled by Tullus's men every month for two decades, and behind it was the Chamber.

---

It was smaller than the Cradle.

That was the first thing Jonah saw, from the road, in the light of the old man's lamp. A cut in the rock the size of his father's two rooms, with a shelf along one wall for sleeping and a slab along the other for everything else, and on the slab, in rows, leaning against the rock, sixteen slabs of the Vein cut thin, dark, each one covered edge to edge in a small even hand. There was a bowl. There was a bucket. There was, in the far corner, low, a hole in the rock the size of a man stooping, and the hole was the tunnel, and beside it, propped, was a chisel worn to half its length, and Jonah understood that the tunnel had been dug with that.

There was nothing else.

"Twenty years," the old man said. He was standing in the doorway of his own cell looking in at it the way a man looks at a room he has just been told is a room. "I have tapped to dying men through that wall and I have written on those and I have dug with that, and once a month a Thinstripe has stood where you are standing and handed me a lamp." He stepped in. He went to the slabs and touched the nearest one, the way he'd touched the shard. "I was Master of the College of Flamens. I had four hundred men under me and a house on Latium with a room in it for my books that was larger than this Pit, and I am going to die here, and it is correct that I should, and I want you to look at this room and understand that it is correct, so that when you leave it you do not carry me with you like a debt."

Jonah stood in the doorway.

"Why is it correct?"

The Censor did not answer that. He turned from the slabs and looked at Jonah in the lamp, and the look was the one from the first night, considering, finding out where the weight was.

"Because I built the box," he said, "and I am in it. That is enough for tonight. You have opened a door with your hand that Tullus's men need a tool and a key for, and you have made a hook out of a fang because I asked you to, and you have understood what a fuse is and why a man would refuse a licence and why another would kill for one. That is the fourth thing and the fifth. Go and carry your buckets." He came to the door. "And close this behind you. With the hand. I want to hear it lock from the inside, once, so that I know the sound."

Jonah went out onto the road.

He put his hand on the steel and asked, and the fang came out of his palm thin and went under the plate and found the pins and the bar, and he turned it back, and the door said the thing it said, and the old man, on the other side of it, in the dark, in the room he had built, said nothing at all.

He picked up the buckets. His fingers were raw. He stood for a moment on the Deep road with the lamps too far apart and thought about a hook, and about a fang that could be a hook, and about a Flamen's thumb coming down on a mark and finding nothing to fuse, and about a wall that was one thing and could not stop a piece of itself, and about the number, which the old man did not know and the glass could not say and which was, he understood, not a number at all. It was a ceiling that was not there.

He'd thought he had a thing in him. He'd thought that since the derelict. A thing from a box.

It was not a thing. It was a door that had never been shut, in a house where every other door had been, and he was standing on the wrong side of a great many doors, and he had just learned that he could open them with his hand.

The hunger, behind his heart, was not far from full. He knew the size of it. He walked down the road with the buckets and did not need the hook, because his fingers were raw and he wanted them to be, and somewhere in the rock behind him, in a room the size of two rooms, an old man sat down on a shelf and listened to a lock he had built, and did not sleep.
