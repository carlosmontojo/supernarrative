# Chapter 40: Counted by Head

The Heron came off Anvil at the second bell, and Jonah stood at the rail of her hold with the lid of cask thirty-one open beside him and watched the rock go.

It went the way rock goes. Slowly, and then not. The lower docks first, the ramp he had come down in a good coat and gone up in a hood, and the gate with twelve boys on it reading arms, and then the tenement stairs, the south yard's among them, with a door at the top of one that had been left open, and then Founding Square with its columns, and the melt-house chimney going on the north side the way it had gone for ninety years, and then the whole of it, the yard-world, with the hull ring above it catching a sun he had seen for three days, and the ring hung there the way it had hung the morning he flew out on a Collar barge with an old captain saying nothing, and nothing around it had changed, and everything in it had.

He felt it go. That was new. He had flown out of Anvil forty times and had never once felt it through his boots, because he had had boots and a deck and nothing under his ribs but a heart, and he stood at the rail now with his hand flat on the Vein's glass of an open cask and felt the rock's thousands of hearts go faint and fainter through the hull, the way the Deep's sixty had gone when the hopper cage went up the Throat, until there was one left, and it was slow, and it was his.

"You're counted," Pike said, behind him.

Jonah turned round.

The old Halyard had a lamp and no paper. He had never once had paper. He stood at the foot of the ladder with the light on his beard and looked at the man by the cask the way he had looked at a backward mark, a long time, and said, "Ten. Yara counts heads when we lift. Not names. Halyards don't write names; the Empire's got enough of those. She counts heads on the deck and says the number and if it's the number we go, and if it's not we don't, and it was ten." He looked at the cask. "Nine's the crew. You're the tenth. She said it with the rest. Counted by head."

"Counted by head."

"That's what a Halyard has." Pike set the lamp on a cask. "It's not much."

Jonah stood at the rail with his hand on the glass and could not, for a moment, find the door of the place where he kept things, and then found it, and put that in with the rest, where there was room.

---

He would learn, a year later, in the City, from a small man with a grain-sack on his shoulder and a clerk's smile, exactly how long it had taken.

Four days.

The Heron had been four days off Anvil, lying to a College dock about her casks, when a defrocked Flamen with three shards on a cracked slab took the pen out of his robe for the last time and used it. He wrote what he had said. He wrote it in the voice for the slab, the old careful one, the way a Flamen finishes: unlicensed, Anvil, lower docks; bearer unknown; lorica present; genius unregistered; grade indeterminate, lines will not hold still; Force nine, Speed ten, Hold seven, Sense nine, Wit no value returned; Plate, Blade, Hush turned in; Seal and Reach malformed; four unrecognized. He wrote the remark. Do not repeat this reading. And then, beneath it, in the same hand, he repeated it: a hood, a Halyard's grey, a struck citizen's line on the right arm, a Belt turn in the voice that was learned and not born, two bodyguards left alive, three shards paid, and a Halyard skimmer on the lower docks whose captain ran Hush turned in, the only other arm in eleven years that had read that way.

He sold it for his robe.

Not for a senator's seat; the grain-men did not give those to defrocked Flamens. For his name off the list, and a house in the City that he never lived in, and the right to be called Flamen again by men who did not mean it, and it went up the ring in the courier packet with the province's post and arrived on a desk in the City nineteen days after a quaestor had written to his father in the subjunctive, and the small man read it twice, the way he read everything, and took a file out of a drawer that had three pages in it, and made a fourth.

Jonah would learn that in a year. He would learn it across a dinner table, from the man himself, over the fish, with a broad stripe on his own arm that a thing had drawn there and a woman with a grey book on the other side of the table who had said, once, unstable, and had been right. And he would think, hearing it: four days. The old man said. He said a small frightened greedy man who has seen you is the thing that ends you, and I stood over him with my boot off a Blank's wrist and did the sum and got it wrong on purpose, and it took four days.

And he would think: and I'd do it again. That's the thing I'll not tell the old man, on the road, at night, when I say his name. That's the line I keep.

But that was a year off, and he did not know it, and he stood in the Heron's hold with the lid of thirty-one open and the rock gone from under his hand and said the names.

---

He said them the way Decimus had read the Deep, from the top, all of them, in the voice for things that were not personal, except that every one of them was.

"Tacitus," he said.

It listened.

"Aulus Terentius Pharus. Censor. Master of the Flamens. He read every page and drew the Pit and could not draw the way out of it, and he taught a Collar on a rock with a stick for five years, and beat him, and laughed once, and coughed blood, and said the name of a house so it had been said. He wanted to save the Empire. He got me instead. He knew that. It's on the slab in my hand."

"Tom Vale. Anvil. Morrow's yard. Nine fingers. South yards. Believed in the ladder. Sold a set of numbers for a winter's food and knew, and never told me, and was happy the night I answered in Latin. Sat in a chair with the door open. Paid by the yard, the melt says. Paid by the man who wrote the letter, out of his own purse, because a good man made him. Mara said his name at the fire where you don't."

"Captain Ochoa. The Kestrel. Said nothing, which was the highest thing. Carried a seal for a senator and died on a decurion's fang over the fish course and said Vibius with the last of it. Morrow said his name right, once, at the melt, and didn't look at me."

"Decurion Varro. The Vigilant. Killed a man without breaking conversation. The first. His feet are in my legs and his contempt is not, anymore, and that took the old man a year."

He said the road. He said Corr, north yards, four years, said a month, a tree. He said Bricks and Hollen and Aulus of Marl and Tallow who was fifteen and Marcus who never used it and the Deep man written as unknown. He said Auger, who said his name to Ulli and who made a Centurion hurry with a second chisel. He said Vetch, who was a guard and was not written, and was now. He said Sorrel. He said the boy from the last transport whose name he did not know, and said so.

"Ullan of Marl. The east fields. Two l's. They always get it wrong. Nine years in a two-year Deep because he sang, and the wall listened and never finished. Carried forty-three. Forty-four. Chose the day. Sat down with his back to it on the eighteenth and stopped singing on the fourth verse of the fallow song, with Pell on one side and me on the other, and it was his. There was a thing he did on Marl and it was his and he kept it. He asked me, once, to take one. I took it."

"Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father." He said that first, the way he had been told to. "Then the rest. Guard on the Kestrel. Guard on the Throat. Said my number seven years and said please, the only one who did, and it was never personal, and I never once believed him. Signed for five more so a tree wouldn't die. Wrote me into the wall in the morning so the road could say so whichever way the night went. Came down slow with his rifle down to say the number first, and got as far as the breath. It's not on him. It's on me. He said write it right. This is right."

He said Gnaeus Sabinus, client of the Princeps' house, who said oh. He said the two boys at the road's end who did not run, and their bands a week old. He said Pollio, who frowned and wrote it down and was right, and Rennick, who wrote them light, and Harrow, who is in, and has less, and has a girl on Marl whose name is Ada and whose message is on a Halyard ship going the wrong way with a sister who owes Pike. He said Pell, north forty, never made quota, who sang the last verse to the wall with his chin up so it would know he was there. He said Tullus, who counted casks and not the men who packed them, and was a decent man, and would be a prefect with a letter beside his name, and he did not know yet what he would do about Tullus, and said so.

He said Kaspar, half a cutting, warm to the shoulder, one word of the old tongue. He said Dace, and the boy who said what.

"Bram. A liar. Everybody says. Knew me from six and looked into a hood from a hand's width and shut his mouth on it and said, bloody Halyards. He carried Corin home once. He said my father's name in the road to two boys with fangs."

"Ezra Morrow. Blank. Paid Collars like citizens and was poor. Made a purser pay for a fire to his face in a clean office and put it on his ledger as a gift to the yard, the only line he's not ashamed of. Told Mara I was alive. Has a daughter with a face that says what a thing is, out loud, in a room. Has a yard, for a while longer."

He said Gaspar Roake, with what he did, which was nothing, and with what he said, which was you've got his hands, and with what he had, which was a bottle, and was not much.

He said Corin Aldane, with the stripe and the quota and the word friend and a boy of three, and the face from the first sand.

He said Silas Marchand, purser, precise, and a bank.

He said Marcus Vibius Priscus, with what he had done, and he said it plainly and in order, the way the old man had said to say the men you are going to lie to, and when he came to the end of what the man had done he stopped, and did not say the other thing, and the thing in the doorway of him put the heart where it kept it, under the name it had heard him not say, and did not say it either.

He said Mara Ansel. He said the Halcyon.

He said the six hundred and forty-two from the slabs, every one, the way he had said them at the bottom of a well without a mouth, and the ones with a line that said there was a thing and it was theirs.

And then he said his own.

"Jonah Vale. Anvil. Morrow's yard. Purser's pilot. Citizen for a week. Four four seven one. Went into the wall on the nineteenth night of the harvest, the register says, in a guard's hand, and died in the Throat five years before that, another register says, in a quaestor's. That's in the book. That's a name in the book like the rest of them, and it stays, because the things with names stay." He put his hand flat on the open cask. "And the one who carries it is called something else now, and that's not in the book, because it's not a name, it's a stripe, and the old man said a stripe is a thing you wear and a name is a thing you are, and I've got both, and I know which is which."

He stopped.

"That's the count," he said. "It starts with you and it ends with me and there's room. There's always room. It's the only thing about a count that's kind."

---

The thing in the doorway did not do the small thing.

He waited for it. He had come to wait for it, at the end of the names, the way a man waits for a hand on his arm, and it did not come, and he stood in the hold of a Halyard ship with his hand on the Vein and felt it not come, and felt, instead, the other thing. The doorway thing. The deciding. It stood in the door of him the way it had stood on a slab at the Throat when a word came, and on a road when a fang came, and at the bottom of a well when a guard went through it, and it decided, and he felt it decide, and did not know what.

Then it spoke.

Not one word. He would keep that; he would keep it the way he kept the water step done right and the plate going in clean in the Kestrel's hold and a laugh on a rock that went on till it coughed. It was the second time it had spoken in four hundred years and it did not say the word it had. It said a thing it had heard, in the old tongue, in the middle of him, in the voice that was very old and had been used once and knew it, and the thing it said was a thing it had heard him say, on a rock and on a road and in a cask and in a hold, more times than any other thing, to it, when it did the hand with no hand and had no hand.

It said: I know.

Jonah stood very still.

Then he laughed. Once, out loud, in the hold, at the beam with the lamp on it, the way he had laughed on the floor of the Cradle when a thing had learned to hold him from its mother and stopped when he said when. Because it had learned to speak from him, the old man had said it would, it has no tongue but yours, and it had learned it, and the first sentence it had made out of the only speech it had was the one he had given it, every time, for a thing that could not be answered, and it had given it back.

"Yes," he said. "All right. Yes. You do."

Pike, at the foot of the ladder, said, to the lamp: "He's talking to it."

"He always has," Yara said, from the top of the ladder, where she had been standing for as long as it took a man to say six hundred and ninety names. "Pike. Come up. Leave him the lamp."

Pike came up.

Yara stood at the top of the ladder a moment longer with her hand on the rail and looked down into her hold at a man with a stripe on him that nobody could see yet, standing beside an open cask of the College's harvest with his hand on the Vein, and she did not say the obvious, because she had said it once, and a Halyard does not say a thing twice.

"Thirty days to the College dock, Sertorius," she said. "Learn your plate. Sleep in the cask. And say them somewhere I can hear, the next time. I'd like to know what they sound like from the top."

She went up. The hatch shut.

Jonah stood in the hold.

He stood with his hand on the glass and the lamp on the beam and the Heron going out into the Belt toward the Interior at the pace a Halyard ship goes when it is going somewhere, and he felt the hull through his palm, and the nine hearts above him, and the one in him, and the thing beside the one in him that had a name and had just said two words with it, and he thought about a woman on a stool with a grey book who had said unstable, and a small man with a grain-sack who had a file with three pages in it and would have four, and a City he had never seen where four hundred houses sat in a chamber with a wall behind them that said come to every cutting in the Empire and heard nothing, and a niche in the ninth row, third from the eastern wall, where a thing had been asleep for three hundred and ninety years that no glass could read.

"I'm not ready," he said. To the dark. In the old tongue. So it was said.

And the thing in the doorway of him, which had one word of its own and one sentence of his, and had just found out which it preferred, said it again.

I know.

---

He would learn the rest of it over the same fish, a year on, and would keep it with the four days. Nineteen days after the Heron lifted off Anvil, a courier packet from the Belt came across a desk in the City with the province's post, and a small man in a plain tunic with a grain-sack embroidered on the shoulder read a defrocked Flamen's paper twice, and took a file out of a drawer, and made a fourth page, and wrote on it, in a clerk's hand, under a reading that said do not repeat this reading:

Repeated. A Halyard skimmer, the Heron. Captain runs dark. Bound for the College dock at Quirinus Interior with thirty-eight casks and a story. Tenth head not on the slate.

And under that, because he was a man who decided what was true before he asked, and had read the Vigilant's report twice, and had never once had a thing go into a wall that he would have liked to see go into one:

He did not go into the wall.

He would hear that from the man's own mouth, with the woman across the table, and he would say, when he heard it, in the old tongue, with the endings right, the thing he had said to a Halyard bosun in a hold with a bowl of fish going cold in his hands, and would go on saying, on every road, at night, until the book was full and he was the last page:

That's the count. It's not much. It's what a man has.
