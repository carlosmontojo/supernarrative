# Chapter 34: Interlude. The Register

Marcus Vibius Priscus had known for nineteen days that his sister was going to ask him, and had prepared, in the manner in which he prepared for the Senate, by deciding what he would not say.

She came to the study at the hour when he read, which was the hour after the household had eaten and before the clients came, and she did not knock, which she had not done since she was six, and she had the book under her arm. Not the College's. Her own, the grey one, with the cord round it that she had tied herself since she was twelve because she did not trust the binding, and she set it on his desk beside the campaign lists and sat down in the chair the clients used and put her hands in her lap.

"Father has written to the Claudian," she said.

"I know."

"He has written to say that I accept, which I have not, and that the betrothal can be announced at the games, which it cannot, and that the house of Vibius is honoured, which it is, and I would like you to write to him tonight and say that his daughter has read the letter and declines." She said it the way she said everything, precisely, in order, and then the other thing, the thing he never did. "You can put it in the subjunctive if you like. He'll take it better from you."

Priscus put down the list.

"Drusus is a decent man."

"He is. I have nothing against him but that I did not choose him, and Father has chosen him for me anyway, because the Claudian house holds the Wall and the Vibian house would like to stand nearer it." She did not look away. "I'm not asking you to agree with me. I'm asking you to write the letter. You raised me. He'll listen to you."

He looked at her for a while.

She was twenty-five. He had been thirteen when she was born and had taught her the old tongue at four and the fang at nine, and had watched her, at nineteen, look down from a rail on Anvil at a Collar in the sand and say, he won, and had heard himself repeat it. There were several things he had not told her.

"I'll write to him," he said.

"Tonight."

"Tonight." He picked up the list again, to have something in his hands. "Was that all?"

"No," Livia said, and untied the cord on the book.

---

She did not open it at once. She sat with the cord loose in her fingers and looked at the shelf behind his head, where the province's papers were, six crates brought back from Anvil and never unpacked. She had gone through them, he thought, in the nineteen days. She had the face she had had at nine with a wooden fang, when she had lost a bout and had gone away and thought about it and come back.

"I was at Tessera in the spring," she said.

"I know. The Master's inspection. You wrote."

"I wrote that it was cold and that the Master is a fool." She turned a page without looking at it. "I did not write about the one who read void."

Priscus did not move.

"Three times. Pollio read him and frowned. The Master said void, and I read him, and he was void with a wobble, the way a thing reads that is holding very still on purpose, and I said unstable, in front of the prefect, which I will pay for at the College for some years." She looked at him. "A cut one. A white line on his arm where a citizen's line had been, six years on a road that takes cut ones in a year, answering a Flamen with his face doing nothing, and I knew him."

"Livia."

"I knew him the way you know a hand across a room. I asked the guard at the cage his intake name. The guard said, Vale, my lady. Citizen for a week. Off Anvil." She put her finger on the page. "Throat, four four seven one, reading unstable. That's what I wrote. In this book. Not the College's. I've read it every day for two months and I have not been able to say why I did not put it in the College's, and I know why, and I would like you to tell me why the boy from the yard on Anvil, whom you told me through a cell slot had been released and had gone home, was on a road at the bottom of a hole on Tessera with a number instead of a name."

He had prepared for this.

He had prepared for nineteen days, since the courier packet from the Belt had come across his desk and he had read a prefect's report of a struck Master living twenty-three years behind a door, a Centurion of the Princeps' house dead on a road, and a Collar who had been in a sealed cask and had gone, the report said, into the wall. He had read the number and had known it, because he had put it there. He had decided what he would not say, and it was everything, and so he said the other thing, in the voice he used for the law.

"He was released," Priscus said. "I signed the release myself, in the week after the festival, on the finding that the salvage was unreported but not unlawful, the vessel being founding-era and the ring being outside the claim. He was given his papers and his citizenship stood and he was told to leave Anvil, because a man who has been read at investiture and then arrested is a man the yards will not hire, and he left. Where he went after that was not the province's business. If he ended in a Pit under a number it was under some other magistrate's hand than mine."

His heart did nothing.

He had worn Aequitas for eighteen years and had spent them learning to say a thing so that his own heart said it too, because a magistrate with Pulse who could be read by Pulse was no magistrate, and he said the words to his sister with his heart flat under them, and watched her hear it.

She had no Pulse. She was a cutting. She had Sense and Scent and Lorica Sight, the scholar's gifts, and none of them read a heart, and she sat in the clients' chair with her finger on a page and read him anyway, with the thing she had, which was twenty-five years of his face.

"That was very good," she said.

He waited.

"You believed it while you said it. That's the trick. You told me once. You decide the thing is true and then you say it, and the heart goes along, because the heart's a fool." She closed the book. "I've never been able to do it. I lie and my heart tells everyone in the room. It's because I'm not you."

"Livia."

"There's a register in the third crate," she said. "From the Pit. A copy of the intake, sent to the quaestor of Anvil for the province's file. It has a line on it. Vale, Jonah. Died in the Throat, three hundred and eighty-nine." She said the year the way the College said a grade. "He died on arrival, according to the paper in your crate, and six years later I read him in a line, and a prefect's report in the Senate's post says a Collar with his number went into the wall this spring after he killed a Centurion with his hands." She stood up. "So he's dead twice. Once on a paper in your study, and once on a road. And you told me he went home."

He did not say anything.

She stood across the desk from him with the grey book under her arm and looked at him the way she had looked at a Collar in a line, twice, and longer the second time, and he understood that she was not going to ask again. She had asked once. That was his. She had learned it from him.

"I don't know what you did," Livia said, at last. "I know it was you, because the register's in your crate, and I know you did it for the house, because you have never once done a thing for any other reason." Her voice did not change. "I'll not ask what. I'll find out. That's what I do; it's the only thing Father let me be. And when I know, I'll come and tell you, and you'll decide whether it's true, and I'll watch you decide."

She went to the door.

"Write to Father tonight," she said. "About the Claudian. I meant that. It's the only thing I've asked you for in seven years that you can give me."

She went out.

---

The Frumentarius came the next morning, at the hour of the clients, and did not wait to be announced, because they did not.

He was a small man in a plain tunic with a grain-sack embroidered on the shoulder where a stripe would be on anyone else, and he sat in the clients' chair without being asked and put two papers on the desk on top of the campaign lists, and Priscus, who would stand for the praetorship next year and had never once been afraid of a man in a chair, made sure his heart said so.

"Senator."

"I'm not a senator yet. My father is."

"Your father is Gracchan," the Frumentarius said, pleasantly, "and you are not, which is why I've come to you and not to him. Quaestor, then. I'm Cominius. You'll not have heard of me; that's the office." He put a finger on the first paper. "This is the report of the cutter Vigilant, of the fourteenth day of the month, six years ago, in the rings of Quirinus. A boarding officer lost. Marines who came back speaking of a hostile lorica aboard a peregrine barge. The barge destroyed with all hands." He put a finger on the second. "This is the report of the prefect of Metalla VII, of this spring. A struck Master of the College, alive twenty-three years in a room. A Centurion of the Princeps' house, dead on a road, the report says by a Collar with his hands. A Collar who was in a sealed cask with a mark the Flamen could not close, and who told a College shackle no, the Flamen says, and it opened. And went, the prefect says, into the wall." He sat back. "I've read the first one twice, quaestor. I've read it twice because the barge that was destroyed with all hands came into Anvil's lower docks three days after it was destroyed, with a purser aboard who wrote a letter, and a pilot who was invested a citizen and then arrested and then went to Tessera under your hand with a number. And I've read the second one once, because once was enough."

Priscus looked at the two papers.

"What is it you want to know?"

"I want to know what a hostile lorica aboard a peregrine barge is," Cominius said. "My office has found three in a hundred years and cut all three. I've a file shut six years on a decurion lost in the rings, because a quaestor on Anvil closed the province's end of it very neatly, with a release he signed and a register that says the boy died on arrival. And a Flamen who says the Collar's reading was void three times, which is a word my office has for a thing it has found three times in a hundred years." He smiled. It was a clerk's smile. "I don't want to know what you did, quaestor. It's on your desk. I want to know what you saw. In the cell. You've Pulse. What did his heart do?"

Priscus had prepared for his sister. He had not prepared for this, and he found, sitting across his own desk from a small man with a grain-sack on his shoulder, that the thing he did in the Senate did not work on a man who had already decided what was true.

"Nothing," he said. "His heart did nothing. He said he didn't know what was in the box, and it was true. He said he hadn't got a lorica, and I said, is it in your possession, and he said no, and it was true." He heard himself say it. "I read a boy who was telling the truth about a thing he didn't understand, and I sent him to the Belt because of what he'd carried, not what he was, and I wrote him dead so that nobody would go looking for what he'd carried. That's what I saw. A boy whose heart did nothing."

"And the thing he carried."

"Was burned."

"By you."

"By me."

Cominius nodded, slowly, the way a clerk nods when a column has come out.

"Then here's what I'll take back to the office." He stood up and gathered the two papers. "Six years ago a boy came off a derelict with a thing in him that read void, and a quaestor with Pulse found his heart doing nothing, and sent him down a hole, and wrote him dead. And this spring a thing that reads void came up out of the same hole and killed a Centurion of the Princeps' house with its hands and went into a wall, the prefect says, on the word of a foreman who didn't follow." He tucked the papers under his arm. "I've never once, in twenty years, had a thing go into a wall that I'd have liked to see go into one. I'll be opening the file. Your name on the first page, because it was your hand; your father's on the second, because it was his seal; the boy's on the third, because he's the one I'm going to find." He went to the door. "Your sister read him at Tessera, I'm told. Unstable, she said. I'd like to talk to her. Not today."

He went out.

Priscus sat in the study with the campaign lists in front of him and the province's crates behind him and the third crate open, where she had left it, with a register in it that had one line he had written in a clerk's hand five years ago, in this room, at this desk, because it was the cleanest way, and he sat for a long time with his heart doing nothing at all.

Then he wrote to his father about the Claudian. He put it in the subjunctive.
