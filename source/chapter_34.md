# Chapter 34: Interlude. The Register

Livia did not knock. She had not knocked since she was six.

Priscus was at his desk in the hour after the household had eaten and before the clients came, with the campaign lists in front of him. He had known for nineteen days that she was going to come, and had prepared for it as he prepared for the Senate: by deciding what he would not say.

She had a book under her arm. Not the College's. Her own, the grey one, with the cord round it that she had tied herself since she was twelve, because she did not trust the binding. She set it on his desk beside the lists, sat in the clients' chair and put her hands in her lap.

"Father has written to the Claudian," she said.

"I know."

"He has written to say that I accept, which I have not. That the betrothal can be announced at the games, which it cannot. That the house of Vibius is honoured, which it is. I would like you to write to him tonight and say that his daughter has read the letter and declines." Precisely, in order, as she said everything. Then: "You can put it in the subjunctive if you like. He'll take it better from you."

Priscus put down the list.

"Drusus is a decent man."

"He is. I have nothing against him but that I did not choose him. Father chose him because the Claudian house holds the Wall and the Vibian house would like to stand nearer it." She did not look away. "I'm not asking you to agree with me. I'm asking you to write the letter. You raised me. He'll listen to you."

He looked at her for a while.

She was twenty-five. He had been thirteen when she was born. He had taught her the old tongue at four and the fang at nine. He had watched her, at nineteen, look down from a rail on Anvil at a Collar in the sand and say, he won, and had heard himself repeat it. There were several things he had not told her.

"I'll write to him," he said.

"Tonight."

"Tonight." He picked up the list again, to have something in his hands. "Was that all?"

"No," Livia said, and untied the cord on the book.

---

She did not open it at once. She sat with the cord loose in her fingers and looked past his head at the shelf where the province's papers were: six crates, brought back from Anvil, never unpacked. She had been through them, he thought. She had the face she had had at nine with a wooden fang, after losing a bout. She had gone away and thought about it and come back.

"I was at Tessera in the spring," she said.

"I know. The Master's inspection. You wrote."

"I wrote that it was cold and that the Master is a fool." She turned a page without looking at it. "I did not write about the one who read void."

Priscus did not move.

"Three times. Pollio read him and frowned. The Master said void, and I read him. Void with a wobble, the reading of something holding very still on purpose. I said unstable, in front of the prefect, which I will pay for at the College for some years." She looked at him. "A cut one. A white line on his arm where a citizen's line had been. Six years on a road that takes cut ones in a year, answering a Flamen with his face doing nothing. I knew him."

"Livia."

"I knew him. You'd know your own hand across a room. I asked the guard at the cage his intake name. The guard said, Vale, my lady. Citizen for a week. Off Anvil." She put her finger on the page. "Throat, four four seven one, reading unstable. That's what I wrote. In this book. Not the College's. I've read it every day for two months. I know why I didn't put it in the College's. The boy from the yard on Anvil. You told me through a cell slot he'd been released and gone home. Tell me why he was on a road at the bottom of a hole on Tessera with a number instead of a name."

He had prepared for this.

Nineteen days, since the courier packet from the Belt came across his desk. A prefect's report: a struck Master alive twenty-three years behind a door, a Centurion of the Princeps' house dead on a road, and a Collar who had been in a sealed cask and had gone, the report said, into the wall. He had read the number and known it, because he had put it there. He had decided what he would not say, which was everything. So he said the rest, in the voice he used for the law.

"He was released," Priscus said. "I signed the release myself, the week after the festival. The salvage was unreported but not unlawful; the vessel was founding-era and the ring outside the claim. He got his papers and his citizenship stood. He was told to leave Anvil, because a man read at investiture and then arrested is a man the yards won't hire. He left. Where he went after that was not the province's business. If he ended in a Pit under a number, it was under some other magistrate's hand than mine."

His heart did nothing.

Eighteen years of Aequitas. Eighteen years of learning to say something so his own heart said it too, because a magistrate with Pulse who could be read by Pulse was no magistrate. He said it to his sister with his heart flat under it, and watched her hear it.

She had no Pulse. She had Sense and Scent and Lorica Sight, the scholar's gifts, and none of them read a heart. She read him anyway, with twenty-five years of his face.

"That was very good," she said.

He waited.

"You believed it while you said it. That's the trick. You told me once. You decide it's true and then you say it, and the heart goes along, because the heart's a fool." She closed the book. "I've never been able to do it. I lie and my heart tells everyone in the room. I'm not you."

"Livia."

She got up. She went to the shelf, hooked her fingers under the third crate and dragged it out onto the floor, and the lid went over with a bang. She lifted out a bound register and dropped it on his desk, on top of the campaign lists, open at the page.

"From the Pit," she said. "A copy of the intake, sent to the quaestor of Anvil for the province's file. Vale, Jonah. Died in the Throat, three hundred and eighty-nine." She said the year as the College says a grade. "Died on arrival, according to the paper in your crate. Six years later I read him in a line. And a prefect's report in the Senate's post says a Collar with his number went into the wall this spring, after he killed a Centurion with his hands." She stood over the desk. "So he's dead twice. Once on a paper in your study, once on a road. And you told me he went home."

He said nothing.

She looked at him as she had looked at a Collar in a line, twice, and longer the second time. She was not going to ask again. She had asked once. That was his; she had learned it from him.

"I don't know what you did," Livia said, at last. "I know it was you, because the register's in your crate. I know you did it for the house, because you have never once done anything for any other reason." Her voice did not change. "I'll not ask what. I'll find out. That's what I do; it's the only thing Father let me be. When I know, I'll come and tell you. You'll decide whether it's true, and I'll watch you decide."

She went to the door.

"Write to Father tonight," she said. "About the Claudian. I meant that. It's the only thing I've asked you for in seven years that you can give me."

She went out. She left the crate on the floor.

---

The Frumentarius came the next morning, at the hour of the clients, and did not wait to be announced, because they did not.

A small man in a plain tunic with a grain-sack embroidered on the shoulder where anyone else would wear a stripe. He sat in the clients' chair without being asked and put two papers on the desk, on top of the register. Priscus, who would stand for the praetorship next year and had never once been afraid of a man in a chair, made sure his heart said so.

"Senator."

"I'm not a senator yet. My father is."

"Your father is Gracchan," the Frumentarius said, pleasantly, "and you are not. Which is why I've come to you and not to him. Quaestor, then. I'm Cominius. You'll not have heard of me; that's the office." He put a finger on the first paper. "The report of the cutter Vigilant, the fourteenth day of the month, six years ago, in the rings of Quirinus. A boarding officer lost. Marines who came back talking about a hostile lorica aboard a peregrine barge. The barge destroyed with all hands."

"I've read it."

"Then you know the barge that was destroyed with all hands came into Anvil's lower docks three days later. With a purser aboard who wrote a letter, and a pilot. The pilot was invested a citizen, then arrested, then went to Tessera under your hand with a number." He put a finger on the second paper. "The prefect of Metalla VII, this spring. A struck Master of the College, alive twenty-three years in a room. A Centurion of the Princeps' house dead on a road, by a Collar's hands, the report says. A Collar who was in a sealed cask with a mark the Flamen couldn't close, and who told a College shackle no, and it opened. And went, the prefect says, into the wall." He sat back. "I've read the first one twice. I've read the second once, because once was enough."

Priscus looked at the two papers.

"What is it you want to know?"

"What a hostile lorica aboard a peregrine barge is," Cominius said. "My office has found three in a hundred years and cut all three. I've a file shut six years on a decurion lost in the rings, because a quaestor on Anvil closed the province's end very neatly. A release he signed, and a register that says the boy died on arrival."

"The register's under your papers."

"So it is." Cominius did not look at it. "And a Flamen who says the Collar read void three times. Void is a word my office has for something it has found three times in a hundred years." He smiled. A clerk's smile. "I don't want to know what you did, quaestor. It's on your desk. I want to know what you saw. In the cell. You've Pulse. What did his heart do?"

Priscus had prepared for his sister. He had not prepared for this. What he did in the Senate did not work on a man who had already decided what was true.

"Nothing," he said. "His heart did nothing. He said he didn't know what was in the box, and it was true. He said he hadn't got a lorica. I said, is it in your possession, and he said no, and it was true." He heard himself say it. "I read a boy telling the truth about something he didn't understand. I sent him to the Belt for what he'd carried, not for what he was. I wrote him dead so nobody would go looking for what he'd carried. That's what I saw. A boy whose heart did nothing."

"And what he carried."

"Was burned."

"By you."

"By me."

Cominius nodded, slowly, a clerk whose column has come out.

"Then here's what I take back to the office." He stood and gathered the two papers. "Six years ago a boy came off a derelict with something in him that read void. A quaestor with Pulse found his heart doing nothing, sent him down a hole, and wrote him dead. This spring something that reads void came up out of the same hole, killed a Centurion of the Princeps' house with its hands, and went into a wall. On the word of a foreman who didn't follow."

"You don't believe it."

"I've never once, in twenty years, had anything go into a wall that I'd have liked to see go into one." He tucked the papers under his arm. "I'll be opening the file. Your name on the first page, because it was your hand. Your father's on the second, because it was his seal. The boy's on the third, because he's the one I'm going to find." He went to the door. "Your sister read him at Tessera, I'm told. Unstable, she said. I'd like to talk to her. Not today."

He went out.

Priscus sat with the lists in front of him and the crate on the floor where she had left it. The register lay open on the desk, at a line he had written in a clerk's hand five years ago, in this room, at this desk, because it was the cleanest way. He sat a long time with his heart doing nothing at all.

Then he wrote to his father about the Claudian. He put it in the subjunctive.
