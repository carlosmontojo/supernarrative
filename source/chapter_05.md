# Chapter 5: Investiture

The Flamens' house on Anvil was the only building on the moon that had been built rather than cut.

Everything else was rock. The tenements were rock, the yard office was rock with a door in it, the quaestor's house was rock with better doors. The Flamens' house stood at the top of the yard road on its own square of levelled ground, white, with columns, with a roof, with steps, looking like something that had been lifted whole off a core world and set down on Anvil to remind everyone what a building was. Collars were not permitted past the steps. Jonah had walked past it every day of his life and had never once wondered what was inside, because wondering was a thing you did about places you might go.

He went up the steps on the last day of Founding Week with his father on one side and Morrow on the other and did not look up at the columns, because he was busy.

He was holding still.

He had practised it for six days. He had lain in the back room with the blanket over the vent and held the thing behind his heart quiet for one hour, then two, then, on the fourth night, for four, until his jaw ached and his vision went grey at the edges and he had to let go and lie there shaking while it moved again, restless, going through his arms and back like a man pacing a cell. Four hours. He'd thought that would be enough. Then he'd found out, from Morrow, what an investiture actually took, and had gone home and lain down and done it again.

"It's a reading," Morrow had said. "A real one. Not a wand. They put your arm on the glass and the Flamen reads what's there, and there's nothing there, so it says nothing. Then they put the cutting on. Then they read again, and it says one, and he fuses it at nine, and you say the words, and it's done. An hour. Less. I've seen forty of them."

"And if there's something there?"

Morrow had looked at him for a long moment.

"Then it says what's there," he'd said, "and I don't know what happens after, because I've never seen it. Jonah. I don't want to know what you're asking me. Do you understand? I want the boy who flew my barge home to walk out of that house with a line on his arm and a ship, and I want to have known nothing, ever, about anything else. Can you give me that?"

"I can try."

"Then try," Morrow had said, and had put his hand on the back of Jonah's neck, briefly, the way he had put it on Ochoa's blanket, and gone.

So Jonah went up the steps holding still, with the thing behind his heart quiet and listening, and the ring on his finger, and the seal under the floorboard in his father's back room where he had put it on the first night and not touched since, and the letter in the quaestor's pocket that he did not know about, and walked into the Flamens' house.

---

It was cool inside, and white, and quiet in a way that Anvil never was. There was a hall with a floor of real stone, pale, and at the far end of it a table that was not a table but a slab of something dark and glassy on a stand, and behind the slab a Flamen in the grey robe, an old Blank with a shaved head and a thin black line on his forearm and eyes that had read a great many arms.

There were perhaps thirty people in the hall. Morrow's people, at the back, Bram and Hollis and the yard foremen in their good coats. Tom. Mara, with her mother Wren, who had never in her life been past the steps and stood like the roof might fall on her. A dozen Blanks from the dock offices who came to every investiture because it was the only thing on Anvil that happened indoors. And at the front, on a bench to one side of the slab, the quaestor and his household.

Priscus sat with his hands on his knees. He had not looked at Jonah when Jonah came in, and did not now. He was looking at the slab, with the face of a man watching a scale settle.

Beside him, his sister was looking at Jonah.

She had a book on her knee, closed, with her finger in it. She had, he saw, been reading until the door opened, and had looked up, and was looking now the way she'd looked at the yard rail, considering, with her head a little on one side, and when his eyes met hers she did not look away and did not smile. She looked at his arm. Then she looked at his feet. Then she went back to her book.

"Jonah Vale," the Flamen said. "Peregrine. Come."

He went.

The slab was the height of a table. Its surface was black and had a depth to it, the way the ice in the rings had depth, and when the Flamen took his right arm and laid it flat on the surface, forearm up, it was neither warm nor cold. It was nothing. It was like putting his arm on a hole.

The thing behind his heart went from still to something past still.

He felt it happen and did not understand it. He had been holding it, the way he'd practised, a second heart held quiet inside the first. And when his arm went on the glass it stopped needing to be held. It went down, on its own, deeper than he had ever pushed it, deeper than the four hours, into some place under his ribs where he could barely feel it at all, and it went there fast, and it went there the way a man goes flat in a ditch when he hears a rifle.

It was hiding. Not because he'd asked. Because it had felt the glass, and knew what the glass was, and had been on the wrong side of a glass before.

He stood with his arm on the slab and his face doing nothing and thought, very clearly, you've done this before. And the thing under his ribs, hiding, listening, gave no sign at all that it had heard, and that was an answer too.

The Flamen put his hands on the slab on either side of Jonah's arm and looked down into it.

Nobody in the hall moved. Jonah could hear Wren Ansel breathing. He could hear, beside the bench, a page turn.

"Peregrine," the Flamen said, to the slab, in the flat voice of a man reading a manifest. "No lorica. No genius. No lineage. Grade, nothing." He looked up. "Clean."

Somewhere behind him, Morrow let out a breath that Jonah would hear in his sleep for years.

---

The cutting came in a dish.

It was a small dish of the same dark glass as the slab, and in it, on a fold of white cloth, was a thing the size of a lentil, black, that caught the light. Jonah looked at it and thought of the box on the derelict and of what had been under the cloth there, breathing, and had to hold his face still with the whole of his will.

"By the authority of the quaestor of the province," the Flamen said, "and in the name of the Republic, this cutting of the Matter is given to Jonah Vale, that he may bear it as a citizen bears it, in service and in law. Say it."

"In service and in law."

The Flamen took the lentil out of the dish with two fingers and set it on the inside of Jonah's forearm, a hand's width below the elbow, on the skin.

It went in.

He had known it would. Morrow had told him. It goes in, Morrow had said, and it's cold, and then it's a friend. But he had not been ready for the feel of it. It was a small thing and it went through his skin like a needle through cloth, no pain, just a pressure, and then it was under, and it was moving, and it was stupid.

That was the first thing he knew about it. It was stupid. It was a mind, of a kind, the way a wasp is a mind, and it came into his arm knowing exactly three things, which were to hold, to strike, and to obey, and it began at once to look for the one who would tell it which. It went up his arm looking. It was happy. It was so happy to be in an arm.

And down under his ribs, in the place where it had been hiding, the other thing opened one eye.

Jonah felt it. He felt the whole of what happened next, and it took less than the time the Flamen took to pick up his stylus, and he would never be able to describe it to anyone, not even the old man, without leaving most of it out. The thing under his ribs did not come up. It did not have to. It reached, from where it was, up the inside of his arm, the way a hand reaches up through dark water, and it took the cutting.

There was no sound. There was no plate. Nothing showed on the skin of his arm, on the glass, in the hall. The stupid happy thing that had been looking for its master was simply taken, all of it, and pulled down, and Jonah felt it go, the three small wants of it, hold and strike and obey, going down past his ribs one at a time and being folded away, and the last of them, obey, went with something that felt almost like relief.

Then something came back up.

It came up the same way, through the dark, and it settled into his forearm exactly where the cutting had gone in, and it lay there, and it was the thing under his ribs, a piece of it, a finger of it, and it was wearing the cutting.

He didn't have another word for it. It had taken the cutting apart and it had put the pieces on, the way a man puts on a dead man's coat, and it lay in his forearm shaped like a Blank's cutting, thinking three small thoughts very loudly on purpose, and under the three small thoughts it was itself, and it was listening.

"Read," the Flamen said, and laid his hands on the slab.

The hall held its breath. Jonah held everything.

"Lorica," the Flamen said, to the glass. "Standard cutting. Genius, standard. Lineage, none. Grade." He paused. Jonah's heart did something. "One."

Then the Flamen looked up, frowning, and looked down again.

"Dark," he said.

Nobody said anything. The Flamen bent closer to the glass, and Jonah, who could not see what was in it, watched the old man's face instead, and saw a man looking at a number that was right and a colour that wasn't.

"Plate reads dark," the Flamen said, half to himself. "Odd on a fresh cutting. They come up grey, mostly." He looked at Jonah's arm, at the skin, which was skin. "Some do that. Deep blood on the Ferrum side, I've seen it. Nothing." He straightened. "Grade one. Standard. Fusing at nine."

He put his thumb on Jonah's forearm where the cutting had gone in, and pressed, and said a word in the old Latin.

Jonah felt the pressure go in. He felt it go looking for the thing it was meant to bind, the cutting, the stupid friend, to put a ceiling on it, and he felt the thing wearing the cutting lie perfectly still and let it. Let it press. Let it find three small wants and a number and lay a ceiling on them, and lie there under the ceiling, folded, patient, and the ceiling came down on nothing at all, and held nothing at all, and the Flamen took his thumb away satisfied.

"Fused," he said. "Mark."

And a line came up on Jonah's arm.

He watched it. Everybody watched it. It came up through the skin of his forearm where the thumb had been, thin as a scratch and black, running a hand's length along the inside of the arm, the way it did on every Blank on Anvil, the mark, the line that said citizen, and Jonah watched it come and knew, with the part of him that knew things now, that it was not a mark. It was the thing under his ribs, drawing. It had looked at the Flamen's arm, and Morrow's, and the dock clerks', and it had learned what the line was for, and it was drawing one, carefully, the way a child draws a house.

On the bench, a page did not turn.

Jonah didn't look. He could feel her looking. She had seen something, when the cutting went in, or when the mark came, or when the Flamen said dark, and he could feel the look on his arm like heat, and he kept his face doing nothing and his eyes on the Flamen, and after a moment, the page turned.

"The collar," the Flamen said.

---

This was the part they came for. Everybody knew it. The reading was for the Flamens and the cutting was for the law and the collar was for the Collars, the moment at the end when the ring came off a man's neck in front of everyone, and there was not a Collar on Anvil who had not imagined it for themselves at least once, lying awake.

The Flamen came around the slab with a tool in his hand. It was a small thing, a set of jaws. He put his other hand on the ring at Jonah's throat and said the word.

It was the word. The same one Varro had said in the hold. Jonah's body had been built to hear it, and it heard it now, and did nothing, because there was nothing at his throat to hear it with, and the Flamen waited for the ring to open the way rings opened, on command, with a click, and it did not.

The Flamen frowned. He said the word again. The ring sat on Jonah's neck like a ring.

"Old issue," the Flamen muttered. "They get sluggish. Hold still." And he put the jaws on the ring and cut it, and it came apart in two curved pieces in his hands, and Jonah's neck was bare, and the Flamen held up the pieces so the hall could see, and the hall made the sound.

Tom was weeping. Jonah heard it before he saw it, the big helpless noise of a man who has held a thing for nineteen years and had it taken off his chest all at once, and then he saw it, and his father's face, and he had to look at the floor.

"Say the words," the Flamen said.

He said them. He said he was a citizen of the Republic in service and in law, and that his body was the Republic's in war and his hand was the Republic's in peace, and that he would bear the cutting he had been given as it was given, no more, and that he would answer to the stripe above him and the Princeps above all, and he said them in the old Latin because they gave them to him in the old Latin, and he got the endings right.

And then he was a Blank, with a line on his arm, and a ship.

Priscus stood up.

He came to the slab, and Jonah understood that this was the quaestor's part, the last thing, the Empire's hand on the citizen's shoulder, and he stood still for it. Priscus was not tall. He did not need to be. He put his hand on Jonah's shoulder, the right one, and looked at him, and it was a short look, and it was not the ledger look from the square. It was another kind. It was the look of a man checking a sum that has come out right and wondering, briefly, why he had expected it not to.

"Citizen," Priscus said. "The Republic acknowledges you."

"Sir."

"Your ship is the Kestrel. Your yard is Morrow's. Your purser, I'm told, is a man named Marchand, who is precise." Something moved in the quaestor's face that might, on another man, have been the ghost of a joke. "Be precise with him. Go and be a captain."

He took his hand away. He turned and went back to the bench and sat, and his sister stood up beside him with the book under her arm and looked at Jonah's arm once more, at the line, and then at his face, and then she said something to her brother that Jonah could not hear, and her brother nodded without looking at her, and they went.

---

The dinner was in Tom's two rooms and it was the best night of Tom Vale's life, and Jonah knew it, and spent it holding still.

There were eleven of them in a space for four. Tom, and Mara and Wren, and Bram and Hollis, who had brought a jug from the Halyard between them and were already halfway down it, and Morrow for the first hour, standing by the door with a cup in his hand because there was nowhere to sit, and two of the yard foremen, and Gaspar Roake from across the road, who had been Tom's friend for thirty years and had come because he'd been asked and sat in the corner with his own bottle and did not look at Jonah once all evening, which Jonah noticed, and put away with the other things.

Tom made a speech. It was not a good speech. It was about the ladder, and how it was real, and how he'd always said, and how Jonah's mother would have. He got that far and stopped and stood with his cup in both hands and his mouth working, and Mara got up and put her arm around him and said the rest for him, which was to Jonah, and was short, and everyone drank.

Jonah drank too. He drank with the thing under his ribs held quiet and the line on his arm itching in a way that a line should not, and Mara's hand on the back of his neck where she could reach it, and he thought, this is it. This is the thing. This is what it's for. And under the thought was the other thing, the thing he did not say, which was that he was a citizen with a ship and a line on his arm, and the line was a drawing, and the citizenship was on a fused cutting that was not there, and every piece of the best night of his father's life was a thing that a man with a stripe could look at and see through, and one of them had.

He put it away.

He was good at that now. He'd got a lot of practice.

The knock came at the second hour.

It was not a knock. It was the flat of a hand, three times, and then the door, which had not been locked because Tom did not own a lock, came open, and a Blank in the grey of the vigiles came through it with four more behind him, and the room went from eleven people to a wall.

"Jonah Vale," the sergeant said. He had a paper in his hand and did not look at it. "Citizen. Enrolled this day. By order of the quaestor of the province you're to come with us, now, on a matter concerning the Flamens."

There was a silence in which Jonah heard, very clearly, Gaspar Roake put his bottle down.

"What matter?" Morrow said. He was at the door. He'd been leaving; his cup was still in his hand. "Sergeant. What matter concerning the Flamens? The man was read this morning. I was there. Clean, and one, and fused. What matter?"

"The quaestor's matter, sir." The sergeant hadn't looked at him. He was looking at Jonah, and there was something in his face that was not enjoyment and not sympathy, that was the face of a man doing a thing he had been told to do and would rather have been told to do somewhere else. "Citizen. Please. Stand up and come. It'll go better."

Jonah stood up.

"And you'll hold," the sergeant said, and said the word.

It was not to a collar. Jonah had no collar. It was to the line on his arm, to the cutting, to the stupid friend that had been fused at nine that morning and belonged now to the Republic in war and in peace and to any stripe above him and to the sergeant, who carried the quaestor's token on a chain around his neck and so had, for the length of this errand, the quaestor's imperium. A Blank's lorica heard that word and held its man in two seconds. Every Blank on Anvil knew it. It was what the line was for.

Nothing happened.

Jonah stood in the middle of his father's room with his arms at his sides and his knees straight and nothing happened at all, and the sergeant waited the two seconds, and then a third, and Jonah watched him understand.

He had known this was coming. He'd seen it on the Kestrel, eleven hours out from Anvil with his hand on the dead ring at his throat. One day a man with a stripe was going to say the word in a room with other people in it and his knees were going to stay straight. He had thought he had time. He had thought, when it came, that he would run, or fight, or do something with the thing under his ribs that would leave a mark on the world.

What he had not thought about was his father.

Two of the vigiles had moved while the sergeant waited. They had moved the way vigiles moved, without being told, and one of them was behind Tom Vale's chair with a hand on the old man's shoulder, and the other was beside Mara, and neither of them had done anything yet, and both of them had plate on their forearms, the pale grey plate of a Blank's cutting, come up over the skin without a sound, and at the wrist of each of them, short, a hand's length, dull, a fang.

"That's odd," the sergeant said, quietly.

"Old issue," Jonah said. "The Flamen said. They get sluggish."

The sergeant looked at him.

Jonah looked back, and thought, with a cold and total clarity that he would come to know very well, that he could do it. The thing under his ribs had come up. It had come up when the word was said and had not been held, because he had not been holding it, because he had been drinking and being a citizen and having a father, and it was in his arms now, and his back, and it was looking at the two grey fangs by his father and by Mara and it was not afraid of them at all. He could feel exactly where the plate would come out. He could feel the crooked fang on the wrong side of his wrist waiting to be asked. Five Blanks. Tiro plate. Grey. He had put a decurion on his back with fourteen years on him, and these were dock vigiles with a token on a chain.

And there was a fang at his father's throat.

Not at it. Near it. Close enough. And one near Mara, who was looking at him, and who had seen the sergeant say the word and had seen nothing happen and had understood, he saw her understand, faster than the sergeant had, everything he had not told her.

He knelt.

He did it slowly, the way a locked man does it, the way he'd seen Gaspar do it in Founding Square, the knees going and the hands going flat and the head going down, and he held the thing in his arms and his back still by main force while he did it, and it was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than the glass. Harder than the hold. Every piece of him that had come out of a stone box wanted to stand, and he put it on its knees in front of five Blanks in his father's room and held it there, and felt, as he did it, something inside him that had not been there before, a small clean tearing, like a seam going.

"Sluggish," the sergeant said, after a moment, in a voice that had decided to believe it. "Right. Bind him. Search the rooms."

They bound him. They searched. It took them a quarter of an hour, and they were not careful, and Tom's two rooms came apart around the eleven people in them, and the vigil at Tom's shoulder kept his hand there the whole time, and Tom sat under it with his face gone grey and said nothing, and Mara stood with a fang beside her and looked at Jonah on his knees on the floor and did not cry, and did not look away, and he would have given the ship and the line and the rest of his life for her to have looked away.

They found the stone box in his kit in the first minute. They held it up. The sergeant looked at it and at Jonah and wrote something down.

They found the seal in the twelfth.

It was under the floorboard in the back room where he'd put it on the first night, wrapped in the cloth that had been in the box, and the vigil who found it did not know what it was and handed it to the sergeant, who did not know either, and who turned it over and looked at the wax on it, and at the mark pressed into the wax, and went, very slightly, pale.

"Right," he said again. "Up. Bring him."

They brought him. He went between two of them with his hands bound behind him and his knees still remembering the floor, past Morrow at the door, who did not say anything and whose cup was on the floor in a puddle, past Bram and Hollis, past the foremen, past Gaspar Roake in his corner, who was looking at the bottle in front of him with the fixed attention of a man who has decided that a bottle is the only thing in the room, and Jonah looked at him as he passed, and Gaspar did not look up.

At the door he turned his head.

Tom had stood up. The vigil had let him. He was standing in the wreck of his two rooms with the festival cup still in his hand and his face doing the thing it did, but the other way, all the way the other way, and he said, "Son," and could not say anything else.

"It's all right," Jonah said. "It's a mistake. I'll be back by morning. Tell Morrow."

He said it in an ordinary voice. He heard it come out and it sounded exactly like himself, and he thought, that's a lie, and I've never lied to him, and I'm going to have to get better at it, and they took him out into the tenement road and the door closed on his father's face.

Mara was the one he didn't look at. He had looked at his father because his father needed it. He didn't look at Mara because she didn't, and because she had understood, and because he could not have held still through it.

They walked him up the yard road in the dark. The sun-lamps were at their night setting and the square was empty and the Flamens' house stood white at the top of the road with its columns, and they went past it, and on, to the quaestor's house, which was rock with better doors, and in through the smallest of them, and down.

Under his ribs, all the way down, the thing that had knelt when he told it to was quiet. It had not liked it. He could feel that. It had done it, and it had not understood why, and it was lying in the dark now, pressed flat, not hungry, not listening, turning the thing over, the not understanding, the way it had turned over the old break in his wrist. Interested. Patient.

Waiting to see what he was going to do about it.
