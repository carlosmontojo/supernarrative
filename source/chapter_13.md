# Chapter 13: Chisel

The stick was a pick haft, ash, a metre long, worn pale where a hand had held it for years, and the Censor beat him with it thirty-one times in the first hour and counted every one out loud in the old tongue.

"Triginta unus," the old man said, and the haft came out of the dark and took Jonah across the back of the knee, and he went down on the rock of the Cradle for the thirty-first time, and lay there, and the thing under his ribs, which had come up in the first minute ready to put plate on every place the stick had touched and had been told no, thirty-one times, lay under his heart and did not understand any of it.

"Get up."

"You said an hour."

"I said an hour of the hand. That was an hour of you falling down. It is not the same thing." The Censor stood over him with the haft resting on his shoulder like a man at ease on a wall, and he was not breathing hard, and he was seventy, or near it, and had eaten one bowl a day for twenty years. "Again. And this time do not step left."

"I didn't step left."

"You stepped left. You have stepped left thirty-one times. Left and a little forward, off the line, every time the stick comes in low, and it is a beautiful step, it is the first step of House Fulvia and a dead decurion did it very well, and it is why you are on the floor, because I know it is coming and I put the stick where you are going to be." The haft came off the shoulder. "Get up, Jonah Vale, and be a man with a hand. Not a man with a decurion in his legs. I do not want the decurion. I want you."

Jonah got up.

---

It went on for three months, and he would remember it as the happiest time in the Pit, which said something about the Pit and something about him and he never decided which.

The shape of it was this. He cut one shard in the day, slowly, at the wall of the seventh chamber, with Corr beside him not saying a month and Bricks at the bucket with his splinted wrist saying nothing at all. He ate at dusk. He said his number to Decimus. And then he picked up two buckets and walked down the Deep road past the lamps that were too far apart, past Ulli sitting on his bucket at the end of it with his eyes closed, into the Cradle, and put the buckets down, and the rock at the back opened, and the old man came out with the lamp and the stick.

Three hours. Every night. The first hour was the hand and the second was the tongue and the third was whatever the old man had decided it was going to be, and then the lamp came down the road and the rock closed and Jonah picked up the buckets and carried them to the cage with his legs shaking in a way that had nothing to do with the Vein, and slept two hours, and got up and said his number.

He did not know how the old man did it. He asked, once, in the second month, lying on the rock after the forty-third fall of the night, and the Censor had said, "I have been sitting in a stone box for twenty years, boy. I have had a great deal of time to decide what I would do with my body if anybody ever gave me a reason. You are the reason. Get up."

The hand was the first thing, and it was the thing he hated, and it was the thing that changed him.

Because he had thought he could fight. Not well. He'd never thought well. But he had put a decurion on his back and a dock thug and a foreman, and he'd done it with Varro's feet and his own hands, and somewhere in him he had begun to think of that as his. And the old man took it away from him one bruise at a time.

He did not teach him anything, the first month. That was the thing Jonah could not have explained to Mara, or to Corr, or to anyone who had not lain on the floor of the Cradle. The Censor did not show him a step or a guard or a way to hold his hands. He stood in the dark with a pick haft and he found out, with the haft, every single thing that Varro had left in Jonah's body, and he hit it.

The Fulvian step. That went first. Left and forward on a low line; the haft was there. Thirty-one times the first night, forty the second, and on the ninth night Jonah stepped right, for no reason, because his legs had finally understood that left meant the stick, and the old man said, "Good," for the first time, and hit him anyway, because the right step had been bad.

The wrist. The turn he'd done on Hask, the hinge that only went one way. The old man let him have the haft's end in both hands, once, in the third week, and turned his whole body through the turn so that it was Jonah on the rock with his own wrist bent back to his elbow, and said, from above, conversationally, "You did that to a foreman. Slow, on purpose, so that Auger would not see what it was. Auger saw. It was a Vibian turn, boy, it is the second thing they teach in that house, and you did it like a Vibian, with the weight back and the hip closed, and the reason you could do it at all is that the decurion had been beaten by a Vibian once and his lorica remembered how." He let go. "That is what an echo is. Not skill. A memory of being beaten. You are carrying a dead man's defeats around in your legs and calling them yours."

The stance. The way he stood. Livia had seen it at the yard rail and Mara had seen it in the square and Auger had seen it in the road, and the Censor saw it in the first minute and put the haft in the back of his knee until he stopped.

"It is not that it is wrong," the old man said, in the second month, sitting on the rock across from him with the lamp turned low and the stick across his knees, while Jonah lay on his back and counted his breaths in the old tongue and waited for the fever to go down. "The step is a good step. The turn is a good turn. The stance is the stance of a man who has fought forty duels and won thirty-eight of them, and if you had learned it from a master over ten years I would be pleased with you. You did not. It came up your arm in a hold out of a corpse. And under it, folded in with it, is the other thing. You told the rock. The thing that looks at Collars."

"Yes."

"When you stepped left on Bricks's chisel, the thing in your legs was looking at Bricks. When you turned Hask's wrist it was looking at Hask. It does not see men. It sees Collars, and it sees them the way a decurion of the equestrian order saw them, which is as things you do on your way somewhere else, and every time you let the feet do it, you let that look through your eyes, and you agreed with it, and you told the rock that too, and you were right to be frightened." The old man's voice did not change. "I am not taking the step away from you because it is bad. I am taking it away because it is not yours, and because you cannot use a dead man's contempt without it using you. When you can do the step because you have chosen to, in your own legs, with your own weight, looking at the man in front of you and seeing a man, then you may have it back. Not before."

"How long?"

"As long as it takes. Get up."

He got up.

---

The tongue was the second hour, and it was the thing that made him understand what the old man was.

He had thought the Censor would teach him Latin the way the yard school had taught him letters, with a slate and a list. The old man did not have a slate. He had a lamp turned to a thread and a voice, and he sat on the rock and spoke, and Jonah sat across from him and listened, and that was the lesson.

The Censor spoke in the old tongue. All of it. From the second night of the second hour he did not say a word of the common speech between the first bell of that hour and the last, and Jonah, who had a hundred numbers and a prayer and the words off the sides of manifests, sat in the dark and drowned.

"You will not understand me," the old man had said, the first night, in the common speech, before he began. "Not for a month. You will sit there and hear a noise, and you will hate it, and you will want me to stop and tell you what the noise means, and I will not, because that is how a Broadstripe child learns it, in a house where the adults speak it over his head at dinner, and by the time he is five he has never once been told what a word means and he knows all of them. You are nineteen. You will be worse at it than a child. You have eleven months. Listen."

He listened.

It was a noise, at first. It was the noise the crowd had made saying the prayer, and the noise the Flamen had made over the glass, and the noise the quaestor had made when he laid the Republic's hand on Jonah's shoulder, all of it, without the gaps, for an hour, and he sat in it and heard nothing and wanted to put his hands over his ears. The old man talked. He talked about the wall, Jonah thought, from the way his eyes went to it. He talked about something that made his mouth go hard, and something that made him laugh, once, dry, and something that made him stop and look at the lamp for a long time before going on. And Jonah understood none of it, and the second hour ended, and the old man said, in the common speech, "Get up," and beat him with the stick for the third hour instead of whatever the third hour was going to be, because Jonah had let his face show that he hated it.

On the twentieth night he understood a word.

It was mater. Mother. It was on the derelict's wall and he had read it and had not known, and the old man said it, in the middle of the noise, and Jonah heard it the way you hear your own name in a crowd, and everything on either side of it went from noise to almost-words for the length of a sentence, and then closed over again.

On the thirtieth night he understood a sentence.

On the forty-fifth he understood that the old man had been telling him, every night, for an hour, in a language he did not speak, the history of the College of Flamens from the Finding to the year 366, and that he had been telling it to a boy who could not understand a word because it was the only way he had ever known to teach anything, and because he had not had anyone to tell it to for twenty years and did not intend to stop.

On the sixtieth night the Censor said something and Jonah answered, without thinking, in the same tongue, badly, with the endings wrong, and the old man stopped, and looked at him in the thread of lamp, and said, in the common speech, for the first time in a month:

"There. That is the thing behind the endings. Do not lose it."

And then, in the old tongue: "Your endings are a disgrace. Again."

---

He was growing.

He could feel it the way he'd felt it on the haul, on the road, on the back of his hand. Not from eating. The thing under his ribs had not eaten since the mouthful, had not been let near the wall in front of a man, had not been given so much as a shard, and it was hungry the way it was always hungry, with a size, and he knew the size and it was, most nights, not far from full, because it was eating him.

Not his flesh. The old man had been clear on that; that was later, that was what happened when the hunger got past a certain mark and there was nothing else. This was the other thing. The thing the rock had called growth. The body used hard, every night, three hours on the rock of the Cradle with a stick finding every place that Varro had left in him and hitting it, and the thing under his ribs went through the body every night after, reading, the way it had on the derelict, finding the new bruises and the new places where the muscle had torn and the new way his legs had learned to stand, and it learned from what it found, and it got, by a size he could almost name, bigger.

The fever came every six or seven nights. He counted it down in the old tongue, on the road, with the buckets, and by the second month he did not need to sit.

The trouble was the day.

He'd sweat, at the wall. That was the thing. A fever that came on at the end of the third hour and was counted down on the road was a fever that was still in him at the dawn count, and Decimus would say his number and look at his face, and Corr, beside him at the wall, would look at his face too, and Corr had stopped saying a month but he had not stopped looking.

"You're going again," Corr said, in the second month. Not unkindly. It was just what he said. "I can see it. It comes off for you and you sweat and your hands shake and you cut like a new man. That's the Vein. It let you go once. It doesn't let go twice."

"I turned around."

"Nobody turns around. You did something, cut one, I don't know what, and it's not going to work again, and when they carry you down that road the second time I'm going to say a month and I'm going to be wrong the other way." Corr went back to his line. "I'm sorry. I like you. You don't talk."

And that was the cover. Jonah understood it on the road that night, with the buckets, and told the old man in the second hour, in bad Latin, and the old man listened and said, in the same tongue, slowly, so Jonah would follow it:

"Yes. Let them think it. A man the Vein is taking sweats and shakes and cuts like a new man and is carried down the road one day and does not come back. You will sweat and shake and cut like a new man for six years, and every man on that road will look at you every morning and see the sickness, and you will be the longest dying anyone in this Pit has ever seen, and nobody will ever look closer, because nobody looks closely at a dying man. It is impolite." A pause. "It is also the only thing in this rock that is free. Use it."

---

The Forms came in the third month, and they came as names.

He had not asked. He'd stopped asking for things the old man had not given; it was faster. But on the ninetieth night, when the first hour ended and Jonah was on the rock for the eleventh time, which was the fewest yet, the Censor did not say get up. He sat down across from him with the stick on his knees and the lamp low and said, in the old tongue, "You have stopped stepping left."

"Nine nights."

"Nine nights. And you have stopped closing your hip on the turn, and you stand like a man who has been beaten a great deal, which is what you are, and not like a decurion, which is what you were. The echo is not gone. It will never be gone. But it is in your hands now instead of your legs, and your hands are yours." The old man looked at him. "So. Four names. Learn them tonight. You will see all four before you die and you will fight at least two."

He laid the stick flat on the rock between them.

"Vibia." He touched the stick, once, at the end nearest Jonah, a single tap. "The house of the man who burned your seal. The house of the girl with the cup. They hold a gift called Verdict, which stops any lorica in its sight for one heartbeat, and their Form is built on the heartbeat. They do not move. They wait, still, and let you come, and when you are half a step into whatever you have decided to do, you stop, because they have stopped you, and their fang is in your throat seam before your heart beats again. Thrust. Stillness. Patience. You will know a Vibian because he is the only man on the floor who is not moving, and because he is already looking at the place on you where he is going to put the blade."

Jonah thought of a man behind a table, reading to the end of a page, and said nothing.

"Claudia." A tap, further along. "The Princeps' house. They hold the Wall, which is plate that does not shed, ever, under anything, and their Form is a wall coming toward you. Grind. Absorb. Take the cut on the plate and step in, and take the next, and step in, and there is no seam you can reach because there is no moment when the wall is not moving at you. You cannot beat a Claudian on the plate. You beat him on the feet, or not at all. Nobody has beaten the Princeps on the feet in forty years. There are men who fight the Claudian Form without the gift, clients, hangers-on, and they are dangerous and they are liars, because a wall that can shed is not a wall, it is a man pretending, and a man pretending is a man who can be made to hurry."

"Fulvia." A tap. "Tide. Speed that climbs. The longer the bout goes, the faster they are, and they know it, so they circle, and they cut you a little, and they circle, and they cut you a little, and in the fourth minute they are twice the man they were in the first and you are bleeding from six places and cannot see them. Your decurion fought a Fulvian Form badly, from a manual. He had the step and not the patience. The step is what you were doing on the floor for a month. It is the first thing they teach and it is the only thing most of them ever learn."

"Aemilia." The last tap, at the far end of the stick. "Mirror. They throw. They stand off and they cast plate at you, shards, and if you throw anything back it comes back at you faster, and they are dishonest and they are effective and every duellist in the City pretends to despise them and is afraid of them. You will not fight an Aemilian on a floor. You will fight one across a room, if you are unlucky, and the only answer is to close, and closing is what they have built the whole Form to prevent."

He picked up the stick.

"Four houses. Four Forms. There are three hundred others but they are all one of these four with the name changed, and the cutters in the Blank garrisons fight a Fulvian step and a Vibian turn and call it drill, and the Thinstripes fight whatever house their patron is a client of, and the Broadstripes fight their own, and everyone in the Empire who has ever held a fang has held it in one of four ways." The pale eyes were steady in the lamp. "And there was a fifth."

Jonah waited.

"Not tonight." The Censor stood up. "Tonight you know four names. Say them."

"Vibia. Claudia. Fulvia. Aemilia."

"In the old tongue."

He said them in the old tongue.

"Your endings," the Censor said, "are still a disgrace. Get up."

---

The news came on the ninety-fourth night and it came up the Throat on a transport, the way everything did.

There were eleven new men in the cage and one of them was Ferrum. Jonah heard it before he saw him; the man said something to the guard at the cage door in the flat yard vowels of the north side of Anvil, and Jonah, forty paces down the road with a chisel in his hand, put the chisel down.

His name was Hollen. He'd been a cutter in the north yards for twenty years and had put a torch through a Blank foreman's hand in a fight over scrip and had been given the mines for it, and he stood in the road with a new shackle on his ankle and a new number and looked at the men looking at him, and Jonah walked up the road to him and said, "Vale. South yards. Morrow's."

Hollen looked at him. His face did the thing faces did.

"You're the one," he said. "Tom's boy. They took you off a dinner."

"Yes."

"Whole moon heard. Vigiles at the door, five of them, and the quaestor's seal on it, and nobody knew why, and then you were gone and nobody knew where." Hollen's eyes went to the white line on Jonah's arm and stayed there. "That's the line. They cut it out."

"Tell me about Tom."

Hollen was quiet for a moment.

He told him. He told it the way a Ferrum cutter told a thing, flat, with the vowels of the north side, standing in a road ten kilometres under a garrison with sixty men listening and pretending not to. Tom Vale had gone to the quaestor's house the morning after, and the morning after that, and every morning for a month, and had stood at the door with his hat in his hand, and had been told nothing, and had gone home. And then he had gone to the yard office, and to the Flamens' house, and to the dock, where the transports went out, and had asked every grey Blank who'd stand still for it where the boy had gone, and had been told nothing, and had gone home. And then he had stopped going anywhere. He'd sat in his two rooms, Hollen said, with the door open, and people had brought him food, the Ansel girl and Morrow's people and the neighbour, Roake, who'd sat with him some nights, and he'd eaten some of it and not the rest, and in the winter he'd stopped eating the rest, and one morning the Ansel girl had come with a bowl and he'd been in his chair with the door open and his hat on his knee.

"When," Jonah said.

"Winter. Two months back. Bit more." Hollen looked at the road. "Morrow's yard paid the melt. Somebody said his name. I wasn't there. I'm north side."

"Who said it?"

"Don't know. Somebody. That's what they told me." Hollen looked back at him. "I'm sorry. He was a good man. Everybody said. He believed in it. The ladder. He used to say it in the Halyard, when he'd had one. My boy's going to have a line." The cutter stopped. "He had one. For a week. I suppose that's something."

Jonah stood in the road.

Two months. Bit more. He counted back, the way he counted everything now, and it came out to the sixtieth night, or near it, the night he'd answered the Censor in the old tongue without thinking and the old man had said, there, that is the thing behind the endings. He'd been lying on the rock of the Cradle with a pick haft's bruise on the back of his knee and a new language in his mouth and he had been, he remembered it exactly, happy, and on Anvil his father had been sitting in a chair with the door open and his hat on his knee, and had been for some time.

"Thank you," he said to Hollen.

"For what?"

"For saying it. Nobody says it. That's the thing here. Nobody says anything." He picked up the chisel. "Cut a shard a day. Don't make Bricks guess. That's the whole of it."

He went back to the wall.

---

He said the names that night on the road, with the buckets, before the Cradle. He'd said them every night for three months, fourteen and then fifteen and then sixteen, adding one when he had one, the way the rock had said, and the thing under his ribs had come to expect them and did not wander, and he said them now, in the dark between the lamps, and when he got to the first one he said it and stopped.

"Tom Vale."

The thing under his ribs listened.

"My father. Nine fingers. He sold something once for a winter's food and never told me, and I know what it was now, and he never knew. He sat in a chair with the door open." His voice was level. He kept it level. "He's in the wall now. Not this one. Anvil's. The melt. Somebody said his name, Hollen says. I don't know who. I'm going to find out."

He said the rest. All of them. He said Mara, the way he did every night, and this time he saw her coming through the door with a bowl, and he stopped again, and made himself go on.

He said Livia. He'd said her every night since the Cradle and had not, on any of those nights, let himself think about it, because the rock had said, add one when you have one, and she was one he had, and that was all. He said her now and thought, for the first time, resenting it: she's the last person who used my name. Not Vale. Not four four seven one. Jonah. She said Jonah Vale, all right, I'll remember it, and she's on Anvil believing her brother, and my father sat in a chair for two months believing the same man, and neither of them knew.

He put it away. There was a great deal in there now.

The Censor was waiting in the Cradle with the lamp and the stick. He looked at Jonah's face once, in the thread of light, and did not say get up, and did not say anything in the old tongue.

"Who," the old man said.

"My father."

"When."

"Two months. A transport came."

The Censor stood for a moment with the stick on his shoulder. Then he laid it down on the rock, carefully, the way a man puts down a thing he has held for a long time, and sat, and said, "Then not tonight. Tonight, sit. Tell me about him. In whatever tongue you like."

Jonah sat.

He told him. It took the first hour and the second. And in the third, when he had run out, the old man reached into the grey rag that was all the Pit had left him of a robe and took out something flat and dark and laid it on the rock between them in the lamplight, and Jonah saw that it was a slab of the Vein, cut thin, the size of a hand, and that its surface was covered, edge to edge, in a hand as small and even as Marchand's, with numbers.

"Four four seven one," the old man said. He touched the slab. "There. Fourth column. And beside it, because you told the rock, Jonah Vale, Anvil, Morrow's yard. Every man who has come down that Throat in twenty years is on one of these. Sixteen slabs. Most of them have a number and nothing else, because most of them never said. Some have a name. A few have a father." He looked up. "Give me his. All of it. Tom Vale, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder. Say it slowly. I have a great many slabs and my hand is not what it was, and I want it right."

Jonah gave it to him.

The Censor wrote it down with a chisel point, slowly, in the old tongue, beside a number in the fourth column of a slab of the thing that had made the Empire, and when he had finished he read it back, once, aloud, and the endings were right.

"Counted," he said.
