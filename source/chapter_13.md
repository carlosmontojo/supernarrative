# Chapter 13: Chisel

"Triginta unus," the Censor said, and the haft came out of the dark and took Jonah across the back of the knee.

He went down on the rock of the Cradle for the thirty-first time. He lay there. The stick was a pick haft, ash, a metre long, worn pale where a hand had held it for years. In the first hour it had found his knee, his ribs, his ear, his mouth. He tasted blood. The genius had come up in the first minute, ready to put plate on every place the stick touched, and had been told no thirty-one times. It lay under his heart and did not understand any of it.

"Get up."

"You said an hour."

"I said an hour of the hand. That was an hour of you falling down. It is not the same." The Censor stood over him with the haft on his shoulder. He was not breathing hard. He was seventy, or near it, and had eaten one bowl a day for twenty years. "Again. And this time do not step left."

"I didn't step left."

"You stepped left. Thirty-one times. Left and a little forward, off the line, every time the stick comes in low. It is a beautiful step. It is the first step of House Fulvia, and a dead decurion did it very well. It is why you are on the floor. I know it is coming, and I put the stick where you are going to be." The haft came off the shoulder. "Get up, Jonah Vale, and be a man with a hand. Not a man with a decurion in his legs. I do not want the decurion. I want you."

Jonah got up. He spat blood on the rock and put his hands up.

The stick came in low. He stepped left. It took him across the knee.

"Triginta duo."

---

It went on for three months. He would remember it as the happiest time in the Pit, and never decided what that said about him.

The shape of it was simple. One shard a day at the wall, slowly, with Corr beside him and Bricks at the bucket saying nothing. Then two buckets down the Deep road, past Ulli on his bucket at the end of it with his eyes shut, into the Cradle. The rock opened. The old man came out with the lamp and the stick. Three hours: the hand, the tongue, and whatever the old man had decided the third hour was. Then the lamp came down the road, the rock closed, and Jonah carried the buckets to the cage with his legs shaking. Two hours of sleep.

"How do you do it?" he asked, in the second month, from the floor, after the forty-third fall of the night. "Three hours. Every night. You're seventy."

"I have been sitting in a stone box for twenty years, boy. I have had a great deal of time to decide what I would do with my body if anybody ever gave me a reason. You are the reason. Get up."

---

The hand was the first hour. He hated it, and it changed him.

He had thought he could fight. Not well. But he had put a decurion on his back, then a dock thug, then a foreman, with Varro's feet and his own hands. The old man took it away from him one bruise at a time.

He taught nothing, the first month. He stood in the dark with a pick haft and found every single thing Varro had left in Jonah's body, and hit it.

The Fulvian step went first. Left and forward on a low line; the haft was there. Thirty-one times the first night, forty the second. On the ninth night Jonah stepped right, for no reason, because his legs had finally understood that left meant the stick.

"Good," the old man said, for the first time. Then he hit him anyway, because the right step had been bad.

The wrist went second. The turn he had done on Hask, the hinge that only went one way. In the third week the old man let him have the haft's end in both hands, once. Then he turned his whole body through the turn, and it was Jonah on the rock with his own wrist bent back to his elbow.

"You did that to a foreman," the Censor said, from above, without heat. "Slow, on purpose, so Auger would not see what it was. Auger saw. It was a Vibian turn, boy. It is the second thing they teach in that house, and you did it with the weight back and the hip closed, the way a Vibian does it. You could do it at all because the decurion had been beaten by a Vibian once, and his lorica remembered how." He let go. "That is what an echo is. Not skill. A memory of being beaten. You are carrying a dead man's defeats around in your legs and calling them yours."

The stance went third. Livia had seen it at the yard rail, Mara in the square, Auger in the road. The Censor saw it in the first minute and put the haft in the back of his knee until he stopped.

"It is not that it is wrong," the old man said, in the second month. He sat across from Jonah with the lamp low and the stick across his knees. Jonah lay on his back and counted his breaths in the old tongue and waited for the fever to go down. "The step is a good step. The turn is a good turn. If you had learned them from a master over ten years I would be pleased with you. You did not. They came up your arm in a hold, out of a corpse. And folded in with them is the other thing. The thing that looks at Collars."

"Yes."

"When you stepped left on Bricks's chisel, the thing in your legs was looking at Bricks. When you turned Hask's wrist it was looking at Hask. It does not see men. It sees Collars, and it sees them as a decurion of the equestrian order sees them: things you do on your way somewhere else. Every time you let the feet do it, you let that look through your eyes, and you agreed with it. You were right to be frightened."

"So take it away."

"I am not taking it away because it is bad. I am taking it away because it is not yours. You cannot use a dead man's contempt without it using you. When you can do the step because you have chosen to, in your own legs, looking at the man in front of you and seeing a man, then you may have it back. Not before."

What he wanted to say was that the wrist-turn had kept him alive twice already, and giving it up meant giving up the only weapon he had left. What he said cost less.

"How long?" he said.

"As long as it takes. Get up."

He got up.

---

The tongue was the second hour, and it showed him what the old man was.

He had expected a slate and a list, the way the yard school taught letters. The old man had no slate. He had a lamp turned to a thread and a voice. He sat on the rock and spoke. Jonah sat across from him and listened. That was the lesson.

"You will not understand me," the Censor said, the first night, in the common speech. "Not for a month. You will sit there and hear a noise, and you will hate it. You will want me to stop and tell you what the noise means, and I will not. That is how a Broadstripe child learns it. The adults speak it over his head at dinner. By the time he is five he has never once been told what a word means and he knows all of them."

"I'm nineteen."

"You will be worse at it than a child. You have eleven months. Listen."

From then on he did not say a word of the common speech between the first bell of that hour and the last. Jonah sat in the dark and drowned.

It was a noise. An hour of it, without gaps. The old man talked about the wall, Jonah thought, from where his eyes went. Something made his mouth go hard. Something made him laugh once, dry. Something made him stop and look at the lamp a long time before going on. At the end of the second hour the old man said, in the common speech, "Get up." Then he beat him with the stick for the third hour, because Jonah had let his face show that he hated it.

On the twentieth night he understood a word.

Mater. Mother. It had been on the derelict's wall, and he had read it and not known. The old man said it in the middle of the noise, and Jonah heard it as you hear your own name in a crowd. For the length of one sentence the noise on either side of it was almost words. Then it closed over again.

On the thirtieth night he understood a sentence.

On the forty-fifth he understood what the old man had been telling him, every night, for an hour, in a language he did not speak. The history of the College of Flamens, from the Finding to the year 366. He had told it to a boy who could not understand a word, because it was the only way he knew to teach. And because he had had nobody to tell it to for twenty years and did not intend to stop.

On the sixtieth night the Censor said something, and Jonah answered without thinking, in the same tongue, badly, with the endings wrong.

The old man stopped. He looked at him in the thread of lamp. He said, in the common speech, for the first time in a month:

"There. That is the thing behind the endings. Do not lose it."

And then, in the old tongue: "Your endings are a disgrace. Again."

---

He was growing.

Not from eating. The genius had not eaten since the mouthful, had not been let near the wall in front of a man, had not been given so much as a shard. It was eating him instead. Not his flesh; that came later, the old man said, when the hunger got past a certain mark. This was growth. The body used hard, three hours a night on the rock with a stick finding every place Varro had left in him. Every night after, the genius went through the body, reading the new bruises and the new torn muscle. It learned from what it found, and it got bigger.

The fever came every six or seven nights. He counted it down in the old tongue, on the road, with the buckets, and by the second month he did not need to sit.

The trouble was the day.

A fever that came at the end of the third hour was still in him at the dawn count. Decimus said his number and looked at his face. Corr looked at his face too.

"You're going again," Corr said, in the second month. Not unkindly. "I can see it. You sweat and your hands shake. You cut the way a new man cuts. That's the Vein. It let you go once. It doesn't let go twice."

What he wanted to say was that he had already turned around once and had no plans to volunteer for a rematch. What he said was shorter, and true anyway.

"I turned around."

"Nobody turns around. You did something, cut one, I don't know what, and it won't work twice. When they carry you down that road again I'm going to say a month, and I'm going to be wrong the other way." Corr went back to his line. "I'm sorry. I like you. You don't talk."

That was the cover. Jonah told the old man that night, in bad Latin. The old man answered in the same tongue, slowly.

"Yes. Let them think it. A man the Vein is taking sweats and shakes and cuts badly. One day he is carried down the road and does not come back. You will sweat and shake and cut badly for six years. You will be the longest dying anyone in this Pit has ever seen, and nobody will ever look closer. Nobody looks closely at a dying man. It is impolite." A pause. "It is also the only thing in this rock that is free. Use it."

---

The Forms came in the third month, and they came as names.

On the ninetieth night the first hour ended with Jonah on the rock for the eleventh time, the fewest yet. The Censor did not say get up. He sat down across from him with the stick on his knees and said, in the old tongue, "You have stopped stepping left."

"Nine nights."

"Nine nights. And you have stopped closing your hip on the turn. You stand as a beaten man stands, which is what you are, and not as a decurion stands, which is what you were. The echo is not gone. It will never be gone. But it is in your hands now instead of your legs, and your hands are yours." He laid the stick flat on the rock between them. "So. Four names. You will see all four before you die, and you will fight at least two."

He touched the stick at the end nearest Jonah. One tap.

"Vibia. The house of the man who burned your seal. The house of the girl with the cup. They hold a gift called Verdict, which stops any lorica in its sight for one heartbeat, and their Form is built on the heartbeat. They do not move. They wait, and let you come. Half a step into whatever you have decided to do, you stop, because they have stopped you. Their fang is in your throat seam before your heart beats again. Thrust. Stillness. Patience. You will know a Vibian because he is the only man on the floor who is not moving, and he is already looking at the place where he will put the blade."

Jonah thought of a man behind a table, reading to the end of a page, and said nothing.

"Claudia." A tap, further along. "The Princeps' house. They hold the Wall, which is plate that does not shed, ever, under anything. Their Form is a wall coming toward you. Grind. Absorb. Take the cut on the plate and step in. Take the next, and step in. You cannot beat a Claudian on the plate. You beat him on the feet, or not at all. Nobody has beaten the Princeps on the feet in forty years."

"And the ones without the gift?"

"Clients. Hangers-on. They fight the Claudian Form without the Wall, and they are dangerous and they are liars. A wall that can shed is not a wall, it is a man pretending, and a man pretending is a man who can be made to hurry."

"Fulvia." A tap. "Tide. Speed that climbs. The longer the bout goes, the faster they are, and they know it. So they circle, and cut you a little. Circle, and cut you a little. In the fourth minute they are twice the man they were in the first, and you are bleeding from six places and cannot see them. Your decurion fought a Fulvian Form badly, from a manual. He had the step and not the patience. The step is the first thing they teach and the only thing most of them ever learn."

"Aemilia." The last tap, at the far end. "Mirror. They throw. They stand off and cast plate at you, shards, and if you throw anything back it comes back at you faster. They are dishonest and they are effective. You will not fight an Aemilian on a floor. You will fight one across a room, if you are unlucky. The only answer is to close, and the whole Form is built to stop you closing."

He picked up the stick.

"Four houses. Four Forms. There are three hundred others, but they are all one of these four with the name changed. The Blank garrisons fight a Fulvian step and a Vibian turn and call it drill. Everyone in the Empire who has ever held a fang has held it in one of four ways." The pale eyes were steady in the lamp. "And there was a fifth."

Jonah waited.

"Not tonight. Tonight you know four names. Say them."

"Vibia. Claudia. Fulvia. Aemilia."

"In the old tongue."

He said them in the old tongue.

"Your endings," the Censor said, "are still a disgrace. Get up."

---

The news came on the ninety-fourth night, up the Throat on a transport.

Eleven new men in the cage, and one of them Ferrum. Jonah heard it before he saw him: the flat yard vowels of the north side of Anvil, said to the guard at the cage door. Forty paces down the road, Jonah put his chisel down.

The man's name was Hollen. Twenty years a cutter in the north yards, and he had put a torch through a Blank foreman's hand in a fight over scrip. He stood in the road with a new shackle and a new number. Jonah walked up the road.

"Vale. South yards. Morrow's."

Hollen's face changed.

"You're the one. Tom's boy. They took you off a dinner."

"Yes."

"Whole moon heard. Vigiles at the door, five of them, and the quaestor's seal on it, and nobody knew why. Then you were gone and nobody knew where." His eyes went to the white line on Jonah's arm and stayed there. "That's the line. They cut it out."

"Tell me about Tom."

Hollen was quiet a moment. Then he told it, flat, in the vowels of the north side, with sixty men listening and pretending not to.

"He went to the quaestor's house the morning after. And the morning after that. Every morning for a month. Stood at the door with his hat in his hand, got told nothing, went home. Then the yard office. The Flamens' house. The dock, where the transports go out. Asked every grey Blank who'd stand still where the boy had gone. Got told nothing." Hollen looked at the road. "Then he stopped going anywhere. Sat in his two rooms with the door open. People brought him food. The Ansel girl. Morrow's people. Roake, the neighbour, sat with him some nights. He ate some of it and not the rest. In the winter he stopped eating the rest."

"When."

"Winter. Two months back. Bit more. One morning the Ansel girl came with a bowl, and he was in his chair with the door open and his hat on his knee." Hollen looked at him again. "Morrow's yard paid the melt. Somebody said his name. I wasn't there. I'm north side."

"Who said it?"

"Don't know. Somebody. That's what they told me." The cutter stopped. "I'm sorry. He was a good man. Everybody said. He believed in it. The ladder. He used to say it in the Halyard, when he'd had one. My boy's going to have a line." A pause. "He had one. For a week. I suppose that's something."

Jonah stood in the road.

Two months. Bit more. He counted back, and it came out to the sixtieth night, or near it. The night he had answered the Censor in the old tongue without thinking. He had been happy. He remembered it exactly. On Anvil his father had been sitting in a chair with the door open and his hat on his knee, and had been for some time.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For saying it plain, and staying to say it. Nobody down here says anything," Jonah said. He picked up the chisel. "Cut a shard a day. Don't make Bricks guess. That's the whole of it."

He went back to the wall.

---

He said the names that night on the road, with the buckets, before the Cradle. Sixteen now. The genius had come to expect them and did not wander. He got to the first one and stopped.

"Tom Vale."

The genius listened.

"My father. Nine fingers. He sold something once for a winter's food and never told me. I know what it was now. He never knew. He sat in a chair with the door open." He kept his voice level. "He's in the wall now. Not this one. Anvil's. The melt. Somebody said his name, Hollen says. I don't know who. I'm going to find out."

He said the rest. All of them. He said Mara, and this time he saw her coming through the door with a bowl. He stopped again, and made himself go on.

He said Livia. He had said her every night since the Cradle without letting himself think about it. He thought about it now, and resented it. She was the last person who had used his name. Not Vale. Not four four seven one. Jonah. She said, Jonah Vale, all right, I'll remember it. She was on Anvil believing her brother. His father had sat in a chair for two months believing the same man, and neither of them knew.

He put it away. There was a great deal in there now.

The Censor was waiting in the Cradle with the lamp and the stick. He looked at Jonah's face once, in the thread of light. He did not say get up.

"Who," the old man said.

"My father."

"When."

"Two months. A transport came."

The Censor stood a moment with the stick on his shoulder. Then he laid it on the rock, carefully. He sat down.

"Then not tonight. Tonight, sit. Tell me about him. In whatever tongue you like."

Jonah sat.

He told him. It took the first hour and the second. In the third, when he had run out, the old man reached into his rag of a robe and took out something flat and dark. He laid it on the rock between them. A slab of the Vein, cut thin, the size of a hand. Its surface was covered edge to edge in a hand as small and even as Marchand's, with numbers.

"Four four seven one." The old man touched the slab. "There. Fourth column. And beside it, because you told the rock: Jonah Vale, Anvil, Morrow's yard. Every man who has come down that Throat in twenty years is on one of these. Sixteen slabs. Most have a number and nothing else, because most never said. Some have a name. A few have a father." He looked up. "Give me his. All of it. Tom Vale, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder. Say it slowly. I have a great many slabs and my hand is not what it was, and I want it right."

Jonah gave it to him.

The Censor wrote it with a chisel point, slowly, in the old tongue, beside a number in the fourth column of a slab of the thing that had made the Empire. When he had finished he read it back, once, aloud, and the endings were right.

"Counted," he said.

"That's not enough," he said. "Not for him."

"No," the Censor said. "It never is. It is what the wall can hold. The rest you carry yourself."
