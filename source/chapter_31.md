# Chapter 31: The Coffin

Harrow lidded him.

He had not asked. He had found the cask by his hands and put his leg over the rim and gone in feet first into the ring, blind, with the letter and the half stick against his chest, and lain down, and had been lying in the hollow with seventy slabs standing round him trying to work out how a man puts two slabs over a gap from underneath when he heard the foreman's boots at the turn.

They came slow. They came the way Harrow came to everything, afraid all the way through and coming anyway, and they stopped at the third row, and Jonah felt him stand there in the dark with no lamp, looking at a cask with its lid off and a man in it who could not see him.

"Decimus," Harrow said.

"Yes."

"And the Centurion."

"Yes."

The foreman did not say anything for a moment. Then Jonah heard him bend, and heard the scrape of the two slabs coming up off the rock where he had left them on the seventeenth, and they went over the gap, and sat on the ring, and did not sit on him.

"I knocked the rows," Harrow said, through the slabs. "I heard the road go quiet and I lay there and then I got up, because I knock the rows. That's all I did. I came down to knock the rows and thirty-one was open and I lidded it, because a cask with its lid off sings wrong."

"Yes."

"And at the count. When Tullus comes down with the lamp and finds what's on the road." Harrow's voice did not change. "I say four four seven one walked into the Cradle. After. Blind, with his hand on the wall. The way Decimus said he would. He'd earned it, Decimus said; let him walk in himself. I say I saw him go in and I didn't follow, because that's not mine." He was quiet. "That's what I've got. That's less."

"It's enough."

"The Flamen's gone up the Throat. There's nobody to seal it." The foreman's hand was on the lid; Jonah felt it through the ring. "Rennick counts lids at dawn. Forty sealed. He'll not look close, he's never looked close, but he counts, and thirty-one's got no mark, and a cask with no mark doesn't go up."

"It'll have one."

Harrow did not ask. He had asked for less and he was keeping it. He put the lid down, and it found the groove, and Jonah lay in the whole dark of the Vein with the cask shut over him for the second time in a day and put his right hand up, flat, against the underside of the lid.

He had drilled it on the rock of the Cradle. Four tries; it had come on the fourth, on the night of the tenth day of the harvest, the knot of closed lines that said this is shut, and he had drilled it thirty times a night since, the way he had drilled the Master's seal for a year, until it came from his palm before he finished asking. He asked. He asked the way he had asked the shackle and the lock and the plate over his throat, in the old tongue, without words, and the thing in the doorway of him, which was forty-one and unsettled and blind and had a Centurion's cutting in it and a guard's count gone through it, put the shape out through his palm into the lid.

It went in.

He felt it go in the way he had felt Pollio's mark go in and come apart, and it did not come apart. It went into the dark glass of the lid and closed, because it was true. A man had shut a lid over himself and said, this is shut, and it was, and there was nothing in the shape that was a lie, because he had not yet asked to be held and was not yet matter and was only a man in a box with his hand on the roof of it. The lines closed. They closed from the inside, from underneath, and he knew, feeling them close, that a Flamen who looked at the mark from above would see a knot that was the wrong way round, the way a word is wrong in a mirror, and that nobody would look, because nobody ever had.

"It's marked," he said.

Harrow's knuckle came down on the side of the cask, once, low.

Jonah did not hear the sound it made. The cask was the wall and did not carry it. But he felt the foreman stand there in the dark after, for the length of a breath, with his hand on the glass, and felt him take it away.

"The north forty," Harrow said.

"Never made quota."

The boots went up the gallery, slow, and stopped at the turn, and went on, and were gone.

---

He lay in the dark and did not ask yet.

He had until dawn. He did not know how long that was; he could not see the lamps and there were no bells in the loading gallery and the only count he had was his own heart, which was going the way it had gone on the derelict on the first night, fast, wrong, with a fever behind it that was white where his eyes had been. Forty-one. Nine grades he had not slept in. The old man had said it would come a day at a time and he had nine days of it standing in him at once, and it was not pain; he had had pain, on the nineteenth day and in the cut and on the road, and this was not that. It was the feeling of a coat that was nine sizes too big being pulled on over everything he was, all at once, and not fitting, and not going to fit, until it did.

He said the names.

He said them the way he said them every night, in the old tongue, in the dark, in order, and when he came to the end of them he said the two from tonight, and stopped, and said them again, because the old man had said that a name said twice on the first night stayed, and he wanted them to stay.

Gnaeus Sabinus. Client of the Princeps' house. He washed out because he could not bear to grind, and went to the Belt with a licence, and harvested men on a road for nine years, and he came down the Throat alone with the fang out because a Blank had put steel in his seam and he had thought about it every night since, and he sent thirty rifles away to have a fight that was not a harvest, and he had one. He said oh.

Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father. He said my number seven years and it was never personal, and he said please, and he came down the road slow with his rifle down to say the number first, and got as far as the breath.

"That's the count," he said, to the dark. "It starts with you and it goes through him and it ends there. For tonight."

And then, because it was the only room he had and it was the Vein and the wall knew what a name was for, he said the other thing, the thing he had never said to it, the thing Ulli had said with his eyes shut against the warm dark of the Cradle. He said, in the old tongue, in a voice that came out of a throat that had had a fang in it three hours ago:

"I'm not ready."

The thing in the doorway listened.

"I'm not. He was. Ullan. He'd chosen the day nine years and he sat down and let go and it was his. I've not chosen anything. I've had it all chosen, a slate, a count, a cask, a road, and I've done what the count said and it's come out where the count said, and there's a man on the road who said please." He put his hand flat on the slab beside him. "I'm going to ask you to hold me. Now. Before the cage. Thirty days, forty, a hold with a crew that skims, and you'll do it, because you do what I ask and you do it well, and I'll be matter, and it'll not be like anything, and I'll come out the other end of it at forty-one settled with the fever gone and a letter on my chest, and I'm telling you before I ask that I'm not ready, so that you'll know I said it. So it's said."

It did the small thing. The hand with no hand.

"I know," he said. "I know. Now."

And it closed on his heart.

---

It was not like the first time.

He would try to tell that, later, to the one person, and would get closer than he had with the first, because the first had been nothing and this was not nothing. The first time it had held him the way the wall held, carefully, without letting him wear, and he had been a slab in a ring, from the outside, asleep. This time he was a slab in a ring with nine grades unsettled in him, and the settling did not stop because he was matter. It went on. It went on in the dark at the bottom of the well where the thing with a name held him, a day a grade, the way the old man had said, except that there were no days, and so it went the way a thing goes when there is nothing to measure it by, which is all at once and forever, and in the middle of it, held, he dreamed.

Not dreamed. He did not have the word. Matter does not dream. But the settling went through the whole of him, the way the cold had on the nineteenth day, and where it went it turned things over, the way Tacitus turned a thing over when it was interested, and the things it turned over were his, and he was there, from the outside, and saw them.

Anvil.

The tenements, and the vent, and a blanket over it, and his father's hands, which had been a rigger's hands and then a clerk's and then nothing, sitting on the edge of a bunk with a form on them that a man could not sign because there was no man to sign it to. Tom Vale. The best night of his life, he had said, once, about a night in a yard when a barge had come in under his hands, and then, on the last day, son, and that was all, and Jonah had put it with the rest and it had weighed for seven years and it weighed now, held, in the dark, and he turned it over and looked at it, and it was a man who had sold a set of numbers for a winter's food and had not known what they were, and had known, and had been happy the night his son answered in Latin.

Mara at the dock gate. Not the last time; the last time he had come through it on his own feet, with her hair tied back for the yards and burn marks on both forearms from the torches, smiling with her whole face, coming at him to put her arms round him the way she always did, and the step he had taken back. His body had done it. He had never told her why, and she had gone careful behind the smile, and had stayed careful, and had been right to.

The Kestrel's yoke in his hands. Not the fight. The flying. The way the barge had answered, the way a thing answers that has been waiting a long time to be asked, and Ochoa at his shoulder saying nothing, which was the highest thing Ochoa said.

A man in a quaestor's house saying, a name is not a mark, with the whole of a broad stripe in his voice, and a woman beside him who had asked what he was called, looking at him for longer than a number takes.

A stick across his ribs.

The stick. Every night for five years. He had thought, on the rock, that he would remember the beatings, and he did not; he remembered the old man's face doing the thing with the grief and the other thing under it, and the one time the other thing was on top, and the laugh, cracked and high and going on, and blood on the lip, and the old man saying, Sertorius, that was Sertorius, do you understand what you did, and Jonah saying, I put my hand where you'd step, and the old man saying, no. You put your hand where I was going. I have been going there for twenty years.

Ullan on the fourth verse.

Decimus on the deck of the Kestrel, the first night, with a list, reading a number and looking up and saying, Vale, and then, please. The only guard who said it. He had put that with the rest on the first night and he took it out now, held, at the bottom of a well, and looked at it, and it was a man of fifty-three with a daughter of nine on Etruria and a tree, being polite to a Collar on a deck because it cost nothing and because he had decided, at some point in a life of reading numbers, that he would be, and had never once stopped, and had been, on the road, with his rifle down, at the end.

That was the one that stayed.

He would tell her that. He would say, I was matter for thirty-one days in a cask on a freighter, held, with a Centurion's cutting settling into me a grade at a time, and I turned everything I had over in the dark the way a man goes through a box, and the thing I had at the end of it was an old guard saying please, and she would say, why that, and he would say: because it was the only thing anyone gave me on that rock that I didn't have to earn.

---

He felt the cage.

Not with a body. Through the ring, through the cask, through forty casks going up the Throat one at a time on a hopper cage, the way he had felt the lift on the nineteenth: the deep men's hands, and the walk, and the cage, and the pull of it, up, past the Shallows, past the seventh chamber where a man was cutting a line for six that was five, past the Throat and the slab and the table and the place where a Flamen had cut his collar, and out.

Cold.

He had not felt it in six years. Not the Vein's cold, the cold in the middle of him; the other one, the surface, the wind that came up the Throat and had never once reached the road. It came through the cask. It came through seventy slabs and a lid with a mark the wrong way round and found him at the bottom of a well and he knew it, from the outside, for what it was, and it was the first thing that had been outside in six years, and it was cold, and it was the sky.

Then the dock. The scales; he felt the cask go on and come off and knew, without a body, that it had weighed what the slate said, because Harrow packed right. Then hands that were not deep men's, gloved, quick, a crew's hands that had loaded casks before and did not care what was in them, and a hold, and the casks going into it in rows, forty, and the hatch, and the thing a ship does when it lets go of a rock.

The Heron. He had the name later. He did not have it then. He had a hull, and the sound a hull makes through the Vein's glass, which is not a sound, and a crew's boots through the hull ring, eight, and two that were plated, and a long dark in which the settling went on, a grade, a grade, a grade, and the coat that was nine sizes too big pulled in, a size at a time, and fitted.

He did not know how many days. He would count them back from the other end: thirty-one. He was told.

And on a day in the middle of them, in the whole dark at the bottom of the well, with the eighth grade settling and the ninth not yet, and the things he had turned over lying where he had left them, and his heart held in a thing's hands that had no hands, the thing spoke.

He would never be able to say what that was like either, and would not try.

It was not a sound. There was no sound in him; he was matter. It was not the old tongue, and it was not the road's, and it was not the noise from the sixtieth night. It was the thing that happens in a man's head when he reads a word, before he has said it, the word arriving whole, from nowhere, in his own voice, except that it was not his voice. It was smaller. It was very old. It had never been used, and it knew it had never been used, the way a man knows the first time he has put weight on a leg that was broken, and it put the weight on, once, carefully, in the middle of him, in the place where the seam had been and was a scar, and it was one word, and the word was:

Name.

He did not answer. He could not. He was matter, held, and a slab does not speak, and he lay at the bottom of the well and felt it wait, the way it had waited under a glass, listening, polite, and he understood, from a very long way off, that it was not asking for its own. It had that. He had said it in the only room in the Pit that knew what a name was for, and it had turned all the way round and stayed. It was asking the other thing. The thing he had asked the wall in the Mouth. The thing Ulli had asked a rock through a wall once, the only thing he ever asked. It had felt a man go through it on the road, all of him, the whole count, a guard with his rifle down, because it had not been asked to stand between, and it had had that in it for thirty days, turning it over, interested, and it had no word for what it was, and it wanted one, and it had used the only word it had.

Name.

He gave it.

Not aloud. He had no mouth. He gave it the way he gave the names every night, in order, in the old tongue, and this time it was one, said twice because it was the first night and he wanted it to stay:

Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father.

The thing in the doorway took it.

He felt it take it the way it had taken the seal shape from Pollio's rod through his palm, with the whole of its attention, and turn it over, and put it somewhere, and it was the first thing it had ever put anywhere that was not matter and not a shape and not a want. It was a man. It had a man in it now, that it had let go through, and the man had a name, and the name was the first word it had ever said, and it would learn, he thought, from a long way off, everything else from that. It would learn the world through names. He had told it the wall's and the old man's and its own, and it had never once said anything back, and it had said one word, and the word was a request, and he had answered it, and it was quiet.

It was quiet a long time.

Then it did the small thing, the hand with no hand, in the dark, at the bottom of a well, to a slab.

And he understood that it had not been asking for the guard's name.

It had known the guard's name. He had said it on the road with his hand on the shoulder and again with his back to the wall and again in the cask, twice, and it had heard all of them, because it heard everything. It had not asked what the man was called. It had asked, with the only word it had, what the thing was called that had gone through it on the road when the man went, and that was in it now, and that it turned over and could not put down.

He did not have a word for it either.

He had not had one for six years. He had put it with the rest, every time, the way he put everything, and had never once taken it out and looked at what it was called, and he lay at the bottom of a well as matter with a thing's hands on his heart and thought, it wants a name for grief, and it's asked me, and I've given it a man's, because that's all I've got. That's all I've ever had. Names. I don't have the other one. Nobody gave it to me.

He gave it what he had.

The count. All of it, from the top, in the old tongue, Tacitus first and then the old man and then the rest, in order, every name, the ones he knew and the ones he had a line for that said there was a thing and it was theirs, six hundred and forty-two from the slabs and the ones from the road and the two from tonight, and he said them at the bottom of a well without a mouth to a thing that had asked him one word, and it listened to all of them, the way it listened to everything, and when he came to the end he said his own number, and answered it, and stopped.

"That's the name," he said. "That's the only one I've got for it. It's called that. It's called all of them."

The thing in the doorway did not say anything.

But the ninth grade settled.

He felt it go in, the last of it, the coat pulling in the last size and fitting, everywhere, right, and the fever behind his eyes that had been white for thirty days went out, the way a lamp goes out, and behind it there was dark, ordinary dark, the dark a man sees, and he was forty-one, and settled, and held, and matter, and somewhere above him, through the ring and the cask and the hull, a boot came down on the deck of a hold, and a knuckle came down on the side of a cask, once, low, the way Harrow's did.

Not Harrow's.

It knocked thirty. It knocked thirty-one.

It stopped.

"This one sings wrong," a voice said, through the glass, through the ring, in the road's tongue with a Belt turn on it that was not Tessera's. "Get the bar."
