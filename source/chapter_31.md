# Chapter 31: The Coffin

Harrow lidded him.

Jonah had not asked. He had found the cask by his hands, put his leg over the rim and gone in feet first, blind, with the letter and the half stick against his chest. He lay in the hollow with seventy slabs standing round him. Two slabs were still on the rock outside, where he had left them on the seventeenth, and he could not work out how a man puts two slabs over a gap from underneath.

Then boots at the turn. Slow ones. Harrow's.

They stopped at the third row. No lamp. Jonah felt the foreman stand there in the dark, looking at a cask with its lid off and a man in it who could not see him.

"Decimus," Harrow said.

"Yes."

"And the Centurion."

"Yes."

Harrow said nothing for a moment. Then he bent. The two slabs scraped up off the rock. The first went over the gap and sat on the ring. The second came down crooked and caught the back of Jonah's hand against the rim. He pulled the hand out from under it and the slab dropped into place. Skin off two knuckles. He put the hand back on his chest.

"I knocked the rows," Harrow said, through the slabs. "I heard the road go quiet and I lay there. Then I got up, because I knock the rows. That's all I did. I came down to knock the rows and thirty-one was open. I lidded it, because a cask with its lid off sings wrong."

"Yes."

"And at the count. When Tullus comes down with the lamp and finds what's on the road." Harrow's voice did not change. "I say four four seven one walked into the Cradle. After. Blind, with his hand on the wall. Decimus said he'd earned it; let him walk in himself. I say I saw him go in and I didn't follow, because that's not mine." A pause. "That's what I've got. That's less."

"It's enough."

"The Flamen's gone up the Throat. There's nobody to seal it." Harrow's hand was on the lid; Jonah felt it through the ring. "Rennick counts lids at dawn. Forty sealed. He's never looked close, but he counts. Thirty-one's got no mark, and a cask with no mark doesn't go up."

"It'll have one."

Harrow did not ask. He had asked for less and he was keeping it. He put the lid down, and it found the groove. Jonah lay in the dark with the cask shut over him for the second time in a day. He put his right hand up, flat, against the underside of the lid.

He had drilled the mark on the rock of the Cradle. It had come on the fourth try, on the tenth night of the harvest: the knot of closed lines that said this is shut. Thirty times a night since. Now he asked for it, in the old tongue, without words. Tacitus was forty-one and unsettled and blind. It had a Centurion's cutting in it and a guard's count gone through it. It put the shape out through his palm into the lid.

It went in.

Pollio's mark had gone in too, and come apart. This one closed, because it was true. A man had shut a lid over himself and said, this is shut, and it was. He had not asked to be held yet. He was not matter yet. He was only a man in a box with his hand on the roof of it, and nothing in the shape was a lie. The lines closed from underneath. A Flamen looking down at the lid would see a knot the wrong way round, mirror-written. Nobody would look. Nobody ever had.

"It's marked," he said.

Harrow's knuckle came down on the side of the cask, once, low.

The cask did not carry the sound in. But Jonah felt the foreman stand there in the dark for the length of a breath with his hand on the glass, and felt him take it away.

"The north forty," Harrow said.

"Never made quota."

The boots went up the gallery, slow. They stopped at the turn, went on, and were gone.

---

He lay in the dark and did not ask yet.

He had until dawn, and no way to count it. No lamps. No bells in the loading gallery. Only his heart, going fast and wrong, with a white fever behind it where his eyes had been. Forty-one. Nine grades he had not slept in. The old man had said they would come a day at a time. He had nine days of them standing in him at once. It was not pain. It was a coat nine sizes too big, pulled on over everything he was. It did not fit yet.

There were two more tonight, and he had killed both of them himself about three hours ago, and he found he could not go to sleep without saying so out loud.

"Gnaeus Sabinus. Client of the Princeps' house. Washed out because he couldn't bear to grind. Went to the Belt with a licence and harvested men on a road for nine years. Came down the Throat alone with the fang out, because a Blank had put steel in his seam once and he'd thought about it every night since. Sent thirty rifles away so he could have a fight instead of a harvest. He got one. He said oh."

"Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father. Said my number seven years and it was never personal. Said please. Came down the road slow with his rifle down to say the number first, and got as far as the breath."

Jonah said, to the dark, "That's the count. It starts with you and it goes through him and it ends there. For tonight." He was quiet a moment. "You'd think a man who talks as much as I do would have more to say to the one thing that's listened to all of it. I don't. Just that."

Then the last thing, which he had not said to anyone in six years. His throat had had a fang through it at dusk. It came out rough.

"I'm not ready."

Tacitus listened.

Jonah said, "I'm not. He was. Ullan. He'd chosen the day nine years. He sat down and let go and it was his. I've not chosen anything. It's all been chosen for me. A slate, a count, a cask, a road. I did what the count said and it came out where the count said. There's a man on the road who said please." He put his hand flat on the slab beside him. "I'm going to ask you to hold me. Now. Before the cage. Thirty days, forty, a hold with a crew that skims. You'll do it well, because you always do. I'll be matter. I'll come out the other end at forty-one settled with the fever gone and a letter on my chest. I'm telling you first that I'm not ready. So it's said."

The hand with no hand.

"I know," he said. "I know. Now."

It closed on his heart.

---

It was not like the first time.

The first time it had held him carefully, a slab in a ring, asleep from the outside. This time there were nine grades unsettled in him, and the settling did not stop because he was matter. It went on inside the hold, a day a grade, except there were no days. At the bottom of the well there was nothing to measure it by, so it went all at once and forever.

Held, he did not dream. Matter does not dream. But the settling went through the whole of him, and where it went it turned his things over. He was there, from the outside, and saw them.

Anvil. The tenements, the vent with a blanket over it. His father sitting on the edge of a bunk with a form on his knees that could not be signed, because there was nobody to sign it to. Tom Vale had sold a set of numbers for a winter's food. He had not known what they were, and he had known. He had been happy the night his son answered in Latin.

Mara at the dock gate. Not the last time. The time before, hair tied back for the yards, burn marks on both forearms from the torches, smiling with her whole face and coming at him to put her arms round him. And the step he had taken back. His body had done it. He never told her why. She went careful behind the smile, and she stayed careful, and she was right to.

A man in a quaestor's house saying, a name is not a mark, with a broad stripe in his voice. Beside him a woman who had asked what he was called, and had looked at him for longer than a number takes.

Decimus on the deck of the Kestrel, the first night, with a list. Reading a number, looking up, saying Vale, and then, please. The only guard who ever said it. A man of fifty-three with a daughter of nine and a tree on Etruria, being polite to a Collar because it cost nothing. He had decided once, somewhere in a life of reading numbers, that he would be, and had never stopped. He had been, on the road, with his rifle down, at the end.

Other things went past. The Kestrel's yoke answering under his hands. The stick across his ribs, and the old man saying, I have been going there for twenty years. Ullan on the fourth verse.

The please was the one that stayed.

He would tell her that, later, the one person. Thirty-one days as matter, a Centurion's cutting settling into him a grade at a time, everything he had turned over in the dark. She would ask what he kept. He would say: an old guard saying please. Because it was the only thing anyone gave me on that rock that I didn't have to earn.

---

He felt the cage.

Not with a body. Through the ring, through the cask, through forty casks going up the Throat on the hopper cage one at a time. The deep men's hands. The pull of the lift. Past the Shallows, past the seventh chamber where a man was cutting a line for six that was five. Past the Throat and the slab. Past the table where a Flamen had cut his collar. Out.

Cold.

Not the Vein's cold. The other one, the surface wind that came up the Throat and had never once reached the road. It came through seventy slabs and a lid with a backward mark, and found him. It was the first thing from outside in six years. It was the sky.

Then the dock. The scales; the cask went on and came off, and it weighed what the slate said, because Harrow packed right. Then gloved hands, quick, a crew's hands, men who had loaded casks before and did not care what was in them. A hold, and forty casks going into it in rows. The hatch. The lurch a ship gives when it lets go of a rock.

The Heron. He had the name later. Then he had only a hull, and crew boots coming through the hull ring, eight, and two that were plated. A long dark. The settling went on, a grade, a grade, a grade. The coat pulled in a size at a time.

Thirty-one days. He counted them back afterward, when he was told.

On a day in the middle of them, with the eighth grade settling and the ninth not yet, Tacitus spoke.

It was not a sound. He was matter; there was no sound in him. It was not the old tongue, and not the road's, and not the noise from the sixtieth night. It was a word arriving whole in his own head, in a voice that was not his. Smaller. Very old. Never used, and knowing it had never been used, the way a man knows the first time he puts weight on a leg that was broken. It put the weight on once, carefully, in the scar where the seam had been.

Name.

He could not answer. A slab does not speak. He lay held and felt it wait, polite, listening, as it had waited under the glass. He understood, from a long way off, that it was not asking for its own name. It had that. He had said it in the only room in the Pit that knew what a name was for, and it had turned all the way round and stayed.

It was asking about the road. It had felt a man go through it there, all of him, a guard with his rifle down, because it had not been asked to stand between. It had carried that for thirty days, turning it over, interested. It had no word for it. It had used the only word it had.

Name.

He gave it one. Not aloud; he had no mouth. In the old tongue, in order, and twice, because it was the first night and he wanted it to stay.

Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father.

Tacitus took it.

He felt it take the name with its whole attention, and turn it over, and put it somewhere. It had never put anything anywhere before that was not matter, or a shape, or a want. This was a man. It had let a man go through it, and now it had a man in it, and the man's name was the first word it had ever said. It would learn the world through names. He had given it the wall's name, the old man's, its own. It had never said one word back. Now it had said one, and the word was a request, and he had answered it.

It was quiet a long time.

Then the hand with no hand, in the dark, to a slab.

And he understood that it had not been asking for the guard's name.

It knew the guard's name. He had said it on the road with his hand on the shoulder, and again with his back to the wall, and twice in the cask. It heard everything. It had asked, with its one word, what to call the thing that had gone through it when the man went. That was in it now, and it could not put it down.

He had no word for that either.

He had not had one in six years. He had put it away every time, with everything else, and never once got it out to see what it was called.

So he told it the truth, which was all he had.

It's called grief, he thought, and I don't know any more about it than you do. It doesn't go. You don't put it down. You carry it and you get stronger, and one day you notice it weighs the same and you've stopped noticing, and that's as good as it gets. I'm sorry. Nobody told me either.

Tacitus said nothing.

But the ninth grade settled.

He felt it go in, the last of it. The coat pulled in the last size and fitted, everywhere, right. The white fever went out behind his eyes, and behind it was ordinary dark, the dark a man sees. Forty-one, settled. Held. Matter.

Somewhere above him a boot came down on the deck of a hold. A knuckle came down on the side of a cask, once, low, the way Harrow's did.

Not Harrow's.

It knocked thirty. It knocked thirty-one.

It stopped.

"This one sings wrong," a voice said, through the glass, in the road's tongue with a Belt turn on it that was not Tessera's. "Get the bar."
