# Chapter 36: Mara

"I'm going, I'm going. I've a bad leg. Give a man a minute."

"You've had your minute, friend."

"I've had a minute and a half. It's forty paces to my stair, Corin. You know it is. You've climbed it."

Jonah stood at the rail with his hood up and did not move.

The man in the road was Bram. Jonah had heard that heart through a bunk wall for ten years. It was going fast now. The voice had always been too loud for a tenement stair, and it carried down the whole dock road to the rail.

The road ran up from the rail to the tenement stairs between the cutting sheds. The sun-lamps were at their night setting. Twelve auxiliaries were coming down it in a line with their fangs out, grey with the black band, plate on their forearms. Doors shut ahead of them. In the middle of the road, alone, going slow, was a man of fifty with a torch-burned face and a bad leg. A hull plate had come down on that leg on the day Jonah turned fifteen. Jonah had held the other end.

Corin was at the head of the line.

He had a stripe. Thin, purple, a real one, sewn on the grey where the band had been. His plate was not thin. Fourteen grades of it, six years of cutting fed on a rising. He walked at the head of twelve men the way a Thinstripe walked. His voice was careful. It said sir to men who did not deserve it and friend to men who were not.

"Forty paces is a long way after the bell, Bram. You know the order. Any Collar on the dock road after curfew is out for the war. Out for the war's a sharpening."

The line stopped behind him.

"I'll not do it. You carried me home once. But there's eleven behind me who don't know you, and they've a quota."

"A quota."

"Two a night. It's the legate's. Keeps the docks quiet." Corin's voice did not change. "Go on. I'll count slow."

"Count how you like. You've a stripe. Nobody's going to check your sums."

"Walk, Bram."

Bram walked. He could not go fast.

Jonah counted the eleven from the rail. Nine hearts steady. Two quick. Two boys with a fang, a band and a quota, who had done the sum and liked the answer. They came out of the line past Corin at the pace of men who have decided.

"Dace," Corin said, without turning.

"Two a night, sir." Dace kept walking.

Bram heard the boots. He turned round on his bad leg in the middle of the road with the lamps on him, forty paces from his stair. Two auxiliaries with fangs out. His face understood it. He said it loud. He said everything loud, and it carried to the rail.

"Tom Vale's boy would've had you both."

The first fang went across his face. The flat of it. Not into. Across, the garrison stroke for a Collar you meant to take your time with. Bram went down with his hands up. The second auxiliary put a boot on his bad leg.

Corin stood with the other nine and looked at the sheds.

Jonah left the rail.

---

He had counted it with the bell going. Twelve auxiliaries. One stripe with Lock in his hand. Two with a quota. Lamps on the road, a hood on him, a captain three days from lying to a College dock who had said don't put your plate up on Anvil. And Bram on the rock with a fang across his face, saying his father's name.

The sum came out the same every time.

He came up the road at a walk. A man running at twelve auxiliaries was a man with a fang out. A man walking was a Halyard crewman going home after the bell. The nine in the line looked at a hood and did nothing. The road was theirs. Corin looked, then went back to watching the sheds.

Dace looked up from Bram. "Curfew, friend."

"I heard the bell," Jonah said. "And I heard your fang land on that man's face before I was close enough to see the band. You're new at this. I can tell."

"Then you'll know what you're out for." Dace straightened. Twenty-five. Blood on the flat of his fang. A band, and a face that was pleased with the band. "Two a night. He's one." He looked the hood over. "You're the other. That's the road's luck. Arm."

Jonah gave him the right one.

He pushed the sleeve back himself. Not burned. A white line where a citizen's line had been. Dace looked at it and knew what it was. A struck one. A man who belonged in a Pit, out on the dock road after the bell in a Halyard's coat. "Sir," he said, turning. "This one's a"

Jonah's hand was on his shoulder.

Nobody saw it move. That was the Form. The Sertorian does not draw. No forearm, no wind-up, no tell. The hand goes where the man is going, and the fang is in it when it gets there. Dace had turned to the line to say cut one. His shoulder turned into Jonah's hand. The fang came out of the palm at the seam between the boy's neck and his new grey plate, and did not stop.

Dace sat down on Bram.

The second one was quicker. He saw his friend sit down and did not know why. He did what the garrison taught. High, at the throat seam of a man in a hood with no plate anyone could see.

Jonah let it come.

The plate came up under the hood, on the neck. The seam shut wrong, backward, in a lump. The fang skidded on it. The boy had braced for a throat and hit nothing. His weight went forward.

Jonah's left hand was where the boy was going.

The fang came out of the palm wrong. An arm long, when he had asked for a hand. It went into the seam at the boy's armpit and out the other side. The boy stood for one breath with a Collar's hand on his shoulder and a fang through him.

"What," he said.

"Tom Vale's boy." In the road's tongue, quietly, so only the boy heard.

The boy's plate came off him. He sat down beside the first one.

Two. In the time it took to say a number. In the lamps, with nine auxiliaries and a Thinstripe forty paces off. The bands on their shoulders were a week old. Jonah took the fangs back into his palms and put the plate away. It went badly, the seals last.

The road was silent. Then it was not.

---

"Lock," Corin said.

He said it with his hand up and a stripe's whole voice in it. The word that put every Collar in the province on his knees. Jonah had knelt for it once, on purpose, on a floor with fangs near his father. Corin walked as he said it, nine men behind him, fourteen grades of plate on the raised hand. The hand was shaking.

Jonah stood. Hood up, hands at his sides. He did not go to his knees. Corin walked ten paces with the word in the air and stopped. The nine stopped because he had.

"Lock," Corin said again.

Nothing happened. It had never happened. Tacitus had heard a word that was the law with a hand behind it, and it did with the law what it always did. Nothing. It had done nothing on a floor on Anvil seven years ago while a boy knelt of his own will. It looked past the word at the fourteen grades of cutting on a careful man's arm, and was interested. No, Jonah told it, in the old tongue, without moving his mouth. Not him. Not tonight. Look at his face.

He read the face from ten paces. Five years of drill on a rock, reading a face across a sand. This was the face from the sand. Not the sand where Corin had won. The first one, at fourteen, called up to sharpen, beaten, alive because the Stripe was bored. He had spent every day since making sure he was never at the bottom of a bout again. He was at the bottom of one now, on a road he owned, with a stripe on his shoulder. He knew it.

"Who are you," Corin said.

"A man you signed a letter about once, without asking what was in it," Jonah said. "You didn't need to ask. You needed me to lose a bout I'd already won, and I hadn't, and you signed anyway. That's all I am to you. It's enough."

"I asked you who you are."

Jonah turned his back on him.

He did not hurry. A man who turns his back on a Thinstripe with a fang has counted the Thinstripe and come out at nothing. Corin felt it. He did not come, because nine men were watching him not come, and a Thinstripe cannot be seen deciding. Jonah bent, took Bram under the arms and hauled him up. Bram's cheek was open. His leg would not hold. Jonah got a shoulder under him and walked him up the road, past the two dead boys, toward his stair. Nobody followed.

"I know you," Bram said into the hood. The cut made his voice thick. "I know you. That was. I know what that was."

"You're a liar, Bram. Everybody says."

"Everybody says."

Bram stopped walking.

He looked into the hood, close, with the lamps on the side of it. He had known Jonah since Jonah was six. He knew what he was looking at, and what it was worth in a road with a Stripe behind. Twenty years ago he had shut his mouth on a beautiful ship. He shut it now. He said, loud, so it carried:

"Halyards. Bloody Halyards. Get me to my stair."

Jonah got him to his stair.

---

She was at the top of it.

He felt her before he saw her. He had felt her through a tenement wall for ten years, and through a hull ring on a chain. She stood at the top of the south-yard stair with the door open behind her and the lamp throwing her shadow down the steps. A boy on her hip. Three, dark, Corin's colouring, thumb in his mouth, asleep on her shoulder through all of it. The bell had gone and her husband was on the road. She had heard the road go silent, and then Bram.

Jonah stopped at the bottom. He had counted this at the rail, on the Heron, in a cask, on a rock for five years. It had never once come out. He stood with Bram's arm over his shoulder and looked up at her. His heart did what it had never done on a deck, or under a glass, or with a fang at his throat.

Grey at the temple, where the lamp caught it. More burns on the forearms than before. Hair tied back for the yards. An iron ring on her hand, plain, Corin's. And at her throat, on a chain under her shirt, the hull ring. A band of dull grey plate, cut from the aft seam of the Halcyon by a boy of fifteen who had cried over it.

The ring knew him. Hull plate was not the Vein. It had no want in it. It had lain against her skin for six years and against his hand for one afternoon, and it knew him through the stair. It was warm. She put her hand to her throat without knowing she had.

"Bram," she said.

"Mara. I'm cut. I'm all right. The Halyard got me up the road."

"Your face, Bram."

"It's a face. It'll keep."

She looked at the hood.

She looked longer than a number took. She had looked at him that way at the dock gate seven years ago, when he stepped back and she went careful behind her smile. She was careful now, through and through. A boy on her hip, a husband on the road, and a hooded man at the foot of her stair who stood, Jonah realised, the way he stood. Varro's feet. Somebody's going to notice, she had told him.

She noticed. He watched her not know what she had noticed. He watched her decide, with her hand at her throat, not to come down.

"Thank you," she said to the hood. In the voice she kept for what she meant. "Whoever you are. Thank you for Bram."

He could not answer. His voice was the one part of him she would know in the dark. A hood, a Belt turn, a hidden face. None of it would hold against one word. So he did what the old man had taught for a room where he could not speak. He inclined his head, once, the Halyard way. He took Bram's arm off his shoulder and set it on the rail. He stepped back.

One step. The step he had taken at the dock gate. His body did it before he could stop it.

Mara's face changed.

Not the careful look. Something under it, for one breath. Then she had it back. She turned with the boy and went in. She left the door open.

Jonah went down the dock road in the dark.

---

Tacitus asked him at the rail.

Not in the old tongue and not in the road's. In the one word it had, in its own voice, very old, used once before at the bottom of a well for a guard.

Name.

Jonah stood with his hands on the rail and Anvil's hull ring above him. Forty paces up the road, nine auxiliaries and a Thinstripe would be standing over two boys with a lamp by now.

"It's not hers you're asking for," he said, aloud, in the old tongue. "You've got hers. I've said it every night for seven years. You heard my heart on that stair, and now you've got something in you that you can't put down. Same as the guard."

It waited.

"I don't know. I've never had a word for that one either. I had her. I had the ring. I had a step I took back at a gate, with something hungry in me, and I never told her why. Then I was in a hole. She said my father's name at a fire where you don't. She married him. There's a boy. She wears it under her shirt where he can see the chain."

He put his hand flat on the rail.

"You want a name. I'll give you the kind I gave you for Decimus. It's the only kind I've got."

It waited.

"The Halcyon. The first ship I ever broke. I was fifteen. I cut the plate for the aft seam and I cried after, because it was beautiful. Bram's a liar, and it was true. That's the name. What happened on the stair is called that. It's a piece of a ship I cut up because I was told to, that somebody kept."

Tacitus took it. He felt it turn the name over and put it somewhere, with the whole of its attention. It had a man in it. Now it had a ship. It would learn the rest from the names. That was what it did. Then the small touch. The hand with no hand.

"I know," Jonah said. "Two days. Then Morrow. Then a Flamen who sells readings. Then I'll know what I am, and I'll go. I'll not come up that stair."

"Corin saw the Form. He said Lock and it didn't take. He saw the hand go where the boy was going. He'll not know what he saw. Nobody's seen it in a hundred years. He'll lie awake. That's on the slate too. Let him."

Up the dock road, a lamp came round the corner of the sheds. It stopped over two boys in the rock, and stayed.
