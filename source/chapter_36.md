# Chapter 36: Mara

The man in the road was Bram.

Jonah knew him from the rail before he could see him, the way he knew the bell. A heart he had heard through a bunk wall for ten years, going fast now. A voice that had always been too loud for a tenement stair, saying, up the dock road, in the dark, "I'm going, I'm going, I've a bad leg, give a man a minute."

"You've had your minute, friend."

"I've had a minute and a half. I've a bad leg and it's forty paces to my stair, Corin. You know it is, you've climbed it."

Jonah stood at the rail with his hood up and did not move.

He could see them now. The dock road ran up from the rail to the tenement stairs between the cutting sheds. The sun-lamps were at their night setting. The auxiliaries were coming down it in a line with their fangs out, twelve, grey with the black band, the plate on their forearms catching the lamps. Ahead of them the road was emptying the way a road empties, doors shutting. In the middle of it, alone, going slow, was a man of fifty with a torch-burned face and a leg that had been under a hull plate on the day Jonah was fifteen. Jonah had held the other end of the plate.

Corin was at the head of the line.

He had a stripe. Jonah saw it on his shoulder where the band had been, thin, purple, a real one, sewn on grey. The plate on him was not thin the way the boys' was. It was fourteen grades of it, six years of a cutting that had been fed on a rising. He walked at the head of twelve men the way a Thinstripe walked, which was the way the cadet had walked into the sand seven years ago. His voice was careful. It said sir to men who did not deserve it and friend to men who were not. It said friend now.

"Forty paces is a long way after the bell, Bram. You know the order. Any Collar on the dock road after curfew is out for the war, and out for the war's a sharpening." Corin stopped. The line stopped. "I'll not do it. You carried me home once. But there's eleven behind me who didn't know you, and they've a quota."

"A quota."

"Two a night. It's the legate's. Keeps the docks quiet." Corin's voice did not change. "Go on. I'll count slow."

Bram went on.

He went slow, because he could not go fast. Jonah watched him go and felt the eleven behind Corin and counted them. Two of them were not slow. Two of them had hearts going the way the boys' had at the road's end, quick, plated, with a fang and a band and a quota. They came out of the line past Corin at the pace men walk when they have decided. Corin said, "Dace," without turning. The one called Dace said, "Two a night, sir," and kept walking.

Bram heard the boots.

He turned round. He turned round on his bad leg in the middle of the dock road with the lamps on him, forty paces from his stair. He saw two auxiliaries coming with the fangs out. His face did what a Collar's face does on Anvil when it understands that the thing everyone says will happen is happening to it. He said, loud, the way he said everything, so it carried down the road to the rail:

"Tom Vale's boy would've had you both."

The first fang went across his face.

Not into. Across. The flat of it, the way the garrison taught for a Collar you were going to take your time with. Bram went down on the rock with his hands up. The second auxiliary put a boot on his bad leg. The two of them stood over him. Corin stood at the head of the line with the other nine and looked at the sheds.

Jonah left the rail.

---

He would tell the whole of it later to the one person. She would say, you'd counted it. He would say, yes. I'd counted it at the rail with the bell going. Twelve auxiliaries. One with a stripe and Lock in his hand. Two with a quota. A road with lamps on it and a hood on me. A captain three days from lying to a College dock who'd said, don't put your plate up on Anvil. And Bram on the rock with a fang across his face and a boot on his leg, saying my father's name. I'd counted it. It came out to a thing I do, and I did it.

He came up the road at a walk.

That was the first thing. He did not run. A man running at twelve auxiliaries was a man with a fang out. A man walking was a Halyard crewman going home after the bell. The nine in the line looked at a hood coming up the road and did not do anything, because the road was theirs and a hood was nothing. Corin looked, and did not do anything either. The hood was between him and the two with the quota, and he was watching the sheds.

The one called Dace looked up from Bram.

"Curfew, friend."

"I heard the bell."

"Then you'll know what you're out for." Dace straightened. He was twenty-five. He had a fang with blood on the flat of it, and a band. He had a face that had done the sum and come out at the band and was pleased with it. "Two a night. He's one." He looked at the hood. "You're the other. That's the road's luck. Arm."

Jonah gave him the right one.

He pushed the sleeve back himself. It was not burned. It had a white line on it where a citizen's line had been. Dace looked at the line and understood what it was, a cut one, a struck one, a thing that should have been in a Pit and was on the dock road after the bell in a Halyard's coat. He opened his mouth to say so to the line. The hand at the end of the arm he was looking at was on his shoulder.

He had not seen it move. Nobody had. That was the Form. The old man had said it on the rock with a stick: the Sertorian does not draw. There is no forearm, no wind-up, no tell, no place the fang comes from that a man can watch. The hand goes where the man is going, and the fang is in it when it gets there. Dace's shoulder had gone into Jonah's hand because Dace had been turning to the line to say cut one. The fang came out of the palm at the seam where the boy's neck met his new grey plate, and did not stop.

Dace sat down on Bram.

The second one was quicker. He had seen his friend sit down and he did not know why. He did the thing the garrison taught, which was the only thing, high, at the throat seam of a man in a hood who had no plate on him that anybody could see. Jonah did not step and did not go under it. He let it come. The plate came up under the hood, on the neck, the way it did now. The seam shut, wrong, backward, in a lump. The boy's fang skidded on a thing that was not there. The boy's face did what the first boy's had done at the road's end. His weight went the way a man's does who has hit nothing where he expected a throat, forward. Jonah's left hand was there.

He had not put it there. He had put it where the boy was going.

The fang came out of the left palm. It was the wrong length, an arm when he had asked for a hand. It went into the seam at the boy's armpit and through, and out the other side. The boy stood on the rock of the dock road for the length of a breath with a Collar's hand on his shoulder and a fang through him. He looked at the hood and said, "What," the way they all said it. Jonah said, in the road's tongue, quietly, so only the boy would hear:

"Tom Vale's boy."

The boy's plate came off him. He sat down beside the first one.

Two. In the time it took to say a number. In the light, with the lamps on him, with nine auxiliaries and a Thinstripe forty paces off. Jonah stood over two dead boys with the bands on their shoulders a week old, the fangs gone back into the palms, his hood up. He put the plate away. It went, badly, the seals last. The road was silent.

Then it was not.

---

"Lock," Corin said.

He said it the way the cadet's instructor had said it on the dais seven years ago, with the hand up and the whole of a stripe's voice in it. The word that put every Collar in the province on his knees. The word Jonah had knelt for once, on purpose, on a floor with fangs near his father. Corin said it now, walking, at the head of nine men, with his hand up and fourteen grades of plate on it. The hand was shaking.

Jonah stood.

He stood in the dock road with the hood up and his hands at his sides. He did not go to his knees. He did not do anything. Corin walked ten paces with his hand up and the word in the air between them, and stopped. A Thinstripe who has said Lock to a Collar and watched the Collar stand is a man who has just found out what is on the road with him, and does not have a word for it. The nine behind him stopped because he had.

"Lock," Corin said again.

Nothing happened. It had never happened. Tacitus, in the doorway of him, had heard a word that was the law with a hand behind it. It had done what it did with the law, which was nothing, the way it had done nothing on a floor on Anvil seven years ago while a boy knelt of his own will to keep a fang from his father. It did nothing now. It looked, past the word, at the fourteen grades of cutting on a careful man's arm, and was interested. Jonah said, in the old tongue, without moving his mouth: no. Not him. Not tonight. Look at his face.

Corin's face.

Jonah had drilled it, on a rock, with a stick, for five years, the reading of a face across a sand. He read this one from ten paces in the lamps. It was the face from the sand. Not the sand where Corin had won. The other one, the first one, at fourteen, when Corin had been called to sharpen and had lost and had lived because the Stripe was bored. He had spent every day since making sure he was never at the bottom of a bout again. He was at the bottom of one now, on a road he owned, with a stripe on his shoulder, and knew it.

"Who are you," Corin said.

Jonah did not answer.

He turned his back on him. He did it the way Sabinus had walked through men with chisels, without hurrying. A man who turns his back on a Thinstripe with a fang is a man who has counted the Thinstripe and come out at nothing. Corin felt that. Jonah knew he felt it. He did not come, because nine men were watching him not come and he could not afford to be seen deciding. Jonah bent and took Bram under the arms, the way Ulli took the sick, and hauled him up off the rock. Bram's face was open across the cheek. His leg would not hold him. Jonah put a shoulder under him and walked him up the road toward his stair, forty paces, past the two dead boys. Nobody followed.

"I know you," Bram said, into the hood. His voice was thick with the cut. "I know you. I know that. That was."

"You're a liar, Bram. Everybody says."

Bram stopped walking.

He stopped in the middle of the dock road with his arm over a hooded man's shoulder and a line of auxiliaries at his back. He looked into the hood, close, with the lamps on the side of it. His face did the thing a Collar's face does on Anvil when a thing everybody says cannot happen has happened. He did not say anything. He had known Jonah since Jonah was six. He knew what he was looking at and he knew what it was worth, in a road, with a Stripe behind. He was a liar, everybody said. He shut his mouth on the truth the way he had shut it on a beautiful ship twenty years ago. He said, loud, so it carried:

"Halyards. Bloody Halyards. Get me to my stair."

Jonah got him to his stair.

---

She was at the top of it.

He felt her before the stair. He had felt her through a tenement wall for ten years. Through a hull ring on a chain. Through a floor with fangs near it. She was at the top of the south-yard stair with the door open behind her and the lamp in the room throwing her down the steps. She had a boy on her hip. Three. Dark, the way Corin was dark, with his thumb in his mouth, asleep against her shoulder with the ease of a child who has slept through worse. She had come to the door because the bell had gone and her husband was on the road with it. She had heard the road go silent, and then Bram. She stood on the top step and looked down.

Jonah stopped at the bottom.

He had counted this. He had counted it at the rail and on the Heron. In a cask. On a rock for five years. It had never once come out. He stood at the foot of the stair with Bram's arm over his shoulder and the hood up, and looked at her. The thing in the doorway of him felt his heart do a thing it had never done, not on a deck, not under a glass, not on a road with a fang in his throat.

She had grey in her hair. Not much. At the temple, where the torch light caught it. She had the burn marks on both forearms, more than there had been, and her hair tied back for the yards. A ring on her hand that was Corin's, iron, plain. At her throat, on a chain that went under her shirt, a thing that was not visible. He could feel it, through the plate, through the stair, through the rock of a world he had flown out of. A band of dull grey hull plate cut from the aft seam of a ship called the Halcyon by a boy of fifteen who had cried, Bram said, because it was beautiful.

The ring knew him.

That was the thing he would keep. Not her face. The ring. Hull plate was not the Vein. It was not a lorica. It had no want in it. It had been on a chain against her skin for six years and against his hand for one afternoon. It knew him, the way the door in the Deep had known his hand. It was warm. She put her hand to her throat without knowing she had done it.

"Bram," she said.

"Mara." Bram's voice was thick. "I'm cut. I'm all right. The Halyard got me up the road."

She looked at the hood.

She looked at it for longer than a number took. She looked the way she had looked at him at the dock gate seven years ago when he stepped back and she went careful behind her smile. She was careful now, all the way through, with a boy on her hip and a husband on the road. And a hooded man at the foot of her stair who had brought Bram home cut and who was standing, Jonah realised, the way he stood. The way Varro's feet had put him. The way she had told him not to. Somebody's going to notice.

She noticed.

He watched her notice. He watched her not know what she had noticed. He watched her decide, on the top step, with her hand at her throat on the thing under her shirt, not to come down.

"Thank you," she said. To the hood. In the voice she used for things she meant. "Whoever you are. Thank you for Bram."

He did not answer. He could not. His voice was the one thing on him she would know in the dark. He had a hood and a Belt turn and a face she could not see. None of it would hold against a word. So he did the thing the old man had taught him for a room where he could not speak. He inclined his head, once, the way a Halyard does. He took Bram's arm off his shoulder and put it on the rail. He stepped back.

One step. The way he had stepped back at the dock gate. His body did it.

Mara's face did something.

He would not know what, later. He would try, and would not get it. It was not the careful thing. It was under the careful thing, the way the fear had been under Sabinus's bored face. It was there for the length of one breath. Then she had it back. She turned with the boy on her hip and went in. She left the door open, the way it had been left open once for a man in a chair.

Jonah went down the dock road in the dark.

---

Tacitus asked him at the rail.

Not in the old tongue and not in the road's. In the one word it had, in the middle of him, in its own voice that was very old and had been used once, at the bottom of a well, for a guard: Name.

He stood at the rail with his hands on it and the hull ring of Anvil above him. Two dead boys were forty paces up a road that nine auxiliaries and a Thinstripe would be standing over with a lamp by now. He thought, it's not asking hers. It has hers. I've said it every night for seven years and it's heard every one. It heard my heart on that stair. It's got a thing in it now that it can't put down, the way it had the guard. It's asked the only way it can.

"I don't know," he said, in the old tongue, aloud, to the dark.

It waited.

"I don't. I've never had a word for that one either. I had her. I had the ring. I had a step I took back at a gate with a thing in me that was hungry. I never once told her why, and then I was in a hole. And she said my father's name at a fire where you don't. She married him. There's a boy. She wears it under her shirt where he can see the chain." He put his hand flat on the rail. "You want a name. I'll give you what I gave you for Decimus. It's the only kind I've got."

He gave it.

"The Halcyon," he said. "The first ship I ever broke. I was fifteen. I cut the plate for the aft seam and I cried after, because it was beautiful. Bram's a liar, and it was true. That's the name. The thing on the stair's called that. It's a piece of a ship I cut up because I was told to, that somebody kept."

The thing in the doorway took it.

He felt it take it the way it had taken the guard's, with the whole of its attention. He felt it turn it over and put it somewhere. It was the second thing it had ever put anywhere that was not matter. It had a man in it and now it had a ship. It would learn the rest, he thought, from those. It would learn everything from the names. That was what it did.

Then it did the small thing. The hand with no hand.

"I know," Jonah said. "Two days. Then Morrow. Then a Flamen who sells readings. Then I'll know what I am, and I'll go. I'll not come up that stair." He took his hands off the rail. "He saw the Form. Corin. He said Lock and it didn't take. He saw the hand go where the boy was going. He'll not know what he saw, because nobody's seen it in a hundred years. He'll lie awake. That's on the slate too. Let him."

Up the dock road, a long way off, a lamp came round the corner of the sheds. It stopped over two boys in the rock, and stayed.
