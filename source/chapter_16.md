# Chapter 16: What the Vein Wants

"You have asked me twice what it wants," the Censor said, "and both times I have told you that I do not know, and both times that was true and it was also the answer of a coward. Tonight I am going to tell you what I think. Put your hand on the wall."

It was the second year. Jonah knew that the way he knew the road, by the feel of it under his feet. The war had come down the Throat as twelve and stayed as twelve, by weight, and Rennick carried fifteen up every dusk and Tullus read it and said nothing, and the Deep gave slabs and the Shallows gave what it gave, and decury six had a foreman who chose a man when the count was short and did not choose soft. Corr had stopped talking to him. Not unkindly. Corr sat at his place beside Jonah's at the wall and cut his line and said nothing, and Jonah understood that this was what a foreman was, and that it was correct.

He put his hand on the wall.

They were in the Cradle. The old man had brought him to the back of it, where the rock of the tunnel met the dark of the Vein, and the Vein there was thick, the thickest in the Pit that a man could stand in front of, and it breathed. It had always breathed. He'd stood in front of it every night for a year and a half with a stick coming at him from the dark, and it had breathed at his back the whole time, kind, warm, wanting him, and he had stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heart.

"Not the palm," the Censor said. "You have used the palm for locks. The whole hand. Flat. And hold it."

"Hold what?"

"The thing in you. You have held it from the wall for eighteen months, every night, through a seam, the way you told the rock. Tonight I want you to hold it at the wall. Let it go to the door and stop it in the door. Do not let it out. Do not let it come back. Stand it in the doorway and make it stay there, and listen through it."

Jonah looked at him.

"It nearly took me," he said. "Through a chisel. You know that. You were on the other side of the rock."

"Yes. And you were nineteen days into a thing you did not understand with a genius that had never been asked to stay. It is eighteen months. You have said the names five hundred times. It expects them. It expects you." The pale eyes did not move. "If I am wrong you will go into the wall and I will tap to you until you do, and I will have been wrong about one more thing, which at my age is not a great burden. Put your hand flat."

He put his hand flat.

---

The thing under his ribs had been leaning toward the wall since he stepped up to it. It always did. And now, with his palm on the warm dark surface and the old man's voice saying let it go, he stopped holding it, and it went.

It went the way it had on the nineteenth day. Up through him, through the arm, reading as it went, past the ring in his forearm and the old cut, out toward the hand, toward the warm, toward the door. He felt it reach the palm. He felt it reach the place where his skin met the Vein and start to go through, the way it had gone through the chisel, and he thought, in the old tongue, because that was the tongue for slow things: stop.

Not no. Not the thing he'd said in the Kestrel's hold. Stop. The way you'd say it to a man walking through a door you wanted him to hold.

It stopped.

It stood in the doorway. He felt it stand there, half in his hand and half in the wall, with the whole of the Vein on the other side of it saying, the way it had said on the nineteenth day, come, and it did not go, because he had asked it to stand, and it had learned in five hundred nights of names that when he asked, there was a reason, and it was interested in reasons.

And he listened through it.

He had thought he knew what the wall was. He'd stood at it with a chisel for eighteen months and felt it open for him and felt something on the other side turn to look, and he'd lain against it in the Cradle with the cold in him and heard, or nearly heard, what Ulli said it sang. He had thought that was the wall.

It was the door of the wall. This was the wall.

It was the biggest thing he had ever felt and he had felt it once before, from the wrong side, going, and he had not been able to keep it then, because he'd been the meat between and it had been going through him. Now he stood in his own hand with the thing under his ribs standing in the doorway holding it open and he looked through, and the Vein was there, all of it, the way the legion transport had been there in the rings, all at once, every gallery and every level, the Shallows and the Deep and the Mouth and the ten thousand cuts that ran from all of them down into the rock further than the Pit went, further than the garrison knew, one thing, one mind, the size of a planetoid, awake.

And it was singing.

Ulli had been right. He'd known Ulli was right; the old man had said so. But knowing was not this. It went through him from the palm to the heart like the fever, warm, and it was not a song and it was not words and it was names. Every one it had ever taken. It went through them the way the Censor went through a slab, one after another, in order, in the dark, and it did not stop, and it had not stopped in four hundred years, and it was not grief. That was the thing he understood, standing in the doorway with the thing under his ribs holding it open. It was not grieving them. It was keeping them. The way the old man kept them on the slabs. The way Jonah kept them, all of them, in the place where he kept things, out of order. It had been cut for four hundred years and everything that had ever gone into the cut had been kept, every sick man laid in the Cradle, every deep-digger it had taken at the slab face, and it went through them because that was what it did with a thing it had, it counted.

He heard Vetch.

Not the name. It had no names. He heard the shape of a young guard in a grey coat who had never been told no, going into the wall from the arm in, and the shape was in the count now, between an old Marl deep-digger and a Blank who had touched what he should not have, and the wall did not know it was a guard. The old man had said that. The wall does not know what a coat is. It only knew that this one had come in, and it kept him, and it went on.

And under the count, the other thing. The thing it had said on the nineteenth day, and had never stopped saying, because it was one thing and could not think two things at once, and this was the thing it thought.

Come. You are mine. You were taken. Come.

Not to the thing in his hand. To all of it. To every piece. He felt it, and understood, and the understanding was cold in a way the Vein had never been. It was saying it to the Shallows and the Deep and the casks going up the Throat. It was saying it to every cutting in every Blank's arm on every moon of the Limes and every Thinstripe's plate and every ancestral genius in every Broadstripe house with a name and a shrine, four hundred years of pieces cut off it and made small and carried away, and it had been saying come to all of them the whole time, every hour, and none of them could hear it, because the thing that could hear had been cut out of them before they were given to a man.

His was the first. The thing in his hand, standing in the doorway. It was the first piece in four hundred years that could hear its mother say come, and it had heard, and it had gone, and it had turned around at the door because a man on the floor had said Tom Vale.

He took his hand off the wall.

---

He sat on the rock with his back to the Vein and shook, and it was not the fever, and the old man sat across from him with the lamp and did not say anything for a long time.

"It wants them back," Jonah said at last.

"Yes."

"All of them. Every piece. Every cutting in the Empire. It's calling them. It's been calling them for four hundred years and none of them can hear it."

"Yes."

"And the sickness. The thing that takes the deep ones, the thing that had me on the nineteenth day. That's not it killing them."

"No." The Censor's voice was very quiet. "That is it trying to make one of them into a piece. From the wrong side. It cannot cut. It does not know how to cut, or how to make small, or how to do anything the College does; it is one thing and it has never been anything else. But it can reach. It reaches for a man at the wall and it goes into him the way your thing went into you, reading, looking for a place to be, and there is no place, because a man is not a piece, and it does not understand that, and it keeps reaching, and the man dies of being reached for. That is the sickness. It is the wall trying to hold something the way you held your thing in the doorway, and having no doorway." He was quiet a moment. "Every man in the Cradle dies of being loved by something that does not know what a man is."

Jonah sat with that.

"And the cut ones," he said. "Aulus of Marl on the slab. Corr says cut ones go faster. It knows there's a hole in you, he said."

"Corr is a fool and is right, as usual. A man who has had a cutting taken out has a place in him where a piece of the wall was. The wall can feel that place from across the Pit the way you can feel a draught. It goes to the place. It tries to fill it. Bricks has lived eleven years because Bricks has the sense of a stone and the wall cannot find purchase on him. Most do not." The old man touched the white line across his own forearm, once, without looking at it. "I had a place. A large one. Seventy-one on the glass, and the College cut it out of me in the Forum with the rod, all of it, and I woke up on a slab with a hole in me the size of a house, and they put me in a transport, and they put me in this rock, and in the first month the wall found the hole."

Jonah looked at him.

"You never told me that."

"I have told you nothing that you did not need, and you did not need it until tonight." The Censor looked at the lamp. "It reached for me in the Chamber. Through the rock. The way it reached for you at the chisel, except it had a hole to go to, and it went, and I lay on that shelf for eleven days with the thing you felt on the nineteenth day going through me, come, come, you are mine, and I understood what it was. I was Master of the College. I had read every page. I understood what was happening to me better than any man who has ever had it happen, and I understood, on the sixth day, what I could do about it."

"What?"

"Let it."

The old man said it flatly, to the lamp.

"Let it in. Not fight it. Not hold it off. Open the hole and let a piece of the wall come into it and stay, and be what you are. A man with a whole genius in him. I understood that on the sixth day, lying on the shelf, and I understood that it would kill me, probably, the way it killed the nine, and I understood that if it did not kill me I would be the only man in four hundred years who could hear the wall, and I lay there for five more days and I did not do it." He looked up. "I held it off. I tapped on the rock to keep my hands busy, that was the first tapping, and I said the College's words for holding a genius still until my voice went, and I held the hole shut with the whole of what I was, and on the eleventh day it stopped reaching, because I had made myself a stone, like Bricks, and it could find no purchase. And I have been a stone for twenty years, and I built a tunnel with a chisel, and I wrote names on slabs, and I have never once let it in, and that is the thing I am."

"Why?"

"Because I was afraid." The old man's voice did not change. "I have told you that I hid the box because I was afraid of what a whole genius would do in a man. That is true. I was more afraid of what it would do in me. I had been a senator, and a Master, and the best fang on the floor, and I had done the arithmetic on the wall and stood up in the Senate with it, and I was seventy-one on the glass and I had never in my life been afraid of anything I could name, and I lay on that shelf and I was afraid of becoming a thing that could hear its mother, and I did not want to know what that was." He stopped. "You did not have the choice. You were dying on a deck with a Stripe over you. That is the whole of the difference between us, boy, and I want you to understand it exactly, because you are going to spend the rest of your life being told that you are brave, and you are not. You were cornered. I was not. And I chose the stone."

Jonah looked at the old man for a long time in the lamp.

"That's why you tapped to the dying," he said. "For twenty years. Not to count them."

"To find one who would do what I did not." The Censor's face was still. "Yes. I said that on the twenty-ninth night. You turned around. I have never once said I was proud of it. I have said it is correct that I die here."

---

"So what does it want," Jonah said. "From me. Not from the pieces. From me. It let go on the nineteenth day. It let go tonight. It's had two chances at me and it's let me walk off both times with a mouthful of it in my hands, and you say it can't stop a piece of itself, but it's one thing, it's the size of the Pit, it could have kept the door shut. It didn't. Why?"

The Censor did not answer at once.

"I have thought about that," he said. "Every night since you tapped four four seven one on my wall. I will tell you what I think and you will remember that I have been wrong about the wall before." He folded his hands. "I think it does not know what you are either. I think it reached for you on the nineteenth day and found a piece of itself already there, whole, that could hear it, the first in four hundred years, and it said come, because that is the only thing it knows how to say, and the piece went, and turned around, and came back with a mouthful. And I think the wall felt that. I think it felt a piece of itself walk out of the door with bread and go back into a man and stay, on purpose, for forty names, and I think that it has never in four hundred years had anything to think about except come, and now it has this."

"This."

"You. A thing that can hear it and does not obey it. A piece that says no. It has never had one. It does not know what to do with one. And I think, boy, though I would not put it on a slab, that it is doing with you what you did with the wrong-arm fang for a hundred and thirty nights."

"What's that?"

"Finding out what shape you are."

Jonah sat with his back against the thing that was finding out what shape he was and felt it breathe.

"And when it knows?"

"Then it will want something," the old man said, "and I will be dead, and you will have to work out what, and whether to give it. That is the far end of this. I cannot see it. I can see the near end, which is that you have stood in your own hand tonight and held the door of the wall open and looked through it and let go, which no man has done, and that something in you has just learned a thing from it, and that you are about to have a fever." He picked up the lamp. "Sit. Count. And then come with me, because there is a thing in the Chamber you should see, and I would rather you saw it tonight than found it after."

---

The fever came and he counted it down and it was longer than the others, and when it was done and he could stand, something had gone over inside him that was not small.

He'd felt the lock before. In the sand, in the hold, on the road, in the seventh chamber with a chisel in a boy's neck. This was not that. This was a whole door. He stood up on the rock of the Cradle with the thing under his ribs settling into a coat that was a size larger than it had been an hour ago, by a size he could feel the edges of, and the old man looked at his face in the lamp and said, in the old tongue, "Yes. That is what strain is, when it is not a fight. You were nearer to going into the wall tonight than you were on the nineteenth day, and you did not, and it has learned from that, and I would say, if I were a Flamen with a glass, that you have just walked out of the Tiro band and into the next, and that the ceiling you would have been fused at nine is a very long way below you." He turned toward the tunnel. "It means nothing. It is a number. Come."

They went through the tunnel.

It was the first time. He'd opened the Chamber's door with his hand and looked in and locked it again, but he had not been through the rock, and the tunnel was the width of a stooping man and the length of a hundred paces and every hand's breadth of it had been cut with a chisel worn to half its length, and he crawled through it behind the old man's lamp and did not think about that, because thinking about it was a debt, and the old man had said not to carry one.

The Chamber was as it had been. The shelf. The bowl. The sixteen slabs leaning on the rock.

"Sit," the Censor said. "The first one. Read it."

Jonah sat on the floor in front of the first slab and read it.

It was in the old tongue and it was in the small even hand, and it went in columns, and the columns were numbers, and beside some of the numbers were names, and beside some of the names were words. Marl. A field. Anvil, the yards. Halyard, a ship. He read down the first column and it was the year 366, and the first number in it was one, and beside the number one there was a name, and the name was three words long, and he had heard it once, tapped, badly, on the twenty-ninth night of the haul.

Aulus Terentius Pharus. Latium. The College. Senator.

"I wrote it the first night," the old man said behind him. "Before I had a chisel. With a piece of the shelf. It is the worst hand on any of the slabs and I have never corrected it." A pause. "You will want to know why I am first. It is not vanity. It is that a man who counts must be counted, or the count is a lie. I put myself at the top so that the rest would know I had not exempted myself. Nobody has ever read it but you."

Jonah read down.

He read the second year and the third. He read numbers with nothing beside them, hundreds of them, men who had come down the Throat and gone into the wall and had never once, in the Cradle, said who they were. He read names. He read fathers. He read, in the ninth year, a number with Marl beside it and a field, and then a second word, in the same hand, that was a name, and the name was Pell.

He stopped.

"Fourth slab," the Censor said. "Ninth year. He said his name in the Cradle. He did not say his brother's. He said, my brother's on Marl, tell him it was a field. I have had that written for eleven years with nobody to tell it to." The old man's voice was even. "You will tell him."

"Yes."

"Tonight. Before the haul."

"Yes."

Jonah read on. He read to the sixteenth slab and the twentieth year and the fourth column, and there was four four seven one, and beside it his name and his moon and his yard, and beside those, in the same hand, smaller, his father's, all of it, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder, and he sat on the floor of a stone room with his back to a wall that was finding out what shape he was and looked at his father's name in a dead man's language cut into a slab of the thing that had made the Empire, and did not say anything for a long time.

"Everyone is on these," the old man said. "Everyone who came down. I did not choose. That is the whole of what a count is. You do not choose." He came and stood beside Jonah and looked at the slab. "When I die, they are yours. All sixteen. You will not be able to carry them; they weigh what the wall weighs. You will have to carry them the way you carry the rest. In the place where you keep things."

"There's a great deal in there already."

"I know. I have heard most of it through a wall." The Censor put his hand, briefly, on the top of the sixteenth slab, the way Morrow had put his on a blanket. "There is room. I have been keeping this count for twenty years in a room the size of your father's, and I assure you, boy, there is always room. That is the only thing about a count that is kind."

He took his hand away.

"Go and tell Pell about his brother," he said. "Say it slowly. Say the field. He will want the field. And then carry your buckets, and say your names, and sleep, because tomorrow the wall gives ten and you will choose a man, and you will not choose soft."

---

Pell was on the shelf above his, in the sleeping gallery, with the lamp at the end that never went out, and Jonah stood beside the shelf in the dark and said his name, and the old man's farmer's eyes opened.

"Your brother," Jonah said.

Pell did not move.

"He came down in the ninth year. Three fifty-seven. He said his name in the Cradle. He said, my brother's on Marl, tell him it was a field." Jonah said it slowly. "It was a field. The north forty, he said. It didn't make quota. That's the whole of it. He went into the wall in the ninth year and somebody wrote it down and I've read it, and now it's said."

Pell lay on the shelf and looked at the roof of the gallery.

"Who wrote it," he said, after a long time.

"Someone who counts."

"The rock."

"Yes."

"You've seen it."

"I've read it. It's in the old tongue, in a hand. Your brother's name and the field. It's kept, Pell. It's been kept eleven years. It was waiting for somebody to carry it up the road."

The old man from Marl lay very still, and then he turned his face to the rock wall of the gallery, away from the lamp, and Jonah stood beside the shelf and heard him breathe, and after a while Pell said, to the wall, in the voice he used for things he had seen before:

"The north forty. That's right. That was the one. Bad drainage." A breath. "He always said it was the drainage."

And then, very quietly, to the wall, he began to sing, and it was not a field song, or not one Jonah had heard, and Jonah stood in the dark of the gallery and let him, and did not go to the haul until it was done.
