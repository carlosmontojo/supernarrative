# Chapter 16: What the Vein Wants

"You have asked me twice what it wants," the Censor said. "Both times I told you I did not know. Both times that was true, and it was also the answer of a coward. Tonight I am going to tell you what I think. Put your hand on the wall."

It was the second year. The count had come down the Throat as twelve and stayed at twelve, by weight. Rennick carried fifteen up every dusk and Tullus read it and said nothing. Decury six had a foreman who chose a man when the count was short and did not choose soft. He'd told Decimus why, once, at the dawn count. "Corr's had four hard weeks and one easy one. I gave him the easy one back today. That's the count, not kindness," Jonah said. Decimus had written it down anyway, which was its own kind of answer. Corr had stopped talking to him after that. He cut his line beside Jonah's and said nothing. That was what a foreman was. It was correct.

They were at the back of the Cradle, where the tunnel rock met the dark of the Vein. It was the thickest Vein a man could stand in front of, and it breathed. It had breathed at his back every night for eighteen months while a stick came at him out of the dark. He had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own heart.

He put his hand on the wall.

"Not the palm," the Censor said. "You use the palm for locks. The whole hand. Flat. And hold it."

"Hold what?"

"The genius. You have held it back from the wall for eighteen months, every night, through a seam. Tonight, hold it at the wall. Let it go to the door and stop it in the door. Not out. Not back. Make it stand there. Then listen through it."

Jonah looked at him.

"It nearly took me. Through a chisel. You were on the other side of the rock," Jonah said.

"You were nineteen days into something you did not understand, with a genius that had never once been asked to stay. It is eighteen months now. You have said the names five hundred times. It expects them. It expects you." The pale eyes did not move. "If I am wrong you go into the wall. I will tap to you until you do. I will have been wrong about one more thing, and at my age that is no great burden. Flat."

He put his hand flat.

---

The genius had been leaning at the wall since he stepped up to it. It always did. Now his palm was on the warm dark and the old man was saying let it go, so he stopped holding it, and it went.

Up through him, through the arm, reading as it went, past the ring in his forearm and the old cut, out toward the hand and the warmth and the door. He felt it reach his palm. He felt it reach the place where skin met Vein and start through, the way it had gone through the chisel. He thought, in the old tongue, because that was the tongue for slow things: stop.

Not no. Not what he had said in the Kestrel's hold. Stop. The word you use to a man in a door when you want him to hold it.

It stopped.

It stood in the doorway, half in his hand and half in the wall. The whole Vein was on the far side of it saying come. It did not go. He had asked it to stand, and five hundred nights of names had taught it that when he asked there was a reason, and it liked reasons.

He listened through it.

He had thought he knew the wall. He had cut it for eighteen months and felt it open for him. He had lain against it in the Cradle with the cold in him and heard, or nearly heard, what Ulli said it sang. That had been the door. This was the wall.

It was the biggest thing he had ever felt. All of it at once, every gallery and every level, the Shallows and the Deep and the Mouth. Ten thousand cuts running down into the rock, deeper than the Pit went, deeper than the garrison knew. One mind, the size of the planetoid, awake.

And it was singing.

Ulli had been right. It was not a song and it was not words. It was names. Every name it had ever taken, one after another, in order, in the dark. It had not stopped in four hundred years. It was not grieving them. It was keeping them. Every sick man laid in the Cradle. Every deep-digger it had taken at the slab face. It counted, because that was what it did with what it had.

He heard Vetch.

Not the name. It had no names. He heard the shape of a young guard in a grey coat going into the wall from the arm in. The shape sat in the count between an old Marl deep-digger and a Blank who had touched what he should not have. The wall did not know it was a guard. It did not know what a coat was. It only knew this one had come in. It kept him and went on.

Under the count was the rest. What it had said on the nineteenth day and had never stopped saying, because it was one mind and could only think one thought.

Come. You are mine. You were taken. Come.

Not to the piece in his hand. To every piece. It was saying it to the Shallows and the Deep and the casks going up the Throat. To every cutting in every Blank's arm on every moon of the Limes. To every Thinstripe's plate. To every ancestral genius in every Broadstripe house with a name and a shrine. Four hundred years of pieces cut off it and carried away. It had been calling all of them, every hour, and none of them could hear. The part that could hear had been cut out of them before they were given to a man.

His was the first. The genius in his hand was the first piece in four hundred years that could hear its mother say come. It had heard. It had gone. It had turned around at the door because a man on the floor said Tom Vale.

He went to take his hand off the wall.

It did not come.

The Vein had his palm. Not the genius; the wall itself, warm, holding, the pull of the whole planetoid on one hand. His wrist bent. His arm went in to the elbow before he knew it was moving. The genius stood in the door and did not go, and the wall pulled at it through him, and his knees started to fold toward the rock.

He set his feet and tore the hand free.

The palm burned. He went down on one knee with the hand against his chest and the Vein breathing at his back. The old man did not move to help him. He did not need to.

---

He sat with his back to the Vein and shook. It was not the fever. The old man sat across from him with the lamp and let him shake.

"It wants them back," Jonah said at last.

"Yes."

"All of them. Every cutting in the Empire. It's been calling them for four hundred years and none of them can hear it."

"Yes."

"And the sickness. What takes the deep ones. What had me on the nineteenth day. That's not it killing them."

"No." The Censor's voice was very quiet. "That is it trying to make a man into a piece, from the wrong side. It cannot cut. It does not know how to make small. It is one thing and has never been anything else. But it can reach. It reaches into a man at the wall and goes looking for a place to be. There is no place, because a man is not a piece. It does not understand that. It keeps reaching, and the man dies of being reached for." He was quiet a moment. "Every man in the Cradle dies of being loved by something that does not know what a man is."

Jonah sat with that.

He thought of the Empire loving its Collars the same way, from a great distance, with a number where a hand should be. He didn't say it. The old man had just handed him the ugliest thing he knew about himself, and there was no room left in the hour for a smaller point.

"And the cut ones. Aulus of Marl on the slab. Corr says cut ones go faster. It knows there's a hole in you."

"Corr is a fool and is right, as usual. A man who has had a cutting taken out has a place in him where a piece of the wall was. The wall feels that place from across the Pit. It goes there. It tries to fill it. Bricks has lived eleven years because Bricks has the sense of a stone and the wall cannot get purchase on him. Most do not." The old man touched the white line on his own forearm, once. "I had a place. A large one. Seventy-one on the glass. The College cut it out of me in the Forum with the rod. I woke on a slab with a hole in me the size of a house. They put me in this rock, and in the first month the wall found the hole."

"You never told me that."

"You did not need it until tonight." The Censor looked at the lamp. "It reached for me in the Chamber, through the rock, and it had a hole to go to. I lay on that shelf eleven days with what you felt on the nineteenth day going through me. Come, come, you are mine. I was Master of the College. I had read every page. I understood what was happening to me better than any man it has ever happened to. And on the sixth day I understood what I could do about it."

"What?"

"Let it."

He said it flatly, to the lamp.

"Let it in. Open the hole. Let a piece of the wall come into it and stay, and be what you are. A man with a whole genius in him. It would probably kill me, the way it killed the nine. If it did not, I would be the only man in four hundred years who could hear the wall."

"And?"

"I lay there five more days and did not do it. I tapped on the rock to keep my hands busy. That was the first tapping. I said the College's words for holding a genius still until my voice went. On the eleventh day it stopped reaching, because I had made myself a stone, and it could find no purchase." He looked up. "I have been a stone for twenty years. I built a tunnel with a chisel. I wrote names on slabs. I have never once let it in. That is what I am."

"Why?"

"Because I was afraid." The voice did not change. "I told you I hid the box because I was afraid of what a whole genius would do in a man. True. I was more afraid of what it would do in me. I had been a senator and a Master and the best fang on the floor. I was seventy-one on the glass and I had never been afraid of anything I could name. I lay on that shelf and I was afraid of becoming something that could hear its mother."

Jonah did not look away.

"You did not have the choice," the Censor said. "You were dying on a deck with a Stripe over you. That is the whole difference between us, boy. Understand it exactly. You will spend the rest of your life being told you are brave, and you are not. You were cornered. I was not. And I chose the stone."

Jonah had a comparison ready, about a man who chose fear and called it caution, next to a boy who never had a choice and got called brave for it anyway. He kept it. The old man had just handed him the ugliest true thing he owned. There was no need to critique the wrapping.

"That's why you tapped to the dying," Jonah said. "Twenty years. Not to count them."

"To find one who would do what I did not. Yes. I said so on the twenty-ninth night. You turned around. I have never said I was proud of it. I have said it is correct that I die here."

---

"So what does it want from me," Jonah said. "Not from the pieces. Me. It let go on the nineteenth day. It let go tonight. Twice it's had me and twice I've walked off with a mouthful of it. You say it can't stop a piece of itself, but it's the size of the Pit. It could have kept the door shut. It didn't. Why?"

The Censor did not answer at once.

"I have thought about that every night since you tapped four four seven one on my wall. Remember that I have been wrong about the wall before." He folded his hands. "I think it does not know what you are either. It reached for you on the nineteenth day and found a piece of itself already there, whole, that could hear it. The first in four hundred years. It said come, because that is all it knows how to say. The piece went, and turned around, and came back with a mouthful. I think the wall felt that. For four hundred years it has had nothing to think about but come. Now it has this."

"This."

"You. A piece that hears it and does not obey. A piece that says no. It has never had one. And I think, though I would not put it on a slab, that it is doing with you what you did with the wrong-arm fang for a hundred and thirty nights."

"What's that?"

"Finding out what shape you are."

Jonah sat with his back against it and felt it breathe.

"And when it knows?"

"Then it will want something, and I will be dead. You will have to work out what, and whether to give it." The old man picked up the lamp. "That is the far end. I can see the near end. You held the door of the wall open tonight, looked through it, and let go. No man has done that. You are about to have a fever. Sit. Count. Then come with me. There is something in the Chamber you should see, and I would rather you saw it tonight than found it after."

---

The fever was longer than the others. He counted it down. When it was done and he could stand, something had gone over inside him, and it was not small. Not the lock; he knew the lock. A whole door. The genius settled into a coat a size larger than it had been an hour ago, and he could feel the edges of the size.

The old man looked at his face in the lamp. "Yes. That is what strain is when it is not a fight. You were nearer the wall tonight than on the nineteenth day, and you did not go, and it has learned from that. If I were a Flamen with a glass I would say you have walked out of the Tiro band and into the next. The ceiling you would have been fused at nine is a very long way below you." He turned to the tunnel. "It means nothing. It is a number. Come."

The tunnel was the width of a stooping man and a hundred paces long, every hand's breadth of it cut with a chisel worn to half its length. He crawled through behind the lamp and did not think about the years in it. Thinking about it was a debt, and the old man had said not to carry one.

The Chamber was as it had been. The shelf. The bowl. Sixteen slabs leaning on the rock.

"Sit," the Censor said. "The first one. Read it."

It was in the old tongue, in the small even hand, in columns. Numbers, and beside some of the numbers names, and beside some of the names a word. Marl. A field. Anvil, the yards. Halyard, a ship. The first column was the year 366. The first number was one. Beside it stood a name three words long. He had heard it once, tapped, badly, on the twenty-ninth night of the haul.

Aulus Terentius Pharus. Latium. The College. Senator.

"I wrote it the first night," the old man said behind him. "Before I had a chisel. With a piece of the shelf. It is the worst hand on any of the slabs and I have never corrected it." A pause. "You will want to know why I am first. It is not vanity. A man who counts must be counted, or the count is a lie. Nobody has ever read it but you."

Jonah read down.

The second year. The third. Hundreds of numbers with nothing beside them: men who came down the Throat and went into the wall and never once, in the Cradle, said who they were. Names. Fathers. In the ninth year, a number with Marl beside it, and a field. Then a second word in the same hand. Pell.

He stopped.

"Fourth slab," the Censor said. "Ninth year. He said his name in the Cradle. He did not say his brother's. He said, my brother's on Marl, tell him it was a field. I have had that written eleven years with nobody to tell it to. You will tell him."

"Yes."

"Tonight. Before the haul."

"Yes."

He read to the sixteenth slab, the twentieth year, the fourth column. Four four seven one. His name, his moon, his yard. Beside those, smaller, his father's: all of it, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder. He sat on the floor of a stone room with his back to a wall that was finding out what shape he was. He looked at his father's name in a dead man's language and said nothing for a long time.

"Everyone is on these," the old man said. "Everyone who came down. I did not choose. That is what a count is." He stood beside Jonah and looked at the slab. "When I die, they are yours. All sixteen. You will not be able to carry them; they weigh what the wall weighs. You will carry them the way you carry the rest."

"There's a great deal in there already."

"I know. I have heard most of it through a wall." The Censor put his hand on the top of the sixteenth slab. "I have kept this count twenty years in a room the size of your father's, and I assure you, boy. There is always room. That is the only thing about a count that is kind."

He took his hand away.

"Go and tell Pell. Say it slowly. Say the field; he will want the field. Then carry your buckets, say your names, and sleep. Tomorrow the wall gives ten. You will choose a man, and you will not choose soft."

---

Pell was on the shelf above his in the sleeping gallery, under the lamp that never went out. Jonah stood beside the shelf in the dark and said his name, and the farmer's eyes opened.

"You've got the face," Pell said.

"What face?"

"The adding one. You get it when you've got a number for somebody and you're working out how to hand it over." He pushed up on one elbow. "Whose."

"Your brother," Jonah said.

Pell did not move.

"He came down in the ninth year. Three fifty-seven. He said his name in the Cradle. He said, my brother's on Marl, tell him it was a field." Jonah said it slowly. "It was a field. The north forty, he said. It didn't make quota. That's the whole of it. He went into the wall in the ninth year and somebody wrote it down. I've read it, and now it's said."

Pell looked at the roof of the gallery.

"Who wrote it," he said, after a long time.

"Someone who counts."

"The rock."

"Yes."

"You've seen it."

"I've read it. In the old tongue, in a hand. Your brother's name and the field. It's kept, Pell. It's been kept eleven years. It was waiting for somebody to carry it up the road."

The old man from Marl lay very still. Then he turned his face to the rock wall of the gallery, away from the lamp. After a while he said, to the wall, in the voice he used for things he had seen before:

"The north forty. That's right. That was the one. Bad drainage." A breath. "He always said it was the drainage."

Then, very quietly, to the wall, he began to sing. It was not a field song, or not one Jonah knew. Jonah stood in the dark and let him, and did not go to the haul until it was done.

In the morning the wall would give ten, and he would have to choose a man.
