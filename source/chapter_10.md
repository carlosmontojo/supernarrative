# Chapter 10: Mother

The wall was warm under the chisel, and then it was warmer.

Nineteen days. Nineteen nights on the shelf with both hands on his chest, holding the pull back through the seam until the bell. Nineteen mornings saying his number and cutting one shard with arms that no longer needed to pretend. Every night the pull was a little stronger. Every morning he was a little less. He had thought, Corr said a month. I'm on nineteen and I'm winning.

He was not winning. He was a rope, and the two ends had been patient. On the nineteenth day one of them stopped.

He was in the seventh chamber, in his place beside Corr, with his shard cut and in the bucket and the chisel back against the Vein, because you stood at the wall until the bell. Under his ribs, it leaned, quieter in the day.

The chisel was warm in his hands. The rock under his feet was warm. And the pale lines in the surface of the Vein, the thin ones he had looked at every day for nineteen days, had moved. He knew where they went. They did not go there anymore. They went to him. They had thickened and turned and gathered toward the chisel, and where the steel touched the wall they came together into a pale patch the size of a hand. The patch was wet, and it was moving up the steel.

"Corr."

Corr looked. Corr had stopped looking at the wall years ago. He looked now, and put his chisel down.

"Step back," Corr said. "Now. Don't drop it. Just step back with it."

Jonah tried.

His hands did not come off the chisel. The chisel did not come off the wall. The pale film had reached his hands and gone through the skin without breaking anything. It was in him, and it was cold.

Under his ribs, it stood up.

It did not lunge. It had learned. It stood up inside him and went to his hands, all of it, the whole of it, up his arm and past the ring and past the closed cut on his forearm, into his hands, and out.

Out through the skin. Out into the film on the chisel. Out into the wall.

"Corr," he said again, and it did not come out right.

Corr had him by the shoulders. Corr was hauling backwards with his whole weight, and Jonah's arms went straight and his hands stayed on the steel and the steel stayed in the wall. Corr's feet slid on the wet rock.

"Let go of it," Corr said. "Let go. Let go."

He couldn't.

There was nothing to see. His hands were on a chisel and something was leaving through them. The pale patch was not pale anymore. It was dark, darker than the rest, the dark of the glass slab. It spread into the wall, ink into water. It was his, and it was going home.

Mother, it said, in the going.

And the wall answered.

Not to his ears. It came up the chisel and through his hands into the place under his ribs, which was emptying. Not a word, not a sound, not the pull. It was the biggest thing he had ever felt. Standing in the Kestrel's hold with the whole legion transport around him, every deck, knowing where the furniture was. Except it was not a ship. It was a mind the size of the planetoid, and it had turned toward the small dark thing coming home through a chisel, and it was saying, with the whole of itself, yes.

Yes. Come. You are mine and you were taken, and come.

Jonah stood between them with steel in his hands and understood exactly what he was.

He was the piece in the way.

Under his ribs, it was going home, and the wall was taking it back. Neither of them was cruel. Neither of them was thinking about the body the small one had lived in for a month, any more than a man walking through a door thinks about the door. He was meat between two minds. And what was going through his hands was not only it. It was him. The parts it had been through, the arm, the shoulder, the heart it had stopped at. His heart went from fast to very fast to something he had no name for. His knees went. Corr went down with him, still hauling. He did not let go of the chisel, because he could not. He heard Corr shouting, far off. Then he heard nothing.

---

He woke in the dark, cold, and somebody was singing.

Not Pell. He knew Pell's songs. This voice was lower and rougher, singing something without words, or with words he didn't know, from very close. He turned his head toward it, and the turn took everything he had.

"Don't," the voice said, breaking off. "Lie still. You're in the Cradle. You know what that is?"

"No."

"Then you're new. Lie still anyway."

He lay still. He was on rock, on his back, with something under his head. The dark was whole. And the cold was not the cold of the Throat. It was in him, in the middle, in the place under his ribs. The place was not empty and not full. It was a room with the door open, and something standing in the doorway, half out, looking back.

"It's still here," he said.

"What is?"

He didn't answer. The singing man let it go.

"I'm Ulli," the voice said after a while. "I'm deep. Sixth year. They sent me up to carry you down, because I'm the one that carries them. I carried you. Now I'm sitting with you, because that's what I do after." A pause. "You're a cut one. I saw the arm. Cut ones don't usually make it as far as the Cradle. Vein takes them at the wall, mostly. Corr's right, they go fast. You've been down here two days."

"Two."

"Two. Bricks brought your bowl. I ate it. You weren't going to." No apology in it.

"You ate my bowl," Jonah said.

"You weren't going to eat it. You were dying."

"I might have surprised you."

"You didn't. I checked." Ulli's voice was rough, and something in it was almost amused. "Next time don't almost die on a day I'm hungry."

"The Cradle's a cut off the Deep. Where the Vein's thickest. They put the sick ones here because it's kind, they say. The Vein wants them, so let them be near it, so it can finish. Nobody works this gallery. Nobody comes but me." The rough voice went quiet. "It's warm. You'll have felt that. Warmest place in the Pit."

It was. The cold was in him, but the rock under him was warm, and the air, and the dark. He understood where he was. The Cradle had a wall of Vein, and they had laid him with his back against it, because the wall wanted him, and it was kind.

"It's not finished," he said.

"No. You're talking. They don't talk, at the end. They sing." Ulli was silent a moment. "That's why I sing. So they've got something to sing with. So it's not just the wall."

"What does the wall sing?"

The singing man didn't answer for so long that Jonah thought he'd gone.

"Names," Ulli said at last. "Not in words. But that's what it is. You'll hear it, if you last. Everybody it's taken. It's got them all, and it goes through them. I sing over the top so the new ones don't have to listen." He shifted on the rock. "Corr thinks I'm mad. Corr's been on the Shallows four years and thinks the Vein's a wall. I've been in the Deep six and I know what it is. It's a thing that counts."

Jonah lay in the warm dark with the cold in the middle of him and thought about what stood half out of him, looking back, and what it had said, going.

What he wanted to say was that he was afraid to ask whether Ulli had ever heard his own name come up out of the wall. What he asked instead was smaller.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"If I start singing. Don't sing over me."

"Why?"

"I want to hear what it's got."

---

The Vein came for the rest of him on the third day, and that was when the rock began to tap.

He'd been sliding. He knew it. A day against the warm wall with Ulli singing, and the cold going out from the middle toward the edges. Each hour there was less of it in him and more of it past his hands, in the wall, in the warm, going home. It was not leaving. It had never been leaving. It was being taken. And it took the parts of him it had been through with it, because it didn't know how not to.

He had tried to hold it. Both hands on his chest, as on the shelf. There was nothing to hold. You could hold something back through a seam. You could not hold something that was already on the other side.

So he lay and slid.

And the rock tapped.

He heard it under Ulli's singing and thought it was the cold. Then his heart. Then Ulli stopped singing and it was still there. It was in the rock behind his head. Not the Vein wall. The rock beside it, the cut edge of the Cradle. The Vein did not tap. The Vein breathed. This was small and hard and dry. Steel on stone. It had a pattern.

"Ulli."

"I hear it."

"What is it?"

"Don't know. Sometimes it does that, this gallery. Not often." The rough voice was careful. "I don't answer it. I don't know what answers back."

Jonah listened.

He had grown up in the yards. Cutter's boy at nine, cutter at twelve, pilot at fifteen, all of it in vacuum on the hulls with a suit on and no channel worth the name. You talked to a man forty metres down a hull by putting your glove on the plate and tapping, and he tapped back. Yard-tap. Every Ferrum child learned it before they learned to read.

This was not yard-tap. It was slower, and the gaps were wrong. But it was close. Close enough that if you knew the one you could feel the shape of the other. He lay with his head against the rock and felt the shape, and it said:

*Cut one.*

He did not move. The cold had his body, and he was listening.

*Cut one. Hear me.*

He got his hand up. It took a long time. He got the knuckles onto the rock beside his head and tapped, in yard-tap, because it was what he had. Four and a pause. Four and a pause. Seven and one. The rock was silent. Then it tapped back, slow, in his own code, badly. A man speaking a language he had only ever read.

*Four four seven one. Good. Listen. It is going.*

Yes, Jonah tapped.

*You cannot hold it. No one can. Do not try.*

I know.

*It is not leaving. It is abandoning you.*

He read that twice with his knuckles on the rock, because it was not the same thing and the rock had meant it.

Why, he tapped.

*Because you are dying and it can tell. It lives in you. When the body it lives in stops, it goes back to the wall or it stops too. It is not betraying you. It is a rat going over the side of a ship that is going down, and it is right, and the ship is you.*

I can't stop dying, Jonah tapped.

*You can. That is the entire lesson and I have three minutes to teach it.*

A pause. Then the taps came harder and faster, a man who had waited twenty years to say something and could not get it short enough.

*Listen. It has no eyes. It has no ears. It cannot hear a word you say and it never will. It has one instrument and that instrument is pointed at you, from the inside, and it reads what you are. Not what you say. Not what you decide. What you are. You cannot lie to it. Nine men have tried it in this room and I listened to all nine.*

Then what do I do.

*Stop agreeing.*

He lay in the dark with the cold at his shoulders and did not follow.

*You are not dying of the wall, boy. You have been dying of agreeing with it for three days. It reads you as a man who is finished, because that is what you are, and so it is going. Be something else and it will read that instead. You have about a minute.*

"What's it saying?" Ulli said, low.

"That I've given up." Jonah's jaw was stiffening. "And it can feel me give up. It's in me."

"Have you?"

He went to say no, and stopped, because the thing behind the door had just told him he could not lie to a creature that read him from the inside, and it seemed a poor moment to start practising on Ulli.

"Yeah," he said. "Three days ago. About four in the morning."

He tapped, slowly, because slow was all he had:

How do I want to live. I've been trying for three days.

*No. You have been trying not to die. That reads the same as quitting, because it is the same thing lying down.* A pause, and then the taps were careful, spaced, a man setting something on a table. *Wanting to live is not general. It is always about something in particular. Find the particular things. Say them out loud.*

Out loud. It can't hear me.

*It cannot. You can. A man believes his own mouth before he believes his own head, and the only thing in this room I need changed is what you are. Say them. Now.*

Jonah lay on the floor of the Cradle with a mind the size of the planetoid pulling something out through his hands, and understood the instruction, and thought it was the stupidest thing anybody had ever told him to do.

He did it anyway, because he was out of better ideas and had ninety seconds.

He opened his mouth. His jaw was cold.

"Tom Vale."

Nothing happened. Ulli did not move. The rock did not tap.

He said it again, and this time he put the whole of it in, because half of it was a noise and a noise was not the point.

"Tom Vale. My father. Nine fingers. He sold a set of numbers once for a winter's food and never told me, and I know what they were now, and he never will. He's sitting in a room on Anvil right now waiting for me to come home."

Something moved in his chest. Not the lorica. His heart.

"Mara Ansel. She measured my hand while I was asleep and made me a ring out of a ship. It's in my arm. She thinks I'm dead."

He was breathing faster. He noticed it from a long way off, the way you notice a gauge.

"Ochoa. My captain. He sat down carefully because of his back, and he told me not to touch it, and I touched it."

It had stopped going.

He felt it stop. Half out, in the warm, with the whole wall saying come. It had not decided anything and it had not forgiven him. The needle had simply moved, and it was reading him again.

"Pell. Sings to the floor; his brother's in your wall. Corr, who tells everybody a month. Bricks. I broke his hand and he still doesn't know why. Decimus, who carried a bandage ten kilometres down a shaft for a number."

"Cut one," Ulli said. "What are you doing?"

"Arguing," Jonah said, and kept going.

"Hask. I killed him in a corridor and he was nothing and I've still got him." His throat shut. He made it open. "A boy from Marl who said no once, and I don't know his name, and I'm sorry. He should have one anyway."

He was shaking now, and the shaking was not the cold. The cold had stopped at his throat and was not going further.

"Titus Varro. Killed him too. He's in my feet."

Then the one he had not meant to say, which came out in the warm dark before he could stop it and could not be taken back.

"Livia Vibia. Brought me water in a cup and asked what I was called. She's asleep on Anvil right now believing something her brother told her, and it isn't true, and nobody is ever going to tell her."

His whole body was going.

"Marcus Vibius Priscus." His voice had changed. He heard it change. "Burned the seal. Put his hand on my shoulder and told me to be precise."

A breath.

"I'm going to be precise."

It turned around.

---

He would never be able to say what that was like, either.

It had been half out, in the warm, going home, and it turned and came back, and the wall said come with everything it had and it did not go.

It had not understood a word. He was certain of that, then and for six years after. It did not know what a father was, or a captain, or a girl with a cup. It had understood the only thing it was built to understand, which was the state of the animal it lived in, and the animal had changed while it was reading.

A minute ago the reading had been: finished.

Now it was: not yet, and here is a list of the reasons, and the list is long.

That was all. There was no bargain in it and no kindness. The old man would spend five years making sure Jonah never once forgot how little had happened on that floor, and how much it turned out to be worth: a thing with no eyes had taken a second reading, and the second reading was different.

It came back.

Back through his hands, reading, the same as it had come in on the derelict, except now it was reading its own road home. It found the cold and went through it, and the cold went out where it went. It found his heart and stopped there, again, interested. His heart went from the nameless speed to very fast, to fast. He breathed.

And on its way back through the wall, it took something.

He felt it pass through the pale wet place going the wrong direction. The wall was still saying come. It said, in whatever way it said things, no. And as it said it, it opened its mouth.

Not a mouth. But that. It went through the door and took a bite of the door going out. A mouthful of the wall, of the warm, of what counted. A child taking bread off the table on its way out. The wall did not stop it. The wall did not know how to stop a piece of itself. The piece came back through Jonah's hands with the rest, went down into him, and was folded away. It was not small.

He felt it settle. And he felt, for the first time since the box, what the old man would later call the number, the figure a Flamen wrote on a glass, except from the inside. A change in the shape of what he could hold. He had been a man carrying something. He was, all at once, a man carrying something bigger, and it fit. Fever came up through him from the middle out, the cold going the other way. He lay on the floor of the Cradle with his back to the warm and shook with it and laughed.

It was not a good laugh. Ulli made a sound.

"You're not singing," the deep man said.

"No."

"You were going. I've sat with forty. I know the sound. You were going and you started saying names at the wall and now you're." Ulli stopped. "What are you?"

"Cold," he said. "Hungry."

That was the other thing. He felt it as he said it. It had come back with a mouthful of the wall. In a month it had never once been full, but its hunger had been a fire's hunger, without size or end. This was different. It had eaten a piece of what it came from, and now it knew what that tasted like. The hunger had a size. He could feel the edges of it, the edges of a room in the dark. He was going to spend the rest of his life knowing exactly how big it was, and how full, and how far from full.

Right then, not far. It had eaten well. It lay behind his heart, fed, folded, the raw place from the Throat gone smooth. And it was doing something he had never felt it do.

It was resting. It had listened since the box, every hour, with that flat total attention, and it had stopped. It had gone home, and turned around at the door, and come back with bread. For the first time, it was not listening to anything at all.

The rock tapped.

He got his knuckles up. Easier now. The cold was gone from his arms.

*Number,* the rock said.

Four four seven one, he tapped.

A long pause. Ulli had stopped breathing beside him, or gone so quiet it was the same. Then the rock tapped, slowly, in his own yard code, done badly, by someone who had waited a long time to say it to a man who could hear:

*Counted.*

Then nothing. The rock was rock. The Vein breathed against his back, warm, kind, wanting him. Behind his heart, it rested and said nothing, because it had never said anything. Ulli sat in the dark and did not sing.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"Who's in the rock?"

The deep man was quiet a long time.

"There's a cut off the Deep," he said at last. "Past the Cradle. Sealed. Steel door, no lamp, nobody goes in. Tullus's men go down once a month with a lamp and come back without it. Six years in the Deep and I've never seen what's behind it." He shifted. "Some of the old ones say there's a man. Some say there was, and he died, and it's the Vein now, tapping. Some say it's the Censor."

"The what?"

"That's what they call it. Him. Whatever taps. Because it counts." Ulli's voice went rough again. "It counts, cut one. That's all anyone knows. Every man that comes down the Throat. They say it knows every number in the Pit and every name the number had before, and it keeps them. Like the wall does. Only it's not the wall."

Jonah lay with his back to the Vein and his knuckles on the rock.

Counted, it had said. That was the whole of what it had to give, and it had given it.

"Ulli. How long do the sick ones last in here? Once they start to go."

"A day. Two."

"How long was I?"

"Three. And you turned around." The deep man's voice was very quiet. "Nobody turns around."

"I know," he said. "I've been told."

He closed his eyes. The fever came up through him, warm, from the middle out, and behind his heart it rested. Somewhere in the rock behind his head, past the Cradle, behind a steel door, something that counted had heard him say every name he had and had said one word back.

I'm going to find out what you are, he thought. And then, before the fever took him, the other thought, the one that would keep him alive for six years:

I'm going to find out what I am. And you already know.
