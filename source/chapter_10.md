# Chapter 10: Mother

It took the Vein nineteen days to come for him, and it came through his hands.

He'd held it off every night. That was the thing he wanted the old man to understand, later, when he told it. He had not been careless. He'd lain on the shelf with both hands on his chest and held the pull back through the seam until the bell, nineteen nights, and got up each morning and said his number and gone to the wall and cut one shard slowly with arms that did not need to pretend anymore, and every night the pull had been a little stronger and every morning he had been a little less, and he had thought, this is how it goes, Corr said, a month, and I'm on nineteen days, and I'm winning.

He was not winning. He was a rope, and the two things on either end of him had been patient, and on the nineteenth day one of them stopped.

---

It was the wall.

He was at it with the chisel, in the seventh chamber, in his place beside Corr, with the thing under his ribs going through him the way it did in the day, quieter, leaning, and the dark breathing depth of the Vein in front of him, and he'd cut his shard. One. Slowly. He'd dropped it in the bucket and gone back to the wall and put the chisel to it because that was what you did, you stood at the wall until the bell, and the wall was warm.

It had always been warm. Since the first day. Warm the way the cloth had been warm through his glove.

It got warmer.

He noticed it the way you notice a room getting dark, late, all at once. The chisel was warm in his hands. The rock under his feet was warm. And the pale lines in the surface of the Vein, the thin ones that ran through the dark like the lines on a map, had moved. He'd looked at them every day for nineteen days and he knew where they went, and they did not go there anymore. They went to him. They had thickened, and turned, and gathered, the way water gathers toward a drain, and where the chisel touched the wall they came together into a place the size of a hand that was paler than the rest, and the pale place was wet, and it was moving toward the steel.

"Corr," he said.

Corr looked. Corr had been in the Shallows four years and had stopped looking at the wall a long time ago, and he looked now, and he put his own chisel down.

"Step back," Corr said, quietly. "Now. Don't drop it, just step back with it."

Jonah tried.

His hands did not come off the chisel. The chisel did not come off the wall. The pale wet place had reached the steel, and had gone up it, he could see it going, a film on the metal, and where it reached his hands it went through the skin the way the ring had gone into his arm, without breaking anything, and it was in him, and it was cold, and the thing under his ribs stood up.

It did not lunge. It had lunged on the first day and hit the inside of him. It had learned. It stood up inside him the way a man stands up when the door he has been waiting at opens, and it went to his hands, all of it, the whole of it, the way it had come in on the derelict, up his arm, reading, except backwards, and he felt it go past the place where the ring was, and past the closed cut on his forearm, and into his hands, and out.

Out through the skin. Out into the pale wet film on the chisel. Out into the wall.

He watched it happen. He would say that, later, and the old man would say, what did it look like, and Jonah would say, nothing, it looked like nothing, my hands were on a chisel and something was leaving through them and I could feel it go and there was nothing to see. There was the wall, breathing. There was the pale place, and it was not pale anymore, it was dark, darker than the rest, the dark of the glass slab, the dark of the thing under his ribs, and it was spreading into the wall like ink into water, and it was his, and it was going home.

Mother, it said. In the going.

And the thing on the other side of the wall said something back.

He heard it. Not with his ears. It came up the chisel and through his hands and into the place where the thing under his ribs had been, which was empty now, or emptying, and it was not a word and it was not a sound and it was not the pull. It was the biggest thing he had ever felt. It was the feeling of standing in the Kestrel's hold with the whole of the legion transport around him, all of it, every deck, and knowing where the furniture was, except it was not a ship, it was a mind, and it was the size of the planetoid, and it had turned toward the small dark thing coming home through a chisel and it was saying, with the whole of itself, yes.

Yes. Come. You are mine and you were taken and come.

And Jonah stood between them with steel in his hands and understood, in the way he understood debris, exactly what he was.

He was the piece in the way.

The thing under his ribs was going home and the thing in the wall was taking it back, and neither of them was cruel, and neither of them was thinking about the body the small one had been living in for a month, any more than a man walking through a door thinks about the door. He was meat between two minds. And the small one was going, and the big one was pulling, and what was going through his hands was not just the thing under his ribs, it was him, the parts of him it had been through, the arm and the shoulder and the heart it had stopped at and been interested in, and he felt his heart go from fast to very fast to the thing he didn't have a name for, and his knees went, and he did not let go of the chisel because he could not, and he heard Corr shouting, far off, and then he did not hear anything.

---

He woke in the dark and he was cold, and somebody was singing.

Not Pell. He knew Pell's songs by now, nineteen days of them, the Marl field songs to the floor at the change of shift. This was a different voice, lower, rougher, and it was singing something without words, or with words he did not know, and it was coming from very close, and when he turned his head toward it the movement took everything he had.

"Don't," the voice said, breaking off. "Lie still. You're in the Cradle. You know what that is?"

"No."

"Then you're new. Lie still anyway."

He lay still. It was not hard. He was on rock, on his back, with something under his head that might have been a blanket, and the dark was not the dark of the sleeping gallery with its lamp at the end. It was a whole dark. And the cold was not the cold of the Throat. It was in him, in the middle of him, in the place under his ribs where the thing had been, and the place was not empty. He could feel that. It was not empty and it was not full. It was a room with the door open and something standing in the doorway, half out, looking back.

"It's still here," he said.

"What is?"

He didn't answer. The singing man let it go.

"I'm Ulli," the voice said, after a while. "I'm deep. Sixth year. They sent me up to carry you down, because I'm the one that carries them, and I carried you, and now I'm sitting with you, because that's what I do after." A pause. "You're a cut one. I saw the arm. Cut ones don't usually make it as far as the Cradle. Vein takes them at the wall, mostly, and Corr's right, they go fast. You've been down here two days."

"Two."

"Two. Bricks brought your bowl. I ate it. You weren't going to." No apology in it. "The Cradle's a cut off the Deep. It's where the Vein's thickest. They put the sick ones here because it's kind, they say. The Vein wants them, so let them be near it, so it can finish. Nobody works this gallery. Nobody comes but me." The rough voice went quiet. "It's warm. You'll have felt that. It's the warmest place in the Pit."

It was. He hadn't noticed, because the cold was in him, but the rock under him was warm, and the air, and the dark, and he understood where he was. He was against it. The Cradle was a room with a wall of Vein, and they had laid him with his back to the wall, because the wall wanted him, and it was kind.

"It's not finished," he said.

"No. You're talking. They don't talk, at the end. They sing." Ulli was silent a moment. "That's why I sing. So they've got something to sing with. So it's not just the wall."

"What does the wall sing?"

The singing man did not answer for so long that Jonah thought he had gone.

"Names," Ulli said at last. "It sings names. Not in words. But that's what it is. You'll hear it, if you last. Everybody it's taken. It's got them all, and it goes through them, and I sing over the top so the new ones don't have to listen." He shifted on the rock. "Corr thinks I'm mad. Corr's been on the Shallows four years and thinks the Vein's a wall. I've been in the Deep six and I know what it is. It's a thing that counts."

Jonah lay in the warm dark with the cold in the middle of him and thought about that, and about the room with the door open, and about what was standing in the doorway half out and looking back, and about what it had said, going.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"If I start singing. Don't sing over me."

"Why?"

"I want to hear what it's got."

---

The Vein came for the rest of him on the third day, and that was when the rock began to tap.

He'd been sliding. He knew that. He'd lain against the warm wall for a day with Ulli singing and the cold in the middle of him going out, slowly, from the middle toward the edges, the way the dark goes out to the edges of a room when the lamp is down, and he'd felt the thing in the doorway go a little further out each hour. It was not leaving. It had never, he thought, been leaving. It was being taken. The big thing on the other side of the wall had said come, and the small thing had gone, and was going, and every hour there was less of it in the room and more of it in the doorway and more of it past the doorway, in the wall, in the warm, going home, and it was taking the parts of him it had been through with it, because it did not know how not to.

He had tried to hold it. He'd put both hands on his chest in the dark the way he had on the shelf and held, and there had been nothing to hold. You could hold a thing back through a seam. You could not hold a thing that was on the other side of a door.

So he lay and slid.

And the rock tapped.

He heard it under Ulli's singing, and thought it was the cold, and then thought it was his heart, and then Ulli stopped singing and it was still there, and it was in the rock behind his head, the wall he was lying against, not the Vein wall but the rock beside it, the cut edge of the Cradle, and it was not the Vein. The Vein did not tap. The Vein breathed. This was small and hard and dry, steel on stone, and it had a pattern.

"Ulli."

"I hear it."

"What is it?"

"Don't know. Sometimes it does that. This gallery. Not often." The rough voice was careful. "I don't answer it. I don't know what answers back."

Jonah listened.

He had grown up in the yards. That was the thing. He had been a cutter's boy at nine and a cutter at twelve and a pilot at fifteen, and every one of those had been done in vacuum, on the hulls, with a suit on and no channel worth the name, and the way you talked to a man forty metres down a hull in vacuum was that you put your glove on the plate and tapped, and he tapped back, and there was a code for it, yard-tap, that every Ferrum child learned before they learned to read.

This was not yard-tap. It was slower. The gaps were wrong. But it was close, the way the old Latin on the manifests was close to the words in the prayer, close enough that if you knew the one you could feel the shape of the other, and he lay with his head against the rock and felt the shape, and it said:

CUT ONE.

He did not move. His whole body did not move, because the cold had it, and because he was listening.

CUT ONE. HEAR ME.

He got his hand up. It took a long time. He got it onto the rock beside his head, the knuckles, and he tapped, in yard-tap, because it was what he had, four taps and a pause and four and a pause and seven and one, and then lay there, and the rock was silent for a long moment, and then it tapped back, slow, in his own code, badly, the way a man speaks a language he has only ever read.

FOUR FOUR SEVEN ONE. GOOD. LISTEN. IT IS GOING.

Yes, Jonah tapped.

YOU CANNOT HOLD IT. NO ONE CAN. DO NOT TRY.

I know.

GIVE IT A NAME.

He lay with his knuckles on the rock and did not understand. The cold had reached his shoulders. The thing in the doorway was nearly all the way through it. He tapped, slowly, because slow was what he had:

It has one.

NOT FOR IT. TO IT. There was a pause, and the tapping came again, harder, faster, a man who has been waiting a long time to say a thing and has found the words wrong. THINGS WITH NAMES STAY. IT DOES NOT KNOW WHAT A NAME IS. IT KNOWS WHAT THE WALL IS. THE WALL HAS EVERY NAME IT EVER TOOK. GIVE IT ONE THE WALL DOES NOT HAVE.

Jonah lay in the dark.

Ulli had gone very quiet, beside him. Ulli, who did not answer the rock because he did not know what answered back, was listening to a dying man tap on a wall with his knuckles and get tapping back, and was saying nothing, and Jonah was grateful for that in a distant way, from the middle of the cold.

Give it a name.

He thought, I don't know what that means. And then he thought, yes I do. It was the wall. It was what Ulli had said. It sings names. Everybody it's taken. It's got them all. And the thing in the doorway, going home, was going to a thing that had every name it had ever taken and went through them, one after another, in the warm dark, a thing that counted, and the thing in the doorway had come from that, had been cut from that, and it was going back to be counted.

Unless it had something the count did not.

He didn't have a name for it. He'd tried. He'd lain on his back in the tenements with a blanket over the vent and asked it what it was, and it had listened, and had no name, and he had none to give it. It was a thing from a box.

But he had names.

He had a great many names. He had been keeping them. He had stopped trying to put them in order a long time ago and had just kept them, all of them, in the place where he kept things, and he had never once thought of them as something he could give.

He opened his mouth. It was hard. His jaw was cold.

"Tom Vale," he said.

Nothing happened. Ulli did not move. The rock did not tap. The thing in the doorway was in the doorway.

"Tom Vale. My father. Nine fingers. He sold something once for a winter's food and never told me. He cried at the Flamens' house." His voice was a whisper and it was the loudest thing in the Cradle. "Mara Ansel. She measured my hand in my sleep. She gave me a piece of the Halcyon and I've got it in my arm and you put it there." He stopped for breath. The cold was at his throat. "Ochoa. Captain. He sat down carefully. He said don't touch it. I touched it."

The thing in the doorway had stopped going.

He felt that. He felt it the way he'd felt it stop on the derelict when it found his heart. It had stopped, half out, in the warm, with the big thing on the other side saying come, and it was listening. It always listened. And the thing on the other side of the wall was saying come, come, you are mine, and the small thing was standing in the doorway hearing a man in the dark say the name of a captain who had sat down carefully, and it did not know what a name was, and it was interested.

"Pell," Jonah said. "Sings to the floor. His brother's in your wall. Corr. Says a month. Bricks. I broke his hand. He didn't understand. Decimus. He brought a bandage down ten kilometres for a number. Hask." His throat closed and he made it open. "Hask. I killed him. He was nothing and I killed him and I've got him. A boy from Marl who said no once. I don't know his name. I'm sorry. I don't know it." He was shaking. "Varro. Titus Varro. I killed him too. He's in my feet. He laughed at you. Morrow. Livia."

He stopped on that one. He hadn't meant to say it. It had come out with the rest and it was in the warm dark now and he could not take it back.

"Livia Vibia. She brought me water in a cup. She asked my name. She's asleep on Anvil believing something her brother said and it wasn't true and she doesn't know and nobody's going to tell her, and I said hers to the dark on the transport because it was the last true thing." His whole body was shaking now. "Priscus. Marcus Vibius Priscus. He burned it. He put his hand on my shoulder and told me to be precise. I'm going to be precise."

The thing in the doorway turned around.

---

He would never be able to say what that was like, either.

It turned around. It had been half out, in the wall, in the warm, going home to a thing that counted, and it turned in the doorway and looked back into the room, at the man on the floor of it saying names, and the big thing on the other side said come, with everything it had, and the small thing did not go.

It did not know what a name was. He was sure of that. It did not know what a father was or a captain or a sister or a boy who said no. But it had listened to every one of them and it had heard something in them that the wall did not have, and the wall had everything, the wall had every name it had ever taken and went through them in the dark, and the thing in the doorway had been going home to be one of those, one more in the count, and it had just heard a man give it, instead, all of his.

Not to keep. He wasn't giving them to keep. He was giving them the way you give a thing to a man who is leaving so he'll have it where he's going.

It came back.

It came back through the doorway and into the room and into him, and it came the way it had come in on the derelict, up through his hands, reading, and this time it was reading its own way home, and it found the cold and went through it and the cold went out where it went, and it found his heart and stopped there, again, interested, the way it always had been, and his heart went from the thing he had no name for to very fast to fast, and he breathed.

And on the way back through the wall, it took something.

He felt that too. He felt it pass through the pale wet place, the door, going the wrong way, and the big thing on the other side was still saying come, and the small thing said, in whatever way it said things, no, and as it said it, it opened its mouth.

Not a mouth. But that. It went through the door and it took a bite of the door on the way, a mouthful of the wall, of the warm, of the thing that counted, the way a child takes a piece of bread off a table going out, and the wall did not stop it, because the wall did not know how to stop a piece of itself, and the piece came back through Jonah's hands with the rest and went down into him and was folded away, and it was not small.

He felt it go in. He felt it settle. And he felt, for the first time since the box, what the old man would later call the number, the thing a Flamen wrote on a glass, except from the inside, as a change in the shape of what he could hold. He had been a man carrying a thing. He was, all at once, a man carrying a bigger thing, and the thing fit, and the fever came up through him from the middle out like the cold going the other way, and he lay on the floor of the Cradle with his back to the warm and shook with it and laughed.

It was not a good laugh. Ulli, beside him, made a sound.

"You're not singing," the deep man said.

"No."

"You were going. I've sat with forty. I know the sound. You were going and you started saying names at the wall and now you're." Ulli stopped. "What are you?"

"Cold," Jonah said. "Hungry."

And that was the other thing. He felt it as he said it. The thing under his ribs had come back and had brought a mouthful of the wall with it, and it had never in a month been full, but it had been hungry the way a fire is hungry, without a size, without an end. This was different. It had eaten a piece of the thing it came from, and it knew now what that tasted like, and the knowing had a size. The hunger had a size. He could feel the edges of it, like the edges of a room in the dark, and he understood that he was going to spend the rest of his life knowing exactly how big it was, and how full, and how far from full.

It was, right then, not far. It had eaten well. It lay behind his heart, fed, folded, with the raw place on it where the piece had been torn off at the Throat gone smooth, and it was doing a thing he had not felt it do before.

It was resting. It had been listening since the box, every hour, with that flat total attention, and it had stopped. It had come home and turned around at the door and come back with bread, and it was, for the first time, not listening to anything at all.

The rock tapped.

He got his knuckles up. It was easier. The cold was gone from his arms.

NUMBER, the rock said.

Four four seven one, he tapped.

There was a pause. It was long. Ulli had stopped breathing, beside him, or had gone so quiet it was the same. And then the rock tapped, slowly, in his own yard code, done badly, by a man who had been waiting a long time to say it to someone who could hear:

COUNTED.

And then nothing. The tapping stopped. The rock was rock. The Vein breathed against his back, warm, kind, wanting him, and the thing under his ribs lay behind his heart, fed, fitting, resting, and said nothing, because it had never said anything, and Ulli sat in the dark and did not sing.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"Who's in the rock?"

The deep man was quiet a long time.

"There's a cut off the Deep," he said at last. "Past the Cradle. Sealed. Steel door, no lamp, nobody goes in. Tullus's men go down once a month with a lamp and come back without it. Six years I've been in the Deep and I've never seen what's behind it." He shifted. "Some of the old ones say there's a man. Some say there was, and he died, and it's the Vein now, tapping. Some say it's the Censor."

"The what?"

"That's what they call it. Him. Whatever taps. Because it counts." Ulli's voice went rough again. "It counts, cut one. That's all anyone knows. Whatever's in there, it counts. Every man that comes down the Throat. They say it knows every number in the Pit and every name the number had before, and it keeps them. Like the wall does. Only it's not the wall."

Jonah lay with his back to the Vein and his knuckles on the rock.

Counted, it had said.

He thought about the wall, singing names in the dark, everybody it had taken, going through them. He thought about the thing under his ribs, that had gone home to be one of those and had turned around at the door because a man had said Tom Vale out loud. He thought about a thing behind a steel door with no lamp that tapped a dying man's number back at him in a code it had learned badly, and said counted, as if that were the whole of what it had to give, and as if it were enough.

"Ulli," he said. "How long do the sick ones last? In here. Once they start to go."

"A day. Two."

"How long was I?"

"Three. And you turned around." The deep man's voice was very quiet. "Nobody turns around."

"I know," Jonah said. "I've been told."

He closed his eyes. The fever came up through him, warm, from the middle out, and the thing behind his heart rested, and somewhere in the rock behind his head, past the Cradle, behind a steel door, something that counted had heard him say every name he had and had said one word back, and he thought, I'm going to find out what you are. And then, before the fever took him, he thought the other thing, the one that would keep him alive for six years, and it was this:

I'm going to find out what I am. And you already know.
