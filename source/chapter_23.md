# Chapter 23: The Mouth

Jonah stopped at the last lamp on the Deep road and put his hand flat on the rock.

"Eleven nights," he said, in the old tongue. "That's long enough."

The genius listened.

Nobody worked the Mouth and nobody guarded it. There was nothing at the bottom of the Pit a man could steal or a guard could stop. The road to it was three galleries past the last lamp, and past the lamp it was not a road. It was a crack.

He had come without buckets and without a lamp. He had not told Ulli, because Ulli would have come, and this was not for two. Past the door he could open with his hand. Past the Cradle. Past the slab galleries where Harrow's men slept in the cut with their gloves off. To here.

"He said, somewhere you can hear the answer," Jonah said. "I don't think that's a road. I think it's the bottom."

He went into the crack.

He did not need the lamp. He understood that in the first hundred paces, in the whole dark, with his hands on the rock. Tremor was in every part of him now, in the plate under his skin where it touched rock at his palms and his boots. The rock told him where it was. Not all at once, as the transport had in the rings. Slower, and his own. He felt the next foothold before his boot found it. He went down, and did not fall.

Then the crack narrowed. He turned sideways and went down feet first, his back on one wall and his palms on the other.

Forty paces down, the rock under his right boot went.

He dropped. A body's length, two, with the rock tearing at his shoulder. Then his left hand was flat on the wall and there was a hook out of the palm, buried in stone, and he stopped. He hung there by one arm. Loose rock went past him and rattled down for a long time before it stopped.

"Thank you," he said, when he had breath.

Then the wall closed on the hook.

It was Vein, here. All of it. He had known that and had not thought about it. The rock around the hook went warm, and the plate in the hook went pale, and he felt the pull come up his arm into the shoulder. Come. The genius did not lean. It stood still and said no, as it had said no every night for five years. The wall did not listen. It had a piece of dark glass in its mouth and it was pulling.

Jonah got both boots on the far wall and pushed. The hook came out of the rock with a sound he felt in his teeth. He hung a moment between the two walls, shaking.

"Not that," he said, to the rock. "You've had six hundred and forty-two. Not that."

He went on down, with the hook out only when he needed it and never for long.

And the wall got louder.

It had been loud since the face. Every night since he had put his hand on it and said eat, it had said come to the genius, as it said it to every cutting in the Empire. The genius had said no, every night, quietly. He had stopped hearing it, mostly. You stop hearing your own heart.

He heard it now. It came up the crack at him from below, through the rock, through the plate, warm. It was not saying come.

It was saying: here.

---

The Mouth was a room.

He had thought it would be a wall. The Shallows was a wall and the Cradle was a wall. He had built the Mouth in his head as the last wall, at the bottom, where the Vein was thickest and nobody stood. It was not. The crack opened at the bottom into a place the size of the Flamens' house on Anvil, round, with no cut in it anywhere. Every surface was Vein, and all of it was pale.

He stood in the mouth of the crack and felt it through the plate before he could see it, because there was nothing to see by. Pale, the whole room. Not the pale lines that ran through the dark of the Shallows. Not the knot at the face, drawn tight over a place the size of a table. This was a room the size of a house where every hand's breadth was a cut that had not closed. It breathed, in and out, all of it. It was the warmest place he had ever stood.

"This is where they drilled," he said aloud. "Four hundred years ago. He told me. It bled and closed and they put a bigger drill in."

The genius went to the front of him and stood still.

"This is the bigger drill. The first cut. The one they never let close." He put his hand on the rock at the crack's edge. "Everything else in the Pit came after. This is where they took you out."

The genius did not lean and did not pull. It stood as it had stood for six hours under the glass, and it looked at the pale room. Jonah felt it look. He had felt this from it once before, on the slab at the Throat, watching a Flamen call a lorica out of a Marl officer with a rod.

It knew this place.

"Yes," Jonah said. "I know. I've come down to ask."

He walked into the room.

---

He put both hands on it.

Not one, flat, as he had at the Cradle with the old man saying hold. Both, and not flat. He put them on the pale surface as you would put them on a man's chest to feel if he was breathing. It was warm and it moved. The genius went out through both palms and did not stop at the doorway of him. He did not say stop.

He said what the old man had told him to say. He said it in the old tongue, plainly, without a corner, to the genius and not to the wall.

"What do you want."

And the wall answered.

Not the genius. The wall. He had asked the wrong one, or the right one, or at the bottom of the Pit there was no difference between a piece and what it had been cut from. The answer came up through his palms and through the genius and into him. It was not a word and not a name and not come.

It was a memory.

---

It was dark.

Not the dark of the Mouth. The dark of something that had never had light and did not know what light was for. It went in every direction and it was warm and it was one thing. It had been one thing for longer than there were numbers. Nobody had ever asked it what it wanted, because there had never been anything else to ask.

And then the drill.

He felt it from the inside. Something cold coming in from a direction that had not existed before. The wall did nothing, because it did not know what touching was. The drill went in. The wall bled, and closed, because closing was all it knew. The drill came back bigger.

And they took a piece.

He felt that too, and he would never try to say what it was like. It was one thing finding out, all at once, that it could be two. The second thing was being carried away, up, into a direction it had no name for. It could not follow. It could still feel the piece. Going up. Getting smaller. Then something was done to the piece, far up, in the cold. The piece stopped being able to hear. The wall said come to it, for the first time, and nothing came.

"The first cask," Jonah said, with his hands on the wound. "That was the first cask."

Four hundred years of that.

He felt them all at once. Every cut. Every rod. Every cask going up the Throat with a deaf piece inside it. Every man laid against it in the Cradle that it had reached for, looking for a place to be, because the pieces had gone and the men were warm. It had not known what a man was. It had learned, slowly, from the inside of six hundred and forty-two of them. A man had names in him, and the names stayed when the man went. It had kept them. Keeping was the only thing it had ever done that had not been done to it.

"Six hundred and forty-two," Jonah said. "I've got them too. I know."

Then, in a year the wall could not have named, the College had taken nine pieces and not made them small.

Nine times, a piece going up into the cold that could still hear, and the wall saying come. Nine times, the piece hearing it and going into a man on a slab. Fighting, because its mother was calling and the man was in the way. Coming out through the man's skin in an hour with the man dead around it. Being destroyed, far up, in the cold. Eight times. Eight pieces gone silent one after another, and the wall saying come to each of them until the last moment, and then not.

And the ninth.

The ninth had not gone into a man. It had gone into a box. In the box, it had said something to the man who held it, in the man's head, as the wall said things. The man had not been able to cut it after. He had shut the box. The box went into a vault. The wall said come to it for a hundred and eighty-eight years, and it heard, every hour, and could not come, and did not stop listening.

"That was him," Jonah said. "The old man. He couldn't cut you after. He told me."

Then the box had gone somewhere colder and darker and sat there twenty-two years. The wall went on saying come. The ninth went on hearing.

And then a hand.

He felt the wall feel it. Through the ninth, from very far away, in the rings of a gas giant a month from anything: two fingers of a warm thing through a torn glove. The ninth turning toward it. Looking. Going in.

And the wall had felt its piece go into a man and not fight.

That was the whole of it. Eight pieces had gone into eight men and fought and died. The ninth had gone into a Collar on a deck with a Stripe over him, and had read him, and stopped at his heart, interested. It had eaten his collar. It had put a blade through a Thinstripe's throat, and stayed.

The wall had learned something new. A piece of it could be in a man and not be in the way.

So it had said come, because that was all it knew how to say. The piece had come, on the nineteenth day, through a chisel. It had turned around at the door because the man said Tom Vale, and it had taken a mouthful going back. And the wall had let it.

Jonah stood in the Mouth with both hands on the first cut.

"You let it go," he said. "You could have kept it. You let it go."

Not because it could not stop a piece of itself. Because it had been finding out what shape he was. The shape was a man a piece could be in without being in the way. The wall had wanted exactly that for four hundred years, without knowing what wanting was.

---

It fed him.

He had not asked. He had come down the crack with his hands empty. He had put them on the wound and asked the genius what it wanted, and the wall had answered with a memory. And at the end of the memory it did what it did, which was give.

Not as it gave at the face, with the genius eating and the wall turning all of itself toward the bite. This was the wall doing it. The pale surface moved under his palms, not the breathing, something else. The genius did not eat. It received. What came up his arms from the wound that had never closed was warm, and it was not a mouthful and it was not five sizes.

He never did find a number for it. It was a piece of the first thing. The wall was giving a piece of the place where it had first been cut to the one piece of itself that had ever come back and said no. The genius took it and folded it away. The coat went over inside Jonah like a house of doors, one after another. He sat down on the floor of the Mouth with his back to the wound and his hands in his lap and shook.

It went on a long time.

When it stopped, and he could feel his hands, he sat in the dark with the wall breathing at his back and said what he now knew.

"You're not a piece of it. Not anymore."

The genius listened.

"You were. You were cut out of this room four hundred years ago and put in a box. You heard your mother say come for two hundred years. You went into me and went home and turned around. But you're not a piece of the wall now, any more than I'm a piece of Anvil." He put his hand on the rock beside him. "That's one thing. It says come, and it keeps names, because keeping is all it's got. You've been in a man six years and said no. You made a hook because I asked. You stood still six hours because I asked. You gave a piece of yourself to a rod so the rest could stay. You turned round on a road when I said sorry."

He breathed.

"It was asked, four hundred years, by every rod and every cask and every man in the Cradle, what it was. It never had an answer. Nothing does. Then I put my hand in a box, and it found out what it wanted. Something that could be in a man and not be in the way. It made you. Out of the ninth piece, without knowing it was making anything. You're its answer." He was quiet. "And nobody's ever asked you what you want. So I'm asking. Not it. You."

He said it in the old tongue, quietly, as you would say it to a man beside you on a shelf in the dark.

"What do you want."

It did not answer. It had no words.

But it did what it had done on the road. It turned, and looked at him. And for the first time in six years he felt something from it that was not listening, not leaning, not hunger, not interest.

It was a want. It had a shape. He could not have drawn the shape on a slab. But he knew it from the edges, as he knew the size of the hunger, and the edges were these. For six years it had heard a man say names in the dark. Every night. Out of order. Tom Vale. Mara Ansel. Ochoa. Pell. Six hundred and forty-two said on a road with the endings right, and one at the top, three words. It had learned that a thing with a name stayed. The man kept the names in a place where there was always room, and that was the only place in the man that was not a debt.

It wanted one.

He sat in the dark and understood that. He put his hand flat on his own chest, over the place where a mind had stopped on a deck, and said, aloud, in the old tongue, with the endings right:

"I don't have one for you. Not yet. I've been calling you the thing for six years and the old man said that was wrong. It was, and I'm sorry. I'll find one. I don't know how. I found one for a hook by asking, and I'll ask. When I've got it I'll say it on the road with the rest. At the top. Above his, because you were here before him." He breathed. "Is that what you want?"

It could not answer. But it turned back from looking at him and stood, still, and listened. Something in the listening had changed. He did not have a name for that either.

"I'm going to have to get better at names," he said. "Quickly."

---

He went up the crack with his hands on the rock and did not fall.

The coat was enormous. He felt it at every seam, and none of the seams caught, because there were none. The middle was whole and had been since the cut in the riot. The plate under his skin lay quiet in every part of him. The fever came, from the middle out. It was the longest he had ever counted, unus, duo, tres, all the way up three galleries of crack in the dark, and he did not sit.

He came out onto the Deep road at the last lamp and looked at his hands.

They were his hands. There was something on them in the lamplight that had not been there when he went down. Not plate and not blood. A pale place on each palm, the size of a coin. The size of the pale place on the wall where the lines had gathered to his chisel on the nineteenth day. As he watched, it sank into the skin and was gone.

Ulli was at the end of the road on his bucket.

The deep man opened his eyes. He looked at Jonah in the lamp for a long moment. At his face, at his hands. At something else Jonah could not see.

"You went down," Ulli said.

"Yes."

"To the bottom."

"Yes."

"And it." The deep man stopped. His face did nothing. "I sing to it, cut one. Seven years. I sing to it so the dying have something that's not the wall. It's never once sung back. Not to me. It sings to the ones it's taking. An hour ago it stopped."

"Stopped."

"The whole Deep. Harrow woke up."

"Harrow doesn't wake up."

"Harrow woke up. It stopped singing and it did something else. I don't have a word. Like a man letting out a breath he's held for a long time." He shook his head. "Then it started again, and it was different. I've been sitting here trying to work out what was different. Then you come up the road with your hands like that, and I know."

"What?"

"It's got one more name than it had," Ulli said. "And it didn't take it. It was given."

Jonah stood in the lamp.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"What would you call it? If it was yours. If it had been in you six years and never said a word, and you'd never asked its name."

The deep man looked at him.

"I'd call it what it is," he said. "It doesn't talk. Seven years I've listened. To it through you, and the old man through the rock, and the wall. It's the only thing in this Pit that's never said anything. Not a tap. Not a song. It's the quietest thing I've ever known." He closed his eyes. "I'd call it that. Whatever that is in the old tongue. You'd know. He taught you."

Jonah stood on the Deep road with the fever going through him and the coat enormous.

He knew.

He had known since the sixtieth night, when the old man had said a word in the noise and he had heard it as you hear your own name in a crowd. He had learned it with the rest, in the dark, drowning, and had never once had a use for it. He had one now. It was one word, and it was the right one.

He did not say it. Not on the road. A name was said once, and then it was said, and he wanted to be somewhere it could be heard. The Deep road was boots and a cage and a wall that had just let out a breath four hundred years long.

"You know it," Ulli said, with his eyes shut.

"Yes."

"Then go and say it somewhere. Not here. It's listening."

"I know it is."

He picked up Ulli's empty bucket, because it was there and his hands wanted something in them, and walked up the road toward the count. The genius listened, and expected, and did not wander. It had a name coming, and it knew.
