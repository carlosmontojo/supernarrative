# Chapter 23: The Mouth

Jonah stopped at the last lamp on the Deep road and put his hand flat on the rock.

"Eleven nights," he said. "Sorry about the wait."

The lorica listened, which was the only thing it had ever done.

Nobody worked the Mouth and nobody guarded it. There was nothing at the bottom of the Pit a man could steal or a guard could stop. The road to it was three galleries past the last lamp, and past the lamp it was not a road. It was a crack.

He had come without buckets and without a lamp. He had not told Ulli, because Ulli would have come, and this was not for two. Past the door he could open with his hand. Past the Cradle. Past the slab galleries where Harrow's men slept in the cut with their gloves off. To here.

Ask it somewhere you can hear the answer, the old man had said. He had not said where that was. Jonah had worked it out the way he worked out everything now, which was by counting: the Vein got thicker the further down you went, and the thickest part of it was the part nobody had ever cut.

He went into the crack.

He did not need the lamp. He understood that in the first hundred paces, in the whole dark, with his hands on the rock. Tremor was in every part of him now, in the plate under his skin where it touched rock at his palms and his boots. The rock told him where it was. Not all at once, as the transport had in the rings. Slower, and his own. He felt the next foothold before his boot found it. He went down, and did not fall.

Then the crack narrowed. He turned sideways and went down feet first, his back on one wall and his palms on the other.

Forty paces down, the rock under his right boot went.

He dropped. A body's length, two, with the rock tearing at his shoulder. Then his left hand was flat on the wall and there was a hook out of the palm, buried in stone, and he stopped. He hung there by one arm. Loose rock went past him and rattled down for a long time before it stopped.

"Thank you," he said, when he had breath.

Then the wall closed on the hook.

It was Vein, here. All of it. He had known that and had not thought about it. The rock around the hook went warm, and the plate in the hook went pale, and he felt the pull come up his arm into the shoulder. Come. The lorica did not lean. It stood still and said no, as it had said no every night for five years. The wall did not listen. It had a piece of dark glass in its mouth and it was pulling.

Jonah got both boots on the far wall and pushed. The hook came out of the rock with a sound he felt in his teeth. He hung a moment between the two walls, shaking.

"Not that," he said, to the rock. "You've had six hundred and forty-two. Not that."

He went on down, with the hook out only when he needed it and never for long.

And the wall got louder.

It had been loud since the face. Every night since he had put his hand on it and said eat, it had said come to the lorica, as it said it to every cutting in the Empire. The lorica had said no, every night, quietly. He had stopped hearing it, mostly. You stop hearing your own heart.

He heard it now. It came up the crack at him from below, through the rock, through the plate, warm. It was not saying come.

It was saying: here.

---

The Mouth was a room.

He had thought it would be a wall. The Shallows was a wall and the Cradle was a wall. He had built the Mouth in his head as the last wall, at the bottom, where the Vein was thickest and nobody stood. It was not. The crack opened at the bottom into a place the size of the Flamens' house on Anvil, round, with no cut in it anywhere. Every surface was Vein, and all of it was pale.

He stood in the mouth of the crack and felt it through the plate before he could see it, because there was nothing to see by. Pale, the whole room. Not the pale lines that ran through the dark of the Shallows. Not the knot at the face, drawn tight over a place the size of a table. This was a room the size of a house where every hand's breadth was a cut that had not closed. It breathed, in and out, all of it. It was the warmest place he had ever stood.

The old man had told him about this room without knowing it was a room. Four hundred years ago the first survey had put a drill into this rock. The rock had bled and closed. They had come back with a bigger drill, and taken out the first cutting, and every lorica in the Empire had come out of the hole they made. Every cut in the Pit since was made somewhere else, further up, where the wall let them.

This was the first one, and it had never closed.

The lorica did not lean and did not pull. It stood at the front of him, still, the way it had stood six hours under a Flamen's glass. And Jonah felt it recognise the place. He had felt that from it once before, on the slab at the Throat, watching a rod pull a lorica out of a Marl officer's arm.

"Yes," he said. "I thought so. That's why we came."

He walked into the room.

---

He put both hands on it.

Not one, flat, as he had at the Cradle with the old man saying hold. Both, and not flat. He put them on the pale surface as you would put them on a man's chest to feel if he was breathing. It was warm and it moved. The lorica went out through both palms and did not stop at the doorway of him. He did not say stop.

He said what the old man had told him to say. He said it in the old tongue, plainly, without a corner, to the lorica and not to the wall.

"What do you want."

And the wall answered.

Not the lorica. The wall. He had asked the wrong one, or the right one, or at the bottom of the Pit there was no difference between a piece and what it had been cut from. The answer came up through his palms and through the lorica and into him. It was not a word and not a name and not come.

It was a memory.

---

It came in order, the way the old man read a column, and Jonah understood it because it was arithmetic. The wall had no words. It had a count.

The count was this.

Four hundred years ago, one piece went out and stopped answering. That was the first cask. Then eleven thousand more, one at a time, up the Throat, each of them going quiet at the top. The wall said come to every single one and got nothing back. It did not know what dying was. It knew what a number that stopped answering was.

Then the men. Six hundred and forty-two of them laid against it in the Cradle, warm, with holes in them where cuttings had been cut out. It had reached into every one, looking for somewhere to be, and killed all of them by reaching. It had not meant to. It had never once been told what a man was.

It kept them anyway. Not their names; it had no words. Their shapes. Keeping was the only thing the wall had ever done that had not been done to it first, so it kept every man it had ever killed, and had been going through them, in order, for four hundred years.

Then nine.

Jonah felt the nine come up through his palms like a hand tightening. Nine cuttings the College had taken whole instead of cutting them small. Eight had gone into eight men on eight slabs, heard their mother calling from a rock a hundred years away, and torn out through the men to get to her. Eight men dead in under an hour. Eight pieces destroyed in the cold at the top. Eight numbers that stopped answering.

The ninth had gone into a box.

In the box it had said one thing to the man holding it, and the man had shut the lid and never opened it again. The box went into a vault. The wall said come to it for a hundred and eighty-eight years. It heard every word and could not move.

Then a hand.

Jonah felt the wall feel it: two fingers of something warm through a torn glove, in the rings of a gas giant a month from anywhere. The ninth turning toward it. Looking at it a long time. Going in.

And not fighting.

That was what the wall had brought him down here to show him. Eight pieces had gone into eight men and torn them open. The ninth had gone into a Collar on a deck, read him from the skin inward, stopped at his heart, and stayed. Six years, and the man was still alive.

On the nineteenth day the wall had said come, because come was the only word it had, and the piece had come.

And then the part Jonah had been carrying wrong for five years.

He had always assumed the wall let it go. Everyone would assume that; the wall is the biggest thing on the rock and a cutting is the size of a fist. He stood in the Mouth with his hands on the first cut and found out that the wall had not let go of anything.

It could not take it back.

He felt the moment from the inside, the way it had felt to something with no words: a piece of itself arriving home after a month away, and no hole anywhere in four hundred years of its own body that was the right shape for what had come back. It had been inside a man. It had eaten a decurion and a shackle and read a body from the skin in, and it had grown a shape doing it, and the shape did not fit. The wall pulled. The wall pulled with the whole of itself, the way it had pulled at every cask and every cutting since the drill, and the thing sat in the wound and would not go down.

So it went back to the man. There was nowhere else. And on the way out it bit the doorway, and the wall could not stop that either, because you cannot get hold of a piece of yourself that no longer fits you.

Jonah stood there and worked out what it meant, and his arms went cold.

"You couldn't keep it," he said. "All this time I thought you let it go, and you couldn't hold it. It didn't fit you anymore."

Nothing answered. The room breathed.

"And you liked that." He said it slowly, because he was getting there while he said it. "That's why I'm not dead. You've been drilling into your own skin for four hundred years to make something that could live in a man, and every one you made came out too small to hear you or too big to leave him alive. And then one came home that had done a month in a man and come out the wrong shape for you and the right shape for him." He breathed. "You didn't build it. You couldn't have. It happened in a hold on a ship you've never heard of, to a Collar who was bleeding out. And when it came back to show you, all you could do was let go of it and watch where it went."

He put his forehead against the warm.

"That's the worst thing I've heard in my life," he said. "Four hundred years and you got it by accident, off a man you were trying to kill at the time."

---

It fed him.

He had not asked. He had come down the crack with his hands empty. He had put them on the wound and asked the lorica what it wanted, and the wall had answered with a memory. And at the end of the memory it did what it did, which was give.

Not as it gave at the face, with the lorica eating and the wall turning all of itself toward the bite. This was the wall doing it. The pale surface moved under his palms, not the breathing, something else. The lorica did not eat. It received. What came up his arms from the wound that had never closed was warm, and it was not a mouthful and it was not five sizes.

He counted it, because the old man had made him count everything. He had gone down the crack at twenty-eight. He knew that number the way he knew his own hands: the old man had put it on him in the autumn, on the floor of the Chamber, and been right about every number for five years.

The first one went over and he thought, twenty-nine, and waited for it to stop.

It did not stop. Thirty went over. Then thirty-one.

Three grades. He had spent eighteen months of stick and haul and strain getting from twenty-six to twenty-eight, and he got three more in the time it took to sit down. A whole Blank's cutting was worth two. A guard was worth one, and you had to kill the guard.

The wall was giving a piece of the place it had first been cut to the only piece of itself that had ever come back and said no. The lorica took it and folded it away, door after door. Jonah sat down on the floor of the Mouth with his back to the wound and his hands in his lap and shook.

It went on a long time. When it stopped he said the number out loud, twice, so it would be a fact and not a feeling.

"Thirty-one."

When it stopped, and he could feel his hands, he understood something else. He had just asked the largest thing on this rock a question, and it had answered him, and it would answer again if he asked again. That was a kind of power he had never once had over anything in his life. It frightened him worse than the fever did.

Then he sat in the dark with the wall breathing at his back and got to the thing he had actually come down for, which was not the feed and never had been.

"Right," he said. "Your turn. You can't answer and I'm going to do it anyway, so bear with me."

The lorica listened, which was what it did.

"I've been calling you *the thing* since I was seventeen." He said it to the dark. "Six years. The old man pulled me up on it about a hundred times and I never once changed it, and I've just worked out why, sitting here, and it isn't flattering."

He rubbed his face with both hands.

"It's because a thing doesn't get owed anything. You can use a thing. You can feed it and drill it and put it in a guard's hip seam and never once ask what that was like for it, and none of that is rude, because it's a thing." He let out a breath. "You've been in me six years. You've never asked me for anything. Not once. Everybody else on this rock wants something off me, including the dead ones, and you've wanted nothing, and I've paid you back by calling you *it* for six years so I wouldn't have to think about it."

The room breathed. Nothing answered, because nothing could.

"And the wall's just told me the rest of it, which is that you didn't even come back for me. You came back because you'd grown the wrong shape and there was nowhere else to go." He laughed once, with no sound in it. "Six years I've been quietly assuming I was special. Turns out I was the only door that was open."

He put his hand flat on his chest, over the place where a mind had stopped on a deck.

"Doesn't change anything, though. That's the part I've just got to. You could have torn out through me any night of the last six years the way the other eight did, and you'd have been within your rights, and you never have." He was quiet a moment. "So you're getting a name. Not because it does anything. I've checked; it doesn't do anything. You're getting one because I've been rude to the only thing in my life that's never asked me for a single thing, and I've noticed, and I'd like to stop."

It turned round inside him and looked at him. He felt it do it. He had felt that twice before in six years and both times something enormous had happened afterwards, and he sat very still.

"I haven't got one yet," he said. "Give me a week."

---

He went up the crack with his hands on the rock and did not fall.

The coat was enormous. He felt it at every seam, and none of the seams caught, because there were none. The middle was whole and had been since the cut in the riot. The plate under his skin lay quiet in every part of him. The fever came, from the middle out. It was the longest he had ever counted, unus, duo, tres, all the way up three galleries of crack in the dark, and he did not sit.

He came out onto the Deep road at the last lamp and looked at his hands.

They were his hands. There was something on them in the lamplight that had not been there when he went down. Not plate and not blood. A pale place on each palm, the size of a coin. The size of the pale place on the wall where the lines had gathered to his chisel on the nineteenth day. As he watched, it sank into the skin and was gone.

Ulli was at the end of the road on his bucket.

The deep man opened his eyes. He looked at Jonah in the lamp for a long moment. At his face, at his hands. At something else Jonah could not see.

"You went down," Ulli said.

"Yes."

"To the bottom."

"Yes."

"You're shaking."

"I'm frightened, Ulli," Jonah said. "I asked it a question, and it answered, and I don't know what to do with a thing that big saying yes to me. I've never had that before. I don't think I want it twice in one week."

Ulli looked at him a long moment and didn't say it would pass, because he didn't know that it would.

"And it." The deep man stopped. His face did nothing. "I sing to it, cut one. Seven years. I sing to it so the dying have something that's not the wall. It's never once sung back. Not to me. It sings to the ones it's taking. An hour ago it stopped."

"Stopped."

"The whole Deep. Harrow woke up."

"Harrow doesn't wake up."

"Harrow woke up. It stopped singing and it did something else. I don't have a word for it. The way you'd let out a breath you'd been holding a long time, except it isn't a man." He shook his head. "Then it started again, and it was different. I've been sitting here trying to work out what was different. Then you come up the road with your hands like that, and I know."

"What?"

"It gave you something," Ulli said. "And you didn't take it. It was given."

"Given."

"Nobody down here has ever been given anything by that wall, cut one. It takes. That's the whole of what it does; I've watched it do it forty-three times." He shook his head slowly. "You walked all the way to the bottom to ask it a question instead of putting a chisel in it, and it gave you something. I don't know what that is. I'd like to."

"It's not nothing to me either."

"Go on, then," Ulli said, eyes shut again. "Say your names. I'll listen for both of us."

"I will," Jonah said.

"Then go."

Jonah stood in the lamp.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"What would you call it? If it was yours. If it had been in you six years and never said a word, and you'd never asked its name."

The deep man looked at him.

"I'd call it what it is," he said. "It doesn't talk. Seven years I've listened. To it through you, and the old man through the rock, and the wall. It's the only thing in this Pit that's never said anything. Not a tap. Not a song. It's the quietest thing I've ever known." He closed his eyes. "I'd call it that. Whatever that is in the old tongue. You'd know. He taught you."

Jonah stood on the Deep road with the fever going through him and the coat enormous.

He knew.

He had known since the sixtieth night, when the old man had said a word in the noise and he had heard it as you hear your own name in a crowd. He had learned it with the rest, in the dark, drowning, and had never once had a use for it. He had one now. It was one word, and it was the right one.

He did not say it. Not on the road. A name was said once, and then it was said, and he wanted to be somewhere it could be heard. The Deep road was boots and a cage and a wall that had just let out a breath four hundred years long.

"You know it," Ulli said, with his eyes shut.

"Yes."

"Then go and say it somewhere. Not here. It's listening."

"I know it is."

He picked up Ulli's empty bucket, because it was there and his hands wanted something in them, and walked up the road toward the count. The lorica lay flat and quiet the whole way up. It had a name coming, and somehow it knew.
