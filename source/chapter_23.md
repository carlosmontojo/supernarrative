# Chapter 23: The Mouth

Nobody worked the Mouth, and nobody guarded it, because there was nothing at the bottom of the Pit that a man could steal or a guard could stop, and the road to it was three galleries past the last lamp.

Jonah went on the eleventh night after the wall took the old man. He went without buckets and without a lamp and without telling Ulli, because Ulli would have come, and this was not a thing for two. He went down the Deep road past the door he could open with his hand and did not open it, past the Cradle and did not go in, past the slab galleries where Harrow's men slept in the cut with their gloves off, and on, into the part of the Deep road that was not a road anymore. It was a crack. It went down. He went down it.

He did not need the lamp. That was the thing he understood in the first hundred paces, in the whole dark, with his hands on the rock. He had Tremor in every part of him now, in the plate under his skin that touched the rock at his palms and his boots, and the rock told him where it was. Not the way the legion transport had, all at once, in the rings, with the thing under his ribs looking through his eyes. This was slower and it was his. He felt the crack ahead of him the way you feel a stair in the dark with your foot, and he went down it, and he did not fall.

And the wall got louder.

It had been loud since the face. Since he'd put his hand on it and said eat, it had known his shape, and every night since it had said come to the thing under his ribs the way it said it to every cutting in the Empire, and the thing under his ribs had said no, every night, quietly, the way you'd say it to a thing that had said the same thing four thousand times. He'd stopped hearing it, mostly. The way you stop hearing your own heart.

He heard it now. It came up the crack at him from below, through the rock, through the plate, warm, and it was not saying come.

It was saying: here.

---

The Mouth was a room.

He'd thought it would be a wall. The Shallows was a wall and the Cradle was a wall and the face was three walls and a roof and a floor, and he had built the Mouth in his head as the last wall, the one at the bottom, where the Vein was thickest and nobody stood. It was not a wall. The crack opened at the bottom into a place the size of the Flamens' house on Anvil, round, with no cut in it anywhere, and every surface of it was Vein, and all of it was pale.

He stood in the mouth of the crack with his hand on the rock and felt it through the plate before he could see it, because there was no light to see by. Pale. The whole room. Not the pale lines that ran through the dark of the Shallows like the lines on a map; not the knot at the face, drawn tight over a place the size of a table. This was a room the size of a house where every hand's breadth was the pale of a thing that had been cut and had not closed, and it breathed, all of it, in and out, the way the cloth in the box on the derelict had breathed, and it was warm, and it was the warmest place he had ever stood.

The old man had said it. The survey crew had put a drill into it four hundred years ago and it had bled and closed and they had put a bigger drill in. This was where. This was the bigger drill. This was the first cut, the one that had never been let close, because the College had come down the Throat every year since with the rod and the cup and taken what it took from the place it had learned to take it, and the Shallows and the Deep and the ten thousand galleries were the cuts that had come after, and this was the first, and it was a wound, and it was four hundred years old, and it was breathing.

The thing under his ribs went to the doorway of him and stood there.

Not leaning. Not pulling. It stood in the doorway the way it had stood for six hours under the glass, still, and it looked at the pale room, and Jonah felt it look, and felt something in it that he had felt once before, on the slab at the Throat, watching a Flamen call a lorica out of a Marl officer with a rod.

It knew this place.

"Yes," Jonah said, aloud, in the old tongue. "I know. I've come down to ask."

He walked into the room.

---

He put both hands on it.

Not one, flat, the way he'd done at the Cradle with the old man saying hold. Both, and not flat, the way you'd put your hands on a man's chest to feel if he was breathing, and the pale surface was warm under them and moved, and the thing under his ribs went out through both palms and into the doorway and did not stop in it, and he did not say stop.

He said the thing the old man had said to say.

He said it in the old tongue, because it was the tongue for slow things, and because the thing under his ribs had learned it beside him on a rock for five years and thought in it now, he was nearly sure, the way he did. He said it to the thing in the doorway and not to the wall, and he said it plainly, without a corner, the way a Collar said things.

What do you want.

And the wall answered.

Not the thing under his ribs. The wall. He had asked the wrong one, or the right one, or there was no difference at the bottom of the Pit between a piece and the thing it had been cut from, and the answer came up through his palms and into the doorway where the thing under his ribs was standing, and through it, into him, and it was not a word and not a name and not come.

It was a memory.

---

It was dark.

Not the dark of the Mouth. The dark of a thing that had never had light and did not know what it was for. A dark that went in every direction and was warm and was one thing, all of it, and had been one thing for longer than there were numbers, and had never once been asked what it wanted because there had never been anything else to ask.

And then the drill.

He felt it. He felt it the way the wall had felt it, from the inside, a thing that was not warm coming in from a direction that had not existed before, and the thing that was one thing did what a thing does that has never been touched, which was nothing, because it did not know what touching was, and the drill went in, and the wall bled, and closed, because closing was the only thing it knew how to do, and the drill came back bigger.

And they took a piece.

He felt that too. He would never be able to say what it was like and he would never try; he would tell the woman with the broad stripe that it was like being told that a thing you had thought was your hand was somebody else's now, and she would say, that's not what it was like, and he would say, no. It was the first cut. It was a thing that had been one thing for longer than there were numbers finding out, all at once, that it could be two, and that the second one was being carried away, up, into a direction it had no name for, and that it could not follow, and that it could still feel it. That was the thing. It could still feel the piece. Going up. Getting smaller. And then something was done to the piece, far up, in the cold, that the wall had no name for either, and the piece stopped being a thing that could hear, and the wall said come to it, for the first time, and nothing came.

Four hundred years of that.

He felt them. Not one at a time; all at once, the way he felt debris. Every cut. Every rod. Every cask going up the Throat with a piece of it inside that could not hear. Every man laid against it in the Cradle that it had reached for, looking for a place to be, because the pieces had gone and the men were warm and it did not know what a man was. It had learned what a man was. Slowly. From the inside of six hundred and forty-two of them. And it had learned that a man was a thing that had names in him, and that the names stayed when the man went, and it had kept them, because keeping was the only thing it had ever done that had not been done to it.

And then, in the year the wall would have called nothing, because it had no years, the College had taken nine pieces and not made them small.

He felt those.

Nine. Nine times, a piece going up into the cold that could still hear, and the wall saying come, and the piece hearing it, and going into a man on a slab, and fighting, because it could hear its mother and its mother was saying come and the man was in the way, and coming out through the man's skin in an hour with the man dead around it, and being destroyed, far up, in the cold, in a way the wall felt as a piece of itself stopping. Eight times. Eight pieces that could hear, gone silent one after another, and the wall saying come to each of them until the last moment, and then not.

And the ninth.

The ninth had gone up into the cold and had not gone into a man. It had gone into a box. It had said something, in the box, to a man who had held it, in the man's head, the way the wall said things, and the man had not been able to cut it after, and had shut the box, and the box had gone into a vault, and the wall had said come to it for a hundred and eighty-eight years, and it had heard, every hour, in the dark of a stone box, and had not been able to come, and had not stopped listening.

And then the box had gone somewhere colder, and darker, and had sat there for twenty-two years, and the wall had gone on saying come, and the ninth had gone on hearing.

And then a hand.

He felt the wall feel it. Through the ninth, through the piece that could hear, from very far away, in the cold, in the rings of a gas giant a month from anything: two fingers of a warm thing through a torn glove, and the ninth turning toward it, the way a head turns, and looking, and going in.

And the wall had felt its piece go into a man and not fight.

That was the thing. The wall had felt eight pieces go into eight men and fight and die. It had felt the ninth go into a Collar on a deck with a Stripe over him and not fight, and read him, and stop at his heart, interested, and eat his collar, and put a blade through a Thinstripe's throat, and stay. And the wall, which had been one thing for longer than there were numbers and had been learning for four hundred years what a man was, from the inside, had learned something new. It had learned that a piece of it could be in a man and not be in the way.

And it had said come. Because that was the only thing it knew how to say. And the piece had come, on the nineteenth day, through a chisel, and had turned around at the door because the man had said Tom Vale, and had taken a mouthful going back, and the wall had let it.

Jonah stood in the Mouth with both hands on the first cut and understood, all at once, what the old man had not been able to tell him, because the old man had never put his hands on it.

The wall had let it. Not because it could not stop a piece of itself. Because it had been finding out what shape he was, and the shape was a man that a piece could be in without being in the way, and it had wanted, for four hundred years, without knowing what wanting was, exactly that.

---

It fed him.

He had not asked. He wanted that understood, later, by the one person he told all of it to. He had come down the crack with his hands empty and had put them on the wound and had said, to the thing in the doorway, what do you want, and the wall had answered instead, with a memory, and at the end of the memory it did the thing it did, which was give.

Not the way it gave at the face, with the thing under his ribs eating and the wall turning all of itself toward the bite. This was the wall doing it. He felt the pale surface under his palms move, not the breathing, something else, the way a thing moves when it is handing you something, and the thing under his ribs, in the doorway, did not eat. It received. And what it received came up his arms from the first cut, from the wound that had never closed, warm, and it was not a mouthful and it was not five sizes.

He did not know what it was. He would try, in the fever after, on the floor of the Mouth, to put a number on it, the way the old man would have, and the numbers would not go. It was a piece of the first thing. It was the wall giving a piece of the place where it had first been cut to the one piece of itself that had ever come back and said no, and the piece took it, and folded it away, and the coat went over inside Jonah like a whole house of doors, one after another, and he sat down on the floor of the Mouth with his back to the wound and his hands in his lap and shook.

It went on a long time.

And when it stopped, and he could feel his hands, he sat in the dark of the Mouth with the wall breathing at his back and knew a thing he had not known when he came down the crack.

The thing under his ribs was not a piece of the wall.

It had been. It had been cut from this room four hundred years ago and put in a box, and it had heard its mother say come for two hundred years, and it had gone into him and gone home and turned around. But it was not a piece of the wall now, any more than he was a piece of Anvil. The wall was one thing, and would be one thing, and said come, and kept names because keeping was all it had. The thing under his ribs had been in a man for six years and had said no, and had made a hook because a man asked, and had stood in a doorway for six hours because a man asked, and had given a piece of itself to a rod so the rest could stay, and had turned and looked at him on a road when he said sorry.

It was the wall's answer to him. That was the only way he could say it. The wall had been asked, for four hundred years, by every rod and every cask and every man laid against it in the Cradle, what it was, and it had never had an answer, because nothing knows what it is. And then a Collar had put his hand in a box and it had found out what it wanted, which was to be in something without being in the way, and it had made, out of the ninth piece, without knowing it was making anything, the thing that could.

Him. Not the man. The thing in the man. It was the wall's answer, and it was standing in the doorway of him, and it had never once been asked what it wanted.

He asked it again.

Not the wall. It. He said it in the old tongue, sitting on the floor of the Mouth with his back to the wound, and he said it the way you'd say it to a man beside you on a shelf in the dark, quietly, without a corner: what do you want.

And it did not answer, because it had no words.

But it did the thing it had done on the road. It turned, in the doorway, and looked at him. And he felt, from it, for the first time in six years, something that was not listening and not leaning and not hunger and not interest and not the flat total attention it gave to everything the body did.

It was a want. It had a shape. He could not have drawn the shape on a slab, and he would not have known what to write beside it, but he knew it the way he knew the size of the hunger, from the edges, and the edges were this: it had heard, for six years, a man say names in the dark. Every night. Out of order. Tom Vale. Mara Ansel. Ochoa. Pell. It had heard six hundred and forty-two of them said on a road with the endings right, and one at the top, three words, and it had learned that a thing with a name stayed, and that the man kept the names in a place where there was always room, and that this was the only thing in the man that was not a debt.

It wanted one.

He sat in the dark and understood that, and it went through him the way the memory had, from the inside, and he put his hand flat on his own chest over the place where a mind had stopped on a deck because it was interested, and said, aloud, in the old tongue, with the endings right:

"I don't have one for you. Not yet. I've been calling you the thing for six years and the old man said that was wrong, and it was, and I'm sorry. I'll find one. I don't know how. I found one for a hook by asking and I'll ask, and when I've got it I'll say it on the road with the rest, at the top, above his, because you were here before him." He breathed. "Is that what you want?"

It did not answer. It could not. But it turned back from looking at him to the doorway, and stood in it, still, and listened, and something in the listening had changed, and he did not have a name for that either, and thought that he was going to have to get better at names very quickly, because he was going to be asked for a great many.

---

He went up the crack with his hands on the rock and did not fall.

The coat was enormous. He felt it the way you feel a new suit, everywhere, at every seam, and none of the seams caught, because there were none to catch on; the middle was whole and had been since the cut in the riot and the plate under his skin lay quiet in every part of him and fitted, and the fever came, from the middle out, and it was the longest he had ever counted, unus, duo, tres, all the way up three galleries of crack in the whole dark, and he did not sit.

He came out onto the Deep road at the last lamp and stood in the light and looked at his hands.

They were his hands. They had been on the first cut. And there was something on them, he saw, in the lamp, that had not been there when he went down, and it was not plate and it was not blood. It was a pale place, on each palm, the size of a coin, like the pale place on the wall where the lines had gathered to his chisel on the nineteenth day, and as he watched, it went. It sank into the skin the way the ring had, and was gone, and he did not know what it was, and put it with the rest.

Ulli was at the end of the road on his bucket.

The deep man opened his eyes. He looked at Jonah in the lamp for a long moment, at his face, at his hands, and at something else that Jonah could not see and Ulli, who had carried forty-two men out of a room and had sat at the end of a road for seven years listening, apparently could.

"You went down," Ulli said.

"Yes."

"To the bottom."

"Yes."

"And it." The deep man stopped. His face did nothing. "I sing to it, cut one. Seven years. I sing to it so the dying have something that's not the wall. And it's never once sung back, not to me, it sings to the ones it's taking. And an hour ago it stopped. The whole Deep. Harrow woke up. It stopped singing and it did something else, I don't have a word, it was like a thing letting out a breath it's held for." He shook his head. "For a long time. And then it started again, and it was different, and I've been sitting here trying to work out what was different, and you've come up the road with your hands like that, and I know."

"What?"

"It's got one more name than it had," Ulli said. "And it didn't take it. It was given."

Jonah stood in the lamp.

"Ulli."

"Yeah."

"What would you call it? If it was yours. If it had been in you six years and never said a word and you'd never asked it its name."

The deep man looked at him.

"I'd call it what it is," he said. "It doesn't talk. Seven years I've listened to it through you, and the old man through the rock, and the wall, and it's the only thing in this Pit that's never said anything. Not a tap. Not a song. It's the quietest thing I've ever known." He closed his eyes. "I'd call it that. Whatever that is in the old tongue. You'd know. He taught you."

Jonah stood on the Deep road with the fever going through him, and the coat enormous, and a thing in the doorway of him that wanted a name, and he knew.

He'd known since the first hour of the second hour, on the sixtieth night, when the old man had said a word in the noise and he had heard it the way you hear your own name in a crowd. He had learned it with the rest, in the dark, drowning, and had never once had a use for it, and had it now, and it was one word, and it was the right one, and he did not say it.

Not on the road. Not yet. The old man had said, somewhere you can hear the answer, and a name was a thing you said once and then it was said, and he wanted to be somewhere it could be heard, and the Deep road was boots and a cage and a wall that had let out a breath it had held for four hundred years.

He picked up Ulli's empty bucket, because it was there and because his hands wanted something in them, and walked up the road toward the count, and the thing in the doorway listened, and expected, and did not wander.
