# Chapter 39: Sertorius

"Indeterminate," Yara said. "That's the word he used."

"That's the word."

"A month I gave you. Thirty-one days in my hold and three on that rock and a Flamen with a cracked glass. You've come up my ramp at dusk with the hood down for the first time, and the word's indeterminate." She had her feet on the chart glass and the port full of Anvil's hull ring, lit, going round. "I could have told you that on the ladder."

"You did. You said, what are you, and I said I didn't know. It turns out the College doesn't either. It's got a word for not knowing, and the word's the whole of the reading." Jonah sat in the other seat. Pike was at the helm and did not turn round. "So I've come to tell you what I am, since the glass won't. And to ask for the rest of the ride."

"To the College dock."

"As crew. Hal's cousin, who worked the hold. From there I walk. Where I'm going next a Halyard ship can't take me, and you'd not want it to."

"The City."

"Yes."

A lamp came along the dock outside. Yara took her feet off the glass.

"Hood," she said.

Jonah put it up. The lamp stopped at the foot of the ramp. Two auxiliaries in grey looked up at a Halyard bridge with three crew on it and a captain looking back at them. They stood there for the length of a breath. Then the lamp moved on.

"They're counting hoods tonight," Pike said to the helm.

"They're counting two boys." Yara put her feet back up. "Take it down. You've earned the dusk." She waited until he had. "You said four names. Three on this rock. You've had three days. Gaspar's still drinking. I've seen him. Corin's still walking the dock road. I've seen that too, with a lamp over two boys and the face of a man who's lost a bout. And Morrow's paying his cutters tomorrow with coin from a Flamen, Pike says. He's not once said where the slab came from, and he's stopped looking tired."

"Yes."

"Three names, three days, and nobody's dead but two boys with bands who'd have cut Bram for a quota. That's not what I'd have counted from the man who took Kaspar's arm." She looked at the port. "So what are you."

Five years on a rock with a stick. Thirty-one days in a cask. A morning with his arm on a glass. He had the answer. It was not the old man's and it was not the glass's. He had told her on the ladder she would have it first.

"Four names and an Empire," he said.

---

"A purser," he said, "who saw something through a hatch and was afraid of it. Who wrote it down, and was seen to for it. That's what they said. Seen to. A house on the upper ring, the tax farm and the bank, for a letter."

"That's a man."

"That's what the Empire does with a man who writes things down. It makes him a publican and lets him ruin a province a paper at a time without raising his voice. He'd have done it on any rock, with any letter. The Empire's built so a Marchand always rises."

"And the Collar."

"A Collar who won a bout against me he couldn't have won, and signed, and took a band. He put a fang in his own people on Tessera for a stripe, and he got the stripe. He reads a quaestor's paper out in a square so a woman will stop waiting. He stands at the head of twelve men with a quota and says friend to the ones he'll not cut himself."

"That's a man too."

"That's a Collar who was called to sharpen at fourteen and lived because the Stripe was bored. He's spent every day since making sure he's never at the bottom of a bout again. The only way up for a Collar is over other Collars. He took it."

"And the drunk."

"The drunk did nothing." Jonah watched the hull ring go round. "He heard a letter read out and said, Tom's boy, he's a good boy, and went to sleep. He watched my father go past his window for a month with a bottle in his hand. He loved him. He says so. He sold me the story for a shard and told it right for the first time. He said he was sorry. He said it's what a drunk has."

"The old man said no innocents."

"He said a count that leaves out the ones who did nothing is a story you tell so you can sleep. I said yes. I've said it every night since. I've had three days on this rock to look at it. It's true, and it's not the whole."

"What's the whole."

"The Empire's built so that doing nothing is a thing you do. A man in a tavern with a bottle is safe if he keeps his head down and dead if he lifts it. Gaspar did the sum every morning for a month and came out at the bottle. The sum was right." He looked at her. "That's what a Stripe's law is worth. A drunk keeping his head down. A Collar taking a band. A purser writing a letter. It makes the four out of whatever men it's given. It would have made them out of me, if I'd stayed."

"So."

"So it's four names and an Empire. I'll not do the four and leave what made them. I'll not do the Empire and leave the four, because they're what it's for."

Yara took her feet off the chart glass.

"You'll kill four men," she said.

"I told the old man I'd decided what to do about them. He said he'd not ask. I lied to him." Jonah did not look away. "I've not decided. I've known that since a drunk said, you've got his hands, and I said, it's the ships. The Empire I can count. A seat and a Form, a City and a wall. The old man drew most of it before he died. A drunk I can't count."

"You'll not kill him."

"No. I'll make him say it sober, once, in a room, with somebody there to hear. That's his. I don't think it's enough."

"And the Collar."

"The Collar's got her. And a boy. He's got a stripe he'll lose the day the Empire doesn't need auxiliaries, which is the day the war's done. He knows it. He's afraid of it every morning, same as he's been afraid of everything since fourteen." He breathed. "I'll not touch the boy. I'll not touch her. What's left of him after that, I'll count when I'm nearer it."

"And the purser."

"The purser's got a bank," Jonah said, "and a bank's a thing that falls."

Pike, at the helm, said to the port: "He talks."

"And the fourth," Yara said. "The one in the City."

He did not answer at once.

He had said the fourth name every night for seven years with what the man had done. Read his heart and found it doing nothing. Sent him down a hole for what he carried. Burned it. Wrote him dead in a clerk's hand. And three nights ago at the rail he had said to Tacitus, not yet, not for him, for me. He had meant the fourth. He had also meant a man on a stool at the bottom of the Throat with a grey book on his knee, who had asked a guard what he was called.

"Marcus Vibius Priscus," he said. "Quaestor. He's got a seat, in a year. He'll stand for praetor and get it. He's got a Form. Verdict, the Vibian, the one that doesn't move. He looks at the place he'll put the blade before he draws. He's got a father who's a Gracchan, which is why I went down a hole. The seal was to the father. The son burned it so the father wouldn't hang." He stopped. "And he's got a sister."

"So?" Yara said.

"So nothing."

She looked at him.

Her heart was not held slow now. It was fast and it was hers, and she was reading him the way she read a hold. Jonah did nothing with his face. His heart, which he had spent seven years teaching to do nothing, did something.

Tacitus felt it. It did not say its word. It had said its word for the stair and been given a ship. It took what it felt and turned it over and put it somewhere. The place was not new. It had had that place for seven years, since a road and a rock. Since a name said every night in the dark and never once in the light. It put the fast heart there, under the name it had heard him not say, and kept it.

"So nothing," Yara said.

"So nothing."

"That's a Halyard's answer. It's the kind we give the Vigilant." She put her feet back on the chart glass. "I'll not ask again. I've a wife. I know what a man looks like who's said so nothing about somebody. It's your count. Carry it up the Throat with the rest."

"Thank you."

"But I've a thing to say, since I said I'd say the obvious once on every ship, and I've not yet." She looked at the port. "You read three names off a manifest. You read the fourth off a manifest. Then you said sister, and you stopped reading. That's all. It's said."

Jonah said nothing.

Pike said to the helm, very quietly, "He's stopped talking."

"Leave it, Pike."

He left it.

---

The hull ring went round twice more. The dock lamps went down to their night setting. Yara hit the lever and the Heron's ramp came up with a bang that went through the deck.

"You said you'd tell me what you are," she said, with her hand on the cold glass of the port. "First. Before the Empire. That was the price. You've told me four names and an Empire and so nothing. That's what you're going to do. It's not what you are."

"No."

"A thing has a name or it's not a thing. That's a Halyard's law too. We name a hull before we lay the keel. So."

"So."

"So who are you now?"

"There was a rock," he said, "at the far edge of the settled worlds. The Roma ark's people didn't want it. There was a legion they didn't want either. Halyard and Ferrum and Marl."

Yara's hand stayed on the glass.

"There was a general on the losing side who'd never once been beaten in the field. He made a Senate there, on the rock, with its own stripes and its own law. He held it eleven years. Then his friends killed him at dinner, because a man who can't be beaten won't let a war end."

"Go on."

"His head went to the City in a box, and the Princeps wept over it. The house was struck from the rolls. His lorica had eleven years of the far edge in it and a gift no glass could read. It's been asleep in a niche in the City for three hundred and ninety years, because nobody could find it to wake it. And there was a Form, built for a lorica that could put its fang anywhere. It was in a book that was burned. An old man read it once in a vault, and taught it to a Collar on a rock with a stick."

"Sertorius," Yara said.

She said it before he did.

"Yes."

"Every Halyard child knows that name." Her voice had not changed, and it had. "It's the one thing we're taught before we're taught to build. There was a man once who gave Halyards a Senate. On a rock. With Ferrum and Marl. He put a stripe on a Halyard's arm that the Halyard had earned and not informed for, and he held it eleven years. Then the Empire came and took it back and struck him."

"Yes."

"We've built ships without a licence ever since. A licence is what you ask the Empire for, and we asked once." She took her hand off the port. "That's who you're going to walk into their Senate as."

"A bastard branch. From the far Limes. It kept the name four centuries and has come to the City to claim the seat. The Senate will let it, because a seat's a vote, and there are men in that room who'll pay for one more and not ask where the far Limes is." Jonah kept his voice level. "That's the old man's count. He drew it on a slab. I'll wear a dead house's stripe, a gift called Quiet, and a name nobody remembers. I'll sit in their chamber. I'll find the four, and the wall, and the Princeps. And I'll say to the Princeps what the old man asked me to say."

"They'll not know what the name means."

"No."

"You will." Yara looked at him. "And every Halyard in the Belt will, the day it's said aloud in that chamber. You'll have us on your deck then, and you didn't ask for us. That's a price too. I'm telling you now, so it's said."

"So it's said."

"And the first name. The Gaius."

"The most common name in the rolls. Nobody remembers a Gaius. He said that. He said, nobody remembers a Jonah either, that's the point of both."

Yara almost smiled. It was the Halyard's almost, and this time it reached something.

"Then say it," she said. "So it's said, on a Halyard deck, before the Empire's heard it. That's what I asked for on the ladder. You've come to give it me a month early, and I'll take it."

---

He said the names first.

Hand flat on the cold glass of the port, in the old tongue, in order. Yara stood beside him and did not understand a word and listened to all of them. Pike did not turn round.

He said Tacitus first, above the old man, because it had been in the box before the old man was in the room. He said Aulus Terentius Pharus, Censor, who read every page, who drew the Pit and could not draw the way out of it. He said his father. He said Ochoa, who said nothing, which was the highest thing. He said Ullan of Marl, the east fields, two l's. He said Decimus Alba, of Etruria, Sula's father, first, before the rest. He said the boys at the road's end and the mate with half an arm and the Centurion who said oh. He said Dace and the boy who said what. He said the six hundred and forty-two from the slabs, all of them. He said the ones from the road, and the ones whose line said something was theirs.

He said Mara Ansel, and the Halcyon. He stopped, and went on.

He said the fourth name with what he had done, and did not say the other. Tacitus put the heart under it anyway.

Then he said his own.

"Jonah Vale. Anvil. Morrow's yard. Purser's pilot. Citizen for a week. Four four seven one. That's in the book. That's a name in the book like the rest, and it stays. I'll say it every night with the others, and I'll not be it."

He took his hand off the port.

Then the new one. Because a thing has a name or it is not a thing. Because Tacitus had stood in the smoke that morning and looked at its own hands and decided it would have one. Because a Halyard captain had asked on a ladder and paid for the answer with a boy's arm and a slab and a month. He said it once, in the old tongue, with the endings right, on a deck the Empire was not on, to a woman who knew what it meant.

"Gaius Sertorius."

The hull ring went round in the port. Pike did not turn.

Tacitus heard it. It took the name and turned it over with the whole of its attention. Then it did something it had never done with a name. It did not put it with the guard and the ship and the fast heart. It took it out again, and held it, and put it on him.

The way a stripe goes on an arm. Drawn.

"That's said," Yara said.

"That's said."

"Then you're crew till the College dock, Gaius Sertorius, Hal's cousin, off the Belt. You'll sleep in the cask with the lid off. You'll not once put your plate up on my ship. When you're what you've just said you are, you'll come back to this deck and tell me if a girl on Marl got a message. I'll put you off it if I don't like what you've become." She went to the helm and put her hand on Pike's shoulder, once. "Pike. Take us off this rock in the morning. I've a College dock to lie to, and I've been on Anvil three days too long."

"Captain."

"And Pike."

"Captain."

"He's stopped talking," Yara said. "Write that down. It's the first time."

Pike wrote nothing down. He had never once in thirty years written anything down. It was the one part of a Halyard the Empire had never been able to read. But he turned round at the helm, for the first time in three days. He looked at the man in the other seat with the hood down, a white line on his arm and a name on him that every Halyard child knew. He nodded, once, slowly, the way a man nods at a hull that has come together faster than the numbers said it should.
