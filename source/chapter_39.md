# Chapter 39: Sertorius

"Indeterminate," Yara said. "That's the word he used."

"That's the word."

"A month I gave you. Thirty-one days in my hold and three on that rock and a Flamen with a cracked glass, and you've come up my ramp at dusk with the hood down for the first time and the word's indeterminate." She had her feet on the chart glass and the port full of Anvil's hull ring, lit, going round. "I could have told you that on the ladder."

"You did," Jonah said. "You said, what are you, and I said I didn't know, and it turns out the College doesn't either. It's got a word for not knowing and the word's the whole of the reading." He sat in the other seat. Pike was at the helm and did not turn round. "So I've come to tell you what I am, since the glass won't, and to ask for the rest of the ride."

"To the College dock."

"To the College dock. As crew. Hal's cousin, who worked the hold. And from there I'll walk, because where I'm going next a Halyard ship can't take me, and you'd not want it to."

"The City."

"Yes."

Yara did not say anything for a while.

"You said four names," she said. "Three on this rock. You've had three days. Gaspar's still drinking; I've seen him. Corin's still walking the dock road; I've seen that too, with a lamp over two boys and the face of a man who's lost a bout. And Morrow's paying his cutters tomorrow with coin from a Flamen, Pike says, and he's not once said where the slab came from, and Pike says he's stopped looking tired." She looked at the port. "Three names, three days, and nobody's dead but two boys with bands who'd have cut Bram for a quota. That's not what I'd have counted from the man who took Kaspar's arm."

"No."

"So what are you."

He thought about it.

He had thought about it on the rock for five years with a stick finding the places he was wrong, and in a cask for thirty-one days, and in a shed with an arm on a glass, and he had the answer now, and it was not the one the old man had given him, and it was not the one the glass had, and he gave it to a Halyard captain on her own bridge because she had asked on the ladder and he had said she would have it first.

"Four names and an Empire," he said.

---

"A purser," he said, "who saw a thing through a hatch and was afraid of it, and wrote it down, and was seen to for it. That's what they said. Seen to. A house on the upper ring and the tax farm and the bank, for a letter. That's not a man. That's what the Empire does with a man who writes things down; it makes him a publican and lets him ruin a province a paper at a time without raising his voice, and he'd have done it anywhere, on any rock, with any letter, because the Empire's built so that a Marchand always rises."

Yara listened.

"A Collar who won a bout he couldn't have won against me, and signed, and took a band. He put a fang in his own people on Tessera for a stripe, and he got the stripe, and he reads a quaestor's paper out in a square so a woman will stop waiting, and he stands at the head of twelve men with a quota and says friend to the ones he'll not cut himself. That's not a man either. That's a Collar who was called to sharpen at fourteen and lived because the Stripe was bored, and has spent every day since making sure he's never at the bottom of a bout again, and the Empire's built so that the only way up for a Collar is over other Collars, and he took it."

"And the drunk."

"The drunk did nothing." Jonah looked at the hull ring going round. "He heard a letter read out and said, Tom's boy, he's a good boy, and went to sleep. He watched my father go past his window for a month with a bottle in his hand. He loved him. He says so. He sold me the story for a shard and told it right for the first time and said he was sorry and said it's what a drunk has." He was quiet a moment. "The old man said no innocents. He said a count that leaves out the ones who did nothing is a story you tell so you can sleep. And I said, yes, and I've said it every night since, and I've had three days on this rock and I've looked at it, and it's true, and it's not the whole."

"What's the whole."

"The whole is that the Empire's built so that doing nothing is a thing you do. It's built so a man in a tavern with a bottle is safe if he keeps his head down and dead if he lifts it, and Gaspar did the sum every morning for a month and came out at the bottle, and the sum was right. That's what a Stripe's law is worth. It's worth a drunk keeping his head down. It's worth a Collar taking a band. It's worth a purser writing a letter. It's a thing that makes the four out of whatever men it's given, and it'd have made them out of me, if I'd stayed." He looked at her. "So it's four names and an Empire. I'll not do the four and leave the thing that made them. And I'll not do the thing and leave the four, because they're what it's for."

Yara took her feet off the chart glass.

"You'll kill four men," she said.

"I said I'd decided what to do about them. I told the old man that on a rock and he said he'd not ask. I lied to him." Jonah did not look away. "I've not decided. I've known that since a drunk said, you've got his hands, and I said, it's the ships. I know what I'll do about the Empire; that's a thing you can count, it's a seat and a Form and a City and a wall, and the old man drew most of it before he died. I don't know what I'll do about a drunk. I'll not kill him. I know that much. I'll make him say it sober, once, in a room, with somebody there to hear, and that's his, and I don't know if it's enough and I don't think it is."

"And the Collar."

"The Collar's got her. And a boy. And a stripe he'll lose the day the Empire doesn't need auxiliaries, which is the day the war's done, and he knows it, and he's afraid of it every morning the way he's been afraid of everything since fourteen." He breathed. "I'll not touch the boy. I'll not touch her. What's left of him after that, I'll count when I'm nearer it."

"And the purser."

"The purser's got a bank," Jonah said, "and a bank's a thing that falls."

Pike, at the helm, said, to the port: "He talks."

"And the fourth," Yara said. "The one in the City."

He did not answer at once.

He had said the fourth name every night for seven years, in the old tongue, in the dark between the lamps, with what the man had done: read his heart and found it doing nothing, sent him down a hole for what he carried, burned it, wrote him dead in a clerk's hand. He had it the way he had the road. And he had said, at the rail three nights ago, to a thing in a doorway that did the hand with no hand: not yet, not for him, for me, and he had meant the fourth, and he had meant the other thing, which had no name, which was on a stool at the bottom of the Throat with a grey book on its knee and had asked a guard what he was called.

"Marcus Vibius Priscus," he said. "Quaestor. He's got a seat, in a year; he'll stand for praetor and get it. He's got a Form; Verdict, the Vibian, the one that doesn't move, he's looking at the place he'll put the blade before he draws. He's got a father, who's a Gracchan, which is why I went down a hole; the seal was to the father and the son burned it so the father wouldn't hang. And he's got a sister."

"So?" Yara said.

"So nothing."

She looked at him.

She looked the way she had looked at him on the ladder with a fang point down and her heart held slow on purpose, except that her heart was not held now, it was fast and it was hers, and she had let him hear it once and had not taken it back, and she was reading him the way she read a hold, and Jonah sat on a Halyard bridge with the hull ring of Anvil going round in the port and did nothing with his face, and his heart, which he had spent seven years learning to have do nothing, did something.

Tacitus felt it.

It felt it the way it had felt it on a stair with a boy on a hip and a ring warm on a chain, and it did not say its word. It had said its word for that one and been given a ship. It did the other thing, the thing it did with everything: it took what it had felt, and turned it over, and put it somewhere, and the place it put it was not new. It had a place already. It had had it for seven years, since a road and a rock and a name said every night in the old tongue in the dark and never once said in the light, and it put the fast heart there, under that name, the one it had heard him not say, and kept it.

"So nothing," Yara said.

"So nothing."

"That's a Halyard's answer. It's the kind we give the Vigilant." She put her feet back on the chart glass. "I'll not ask again. I've a wife and I know what a man looks like who's said so nothing about somebody, and it's your count, and you can carry it up the Throat with the rest." She looked at the port. "But I've a thing to say, since I said I'd say the obvious once on every ship, and I've not yet. You've four names and you've said three the way a man reads a manifest, and you've said the fourth the way a man reads a manifest, and then you said sister, and you stopped reading a manifest. That's all. It's said."

Jonah did not say anything.

Pike said, to the helm, very quietly, "He's stopped talking," and Yara said, "Leave it, Pike," and he left it.

---

She asked him the name at the port, when the hull ring had gone round twice more and the dock lamps had gone down to their night setting and the Heron's ramp was up.

"You said you'd tell me what you are. First. Before the Empire. That was the price." She stood with her hand on the cold glass. "You've told me four names and an Empire and so nothing. That's what you're going to do. It's not what you are. A thing has a name or it's not a thing; that's a Halyard's law too, we name a hull before we lay the keel. So."

"So."

"So who are you now?"

He told her.

He told her about a rock at the far edge of the settled worlds that the Roma ark's people had not wanted, and a legion they had not wanted either, Halyard and Ferrum and Marl, and a general on the losing side who had never once been beaten in the field, who had made a Senate there, on the rock, with its own stripes and its own law, and had held it eleven years, and had been killed at dinner by his friends because a man who cannot be beaten will not let a war end. He told her about a head in a box and a Princeps who wept and a house struck from the rolls, and a lorica with eleven years of the far edge in it and a gift that no glass could read, sleeping in a niche in the City for three hundred and ninety years because nobody could find it to wake it. He told her about a Form built for a lorica that could put its fang anywhere, in a book that was burned, that an old man had read once in a vault and taught to a Collar on a rock with a stick.

"Sertorius," Yara said.

She said it before he did.

"Yes."

"Every Halyard child knows that name." Her voice had not changed and it had. "It's the one thing we're taught before we're taught to build. There was a man once who gave Halyards a Senate. On a rock. With Ferrum and Marl. He put a stripe on a Halyard's arm that the Halyard had earned and not informed for, and he held it eleven years, and then the Empire came and took it back and struck him and we've built ships without a licence ever since because a licence is a thing you ask the Empire for, and we asked once." She took her hand off the port. "That's who you're going to walk into their Senate as."

"A bastard branch. From the far Limes. That kept the name four centuries and has come to the City to claim the seat, and the Senate'll let it, because a seat's a vote and there are men in that room who'll pay for one more and not ask where the far Limes is." Jonah kept his voice level. "That's the old man's count. He drew it on a slab. I'll wear a dead house's stripe and a gift called Quiet and a name nobody remembers, and I'll sit in their chamber, and I'll find the four, and I'll find the wall, and the Princeps, and I'll say to him a thing the old man asked me to say."

"They'll not know what the name means."

"No."

"You will." Yara looked at him. "And every Halyard in the Belt will, the day it's said aloud in that chamber, and you'll have a thing on your deck then that you didn't ask for, which is us. That's a price too. I'm telling you now, so it's said."

"So it's said."

"And the first name. The Gaius."

"The most common name in the rolls," Jonah said. "Nobody remembers a Gaius. He said that. He said, nobody remembers a Jonah either, that's the point of both."

Yara almost smiled. It was the Halyard's almost, and this time it reached something.

"Then say it," she said. "The way you said the price. So it's said, on a Halyard deck, before the Empire's heard it. That's what I asked for on the ladder and you've come to give it me a month early, and I'll take it."

---

He said the names first.

He said them at the port with his hand flat on the cold glass, in the old tongue, in order, the way he had said them every night for seven years, and Yara stood beside him and did not understand a word and listened to all of them, and Pike at the helm did not turn round. He said Tacitus, first, above the old man, because it had been in the box before the old man was in the room. He said Aulus Terentius Pharus, Censor, who read every page, who drew the Pit and could not draw the way out of it. He said his father. He said Ochoa, who said nothing, which was the highest thing. He said Ullan of Marl, the east fields, two l's. He said Decimus Alba, of Etruria, Sula's father, first, before the rest. He said the boys at the road's end and the mate with half an arm and the Centurion who said oh. He said Dace and the boy who said what. He said the six hundred and forty-two from the slabs, all of them, the way he had said them at the bottom of a well without a mouth, and the ones from the road, and the ones with a line that said there was a thing and it was theirs.

He said Mara Ansel, and the Halcyon, and stopped, and went on.

He said the fourth name with what he had done, and did not say the other, and the thing in the doorway put the heart under it anyway.

Then he said his own.

"Jonah Vale," he said. "Anvil. Morrow's yard. Purser's pilot. Citizen for a week. Four four seven one. That's in the book. That's a name in the book like the rest, and it stays, because a thing with a name stays, and I'll say it every night with the others, and I'll not be it."

He took his hand off the port.

And then, because a thing has a name or it is not a thing, and because a thing in the doorway of him had stood in the smoke that morning and looked at its own hands and decided it would have one in the book, and because a Halyard captain had asked on a ladder and had paid for the answer with a boy's arm and a slab and a month, he said the new one. Once. In the old tongue, with the endings right, the way the old man would have wanted it said, on a deck the Empire was not on, to a woman who knew what it meant.

"Gaius Sertorius."

The bridge did not do anything. The hull ring went round in the port. Pike, at the helm, did not turn.

But the thing in the doorway heard it.

He felt it hear. It heard it the way it had heard its own name in the only room in the Pit that knew what a name was for, and it did not turn all the way round, because it had turned all the way round once and stayed; it did the other thing. It took the name and turned it over with the whole of its attention and put it where it had put a guard and a ship and a fast heart, and then it did something it had never done with a name, which was to take it out again, and hold it, and look at it, and put it, not with the rest, but on him.

The way a stripe goes on an arm. Drawn.

"That's said," Yara said.

"That's said."

"Then you're crew till the College dock, Gaius Sertorius, Hal's cousin, off the Belt, and you'll sleep in the cask with the lid off and you'll not once put your plate up on my ship, and when you're the thing you've just said you are you'll come back to this deck and tell me if a girl on Marl got a message, and I'll put you off it if I don't like what you've become." She went to the helm and put her hand on Pike's shoulder, once, the way a captain does. "Pike. Take us off this rock in the morning. I've a College dock to lie to and I've been on Anvil three days too long."

"Captain."

"And Pike."

"Captain."

"He's stopped talking," Yara said. "Write that down. It's the first time."

Pike wrote nothing down. He had never once in thirty years written a thing down; it was the one thing about a Halyard that the Empire had never been able to read. But he turned round at the helm, for the first time in three days, and looked at the man in the other seat with the hood down and a white line on his arm and a name on him that every Halyard child knew, and nodded, once, slowly, the way a man nods at a hull that has come together faster than the numbers said it should.
