# Chapter 29: Harvest

He was matter for four hours, and it was not like anything.

He would try to tell it later and there would be nothing to tell. There was no dark, because dark is a thing a man sees. There was no cold. There was no count, no names, no heart he could find; there was a place a very long way down where a thing with a name held something the way the wall held six hundred and forty-three men, carefully, without letting it wear, and the something was him, and he knew it the way a man knows he is asleep, from the outside, without being able to do anything about it. He was a slab. He was seventy-one slabs in a ring in a cask in a row, and the row was the wall, and the wall breathed round him and did not reach, because he was its arm.

He felt the lift.

Not with his body. Through the ring, through the slabs, through the cask that was the Vein, the way he had felt the mark go into the seventh lid: four men's hands on the glass, and the cask coming up off the rock, and the tilt of it, and the walk. Forty paces. He counted them from the bottom of the well without knowing he could. The set-down. The scrape of the groove against the rock at the road's end. Then another cask beside it, and another, and the eight of them in a row where the Flamen stood with the rod, and the deep men's hands going away.

And then the rod.

He felt it come. It came at arm's length, the way it had for the seventh, a young Flamen's arm held straight out because the Vein wanted the cutting in it, and the rod touched the lid, and the mark went in.

It went into the lid. He felt that; he had drilled the shape thirty times a night and it was the shape, the knot of closed lines that said this is shut, and the lid took it, and the mark went down through the lid into the ring the way a mark goes into a thing that is all one piece, looking for the rest of itself, to close.

And it found him.

He would think about that for a long time, on a freighter, in a hold, at the bottom of a count. The mark was a shape that said this is shut and this is counted and this is matter, and it went down through seventy-one slabs of matter looking for the bottom of the cask to close on, and in the middle of the ring it found a thing that a thing with a name was holding as matter, carefully, well, and the thing was a man. It was a lie. It was a good lie; it was the best lie a thing with a name had ever told, and it had told it out of love, and the mark went into it and could not close, because a mark is a shape and a shape has to be true all the way through, and the plate will not hold on a lie. The old man had said it on the rock of the Cradle with a stick. The thing you are lying to yourself about is the thing the plate will not cover.

The mark came apart.

He felt it come apart in the lid, the lines letting go of each other, and felt the rod jerk back at arm's length, and felt, through the ring, a young Flamen's heart go from slow to very fast.

"It won't take," Pollio said.

---

He heard that. He heard it from the bottom of the well, through the glass, and he understood it, and he understood what came next, and he said, in the old tongue, from a very long way down, to the thing that was holding him: let go.

It did not, at once.

That was the thing he would keep. It had held him four hours the way the wall held, and it did not want to stop, because stopping meant the mark and the rod and the road, and it had learned to hold from its mother and it had learned to hold well, and it stood at the bottom of him with its hands closed on his heart and did the doorway thing, the deciding, for the length of one breath he did not have.

Then it let go.

His heart came up through the names of things. It came up too fast; it had come up slowly on the floor of the Cradle with the wall beside him and it came up now the way a man comes up out of water he has been under too long, all at once, everywhere, and the dark was dark again and the cold was cold and his hands were flat against the slabs on either side and he could feel them, and above him, through the lid, the rod touched again.

"It won't take. I've put it twice." Pollio's voice was high. "There's something in this cask that isn't slabs."

"A hundred and twelve, Flamen." Harrow's voice. Flat. The voice for a bite. "I packed it. Thirty-one. I packed it the seventeenth."

"Then something's shifted. Open it."

Nobody moved. Jonah felt them not move, four hearts at the road's end going fast, and two more behind them that were not deep men's, that were slow and young and plated, the two guards who came down with the Flamen every day and stood at the road's end with him because a lorica could not go nearer a cask than a Flamen could, and then Pollio said it again, louder, in the voice of a man who has been told he is in charge of a thing and has just found out what it is, and one of the guards said, "Flamen, we can't," and Pollio said, "Then they can," and Harrow said, "Sir," and stopped, because there was nothing after it.

Jonah lay in the ring with his hands on the slabs and did the count.

It came out in the time it took Harrow to say sir. It came out the way the old man's did, all at once, without looking, and it came out to this: the cask was open in a minute whatever anyone said, because a Flamen with a rod that had failed twice would have it open if he had to lift the lid himself with a cutting in his arm and the Vein reaching for it. And when it was open there was a Collar in it, and the Collar was on a slate in the deputy's hand at two hundred and forty, and the deputy had written it, and the foreman had packed it, and the one who carries had gone into the wall the night before and the cut one had sat with him, and every man on the Deep road was in it, one way or another, because that was what a count did when it failed. It went through everyone who had touched it. He had known that in the loading gallery with Harrow's lamp on the floor and he had taken the bowl anyway, and now the count had failed, and there was one line on it he could still write.

"Harrow," he said, through the lid.

The road's end went silent.

"Open it. Do what he says. You didn't know."

---

They opened it.

Harrow's hands did it; he felt them on the lid, the same hands that had lidded it, and the lid came up out of the groove and went back, and there was light. Not much. A lamp on the rock at the road's end and the Flamen's face over it, white, young, with the rod out in front of him the way a man holds a thing between himself and a dog, and behind him the two guards with their hands on their fangs, and Harrow beside the cask with his face doing nothing at all.

Jonah sat up.

It was hard. His heart had come back and his hands had come back and the rest of him had been a slab for four hours and did not want to be anything else, and he sat up in the ring of the Vein with seventy slabs standing round him and put his hands on the top course and looked at the Flamen over the rim of the cask, and the Flamen looked at a shaved, scarred Collar with a white line on his arm sitting in a cask of the harvest at the road's end of the Deep, and knew him.

"Four four seven one," Pollio said.

"Flamen."

"I read you." The rod did not shake, and Jonah thought, without wanting to, Harrow's lamp didn't either. "In the line. Six weeks ago. I read you and I frowned and I wrote it down, and she read you after me and said I was wrong, and the Master said I was wrong, and I went up the Throat with it written down and I have thought about it every day since." His voice was high and it was steady. "What are you."

"Dying," Jonah said. "Six years. Everybody says so."

He put his hands on the rim and stood up in the cask.

He did it slowly, so they would see what he was, which was a Collar in a cask, unarmed, with his hands shaking. He said, while he did it, in the road's tongue, loud enough for the four deep men and the two guards and the Flamen and the whole of the road's end:

"He didn't know. Harrow. He packed a hundred and twelve on the seventeenth like he packs every cask, and I came down on the sixteenth night with no lamp and moved forty-two of them, and they're in twenty-nine and thirty, six deep, on top, under the lid. Count them. I'll wait." He looked at Harrow. "You packed right. You've packed nine harvests. I'm the thing that was wrong with it."

Harrow did not look at him.

"Get him out," Pollio said. "Get him out of the cask and get the iron on him. Now. Before it."

"Before it what," Jonah said.

The Flamen did not answer. He did not have to. Jonah knew what the rod had felt in the lid, because he had felt it too: a mark going into a thing and finding a lie and coming apart, and a young Flamen who had read a void reading six weeks ago and had it now, in his arm, in the rod, what the void had been. There was something in the cask that was not slabs. It was standing up.

The guards came.

They came the way garrison came, together, one for each arm, and they were young and plated and grey and they came to the rim of the cask and no nearer, because the cask was the Vein and their cuttings knew it, and Jonah felt both their loricae through the rock and through the ring, reaching back from him, the way Varro's had reached back from the seed, and he thought, with the part of him that counted, they can't touch me. They can't put a hand in the cask. And they didn't. One of them took a thing off his belt, and it was not iron, it was the College's dark glass, a ring of it the width of a wrist with a hinge, and he held it out at the end of his arm the way the Flamen held the rod, and said:

"Hand."

Jonah looked at it.

He had worn one. Six years ago, on his ankle, a thing that had woken up in the night and decided it owned the leg, and the thing under his ribs had eaten it in the dark on the first night in the Pit, one small dead want at a time, hold, hold, hold, and he had lain on a shelf with the dead ring on his ankle and nobody had known. This one was not dead. It was awake in the guard's hand the way the shackle had been awake on his ankle, and it was the College's and not the garrison's, and it was the Vein, and it wanted, and it was already saying so. He heard it. That was new. Six years ago he had felt it; he had lain on the shelf and felt hold come up his leg over and over and been folded away. He heard it now, through the ring, through the cask, through the plate under his skin, the way he had heard the Vein say come on the road with his hand on the rock: a piece of the wall, cut and shaped a hundred years ago to do one thing, saying the thing, hold, to whatever it was put on, with the whole of a want that had nothing else in it.

He put out his hand.

The guard closed the ring on his wrist. It went on and the hinge went in and it shut, and it tightened, and it said hold, and it was not saying it to his wrist. He understood that as it tightened. A garrison shackle held an ankle. A College shackle was for a man they took up the Throat to the slab, and it did not hold his wrist, it held the cutting in his arm, whatever it was, and it locked it. He felt it go for the doorway. He felt it go through the skin and into the plate and find Tacitus, fed, awake, up out of the cellar with its hands just opened from his heart, and say to it, hold, the way the wall said come, and Tacitus, which had said no to the wall six years ago through him and had been polite for six years and had just told a lie out of love and been found, heard it.

And Jonah said no.

Not in his head. Aloud, in the old tongue, to the ring on his wrist, the way he had said sorry to the road and the names to the dark and Tacitus to a thing in a doorway. He said it the way the wall said come, to a piece of the wall, and what he said was: no. Not him. You'll not hold him. Let go.

The ring let go.

It did not die, the way the first one had. It did not tighten and burn and get eaten. It stopped. He heard it stop, heard the want go out of it the way a song goes out of a man on the fourth verse, not finished, told, and the dark glass sat on his wrist, open, the hinge undone, a thing that had been told no by the only voice it had ever had a word for, and the young guard who had closed it looked at his own hand.

"It," the guard said.

"Yes," Jonah said. "It."

The plate came up.

He did not bring it. He had stopped pretending, at the bottom of the Deep, that he brought it; it came up on him in the crack of the Flamen's lamp at the road's end of the Deep, everywhere, fitted, right, dark on dark, the way it had in the cut on the night of the riot, and the ring on his wrist went into him. He ate it. Not slowly. He was standing in a cask of the harvest at thirty-two with eleven slabs in him and a Flamen's rod pointed at his face, and a College shackle that had been told no was a piece of the wall that nobody was using, and it went in through his wrist in one bite and was not, and the hunger, which had been polite for six years, was not polite, and he let it not be, and stood up in the cask in full plate with the crooked fang out of the back of his right wrist and looked at the Flamen.

"Run," he said. "Go on. Up the road. Tell him."

Pollio ran.

---

The guards did not.

He would give them that, later, in the book, in the line where he wrote what a man did. They were Tiro; they were boys of eighteen from somewhere that was not Anvil, with grey plate and short fangs, and they had watched a Collar say no to a College shackle and eat it, and they did not run. They drew. They drew together, the way garrison drew, one high one low, and came at the rim of the cask, and stopped, because the cask was the Vein.

Jonah came out of it.

He came out over the rim the way a man comes over a rail on a deck, one hand, the whole weight, and landed on the rock at the road's end between them, on the road side, off the Vein, where their cuttings would let them come, and they came. High and low. The high one for the throat seam, the way the garrison taught, and the low one for the hip, and both together, and for the length of one breath it was the seventh chamber on the night of the riot and he was a foreman with a chisel and no plate against two boys with a lamp.

He was not.

He was thirty-two and settled and fed, and he had drilled the Claudian Wall for a year on the rock of the Cradle against a stick, and he did it now, for the first time in the light, the way it was meant: he did not step. He was the wall. The high fang came in at the throat seam and found plate there, because the plate had gone there, up, over the seam, the way the old man had said it could if a man did not lie to himself about where he was afraid, and the short grey fang skidded on it and went past his ear, and the low one hit his hip and stopped, a finger in, the way Auger's chisel had stopped in Sabinus.

He took the high one's wrist. The pull, not the turn. He pulled the arm straight and stepped through, and the boy went past him, and the crooked fang went into the seam at the boy's armpit on the way past, all the way, the way it had in the cut, and the boy's plate came off him in a shiver and he sat down on the rock and did not get up.

The low one had his fang back.

He was quick. He was quicker than the first, and he had seen his friend sit down, and he came in the way a man comes in who has decided, low, short, a feint at the belly and the real one for the throat, the way Auger had come at Sabinus, and Jonah saw it come and thought, not the Wall. Not for this one. And did the half step.

It was not the water. He knew that as he did it; it was late and it was ugly and it was into the place the fang was not, and the fang went past his hip close enough to skid, and he turned inside it and put the crooked fang under the boy's jaw where the plate on the chest met the plate on the face, a finger wide, without aiming, the way a hand goes to a bucket handle.

The boy's plate came off. He sat down against the cask he had not been able to touch, and looked at Jonah, and said something Jonah did not hear, and stopped.

Jonah stood between them on the rock at the road's end with the plate on him and the fang out and two cuttings in two dead arms three paces off, and the hunger looked at them and said, plainly, without words, those.

"No," he said, aloud. "Not yet. Not here."

It did not argue. It had eaten a shackle and it had been told no by the man who had told a shackle no, and it settled, and it did not stop leaning, and he stood in the leaning and looked up the road.

The deep men had not moved.

Four of them, at the casks, with their gloves off. Harrow in front. They had watched a cut one who had carried their seventh cask stand up out of the thirty-first in plate and eat a College shackle and put two Blanks down at the road's end in the time it took to say a number, and they stood where they were, and Harrow's face did nothing at all, and then the foreman said, in the road's tongue, very quietly:

"I've a girl on Marl."

"I know."

"Then you'd better be on the dock with a knife in you." Harrow looked at the two boys on the rock. "They'll come down. All of them. With the rifles. Tullus'll come himself; he's nothing left to lose and he knows it." He looked at the casks. "They can't come in here."

"No."

"A lorica can't come within an arm of a cask. That's why we pack. That's the only reason there's a Collar on this road; that's the only reason there's a road." The foreman's voice did not change. "Forty casks in a cut the size of the Cradle and the Vein in every wall of it. They can stand at the turn with rifles and they can shoot down the gallery, but they can't come in, and a man in among the casks is a man they can't see to shoot." He looked at Jonah. "You could hold it a month."

"I could hold it till the harvest's late."

"Yes."

"And a harvest that's late is forty casks a frightened man can't send up the Throat before the letter comes, and a Deep that's not packing, and a Shallows that's cutting for nothing." Jonah looked at the road. "And he'll count that. He counts casks. He'll count what it costs him to have me in here a month against what it costs him to have me dead on the road, and he'll come out where I'd come out, and then he'll go up to the Shallows, and he'll start at decury six."

Harrow did not say anything.

"Pell," Jonah said. "Then the rest of the six. Then you, at the post, one a day at the turn, in the light, where I can see. That's what it costs. That's what a man with nothing to lose spends." He put the plate away. It went; that was still the thing he would keep. "Get your men back in the slab galleries. All of them. Lie down with your gloves off and be asleep when they come. You packed a hundred and twelve. You opened it when the Flamen said. You never saw anything after the lid came off, because you were behind it."

"And you."

"I'll be at the turn," Jonah said. "In the light. Where they can see what I am."

---

They came down at the seventh bell.

He felt them come. He was sitting on the rock at the turn of the loading gallery with his back to cask one and his hands on his knees and the two boys laid out beside him with their eyes closed, because he had closed them, and he felt the road fill with boots through the rock the way he had felt it fill on the night of the riot: twenty. Thirty. Rifles. Lamps. Coming down the Deep road past the Cradle, past the door, past the slab galleries where eleven deep men lay with their gloves off and their hearts going, and stopping, at the turn, where the road became the gallery and the gallery became the Vein, at exactly the distance a man's cutting would let him stand from forty casks.

An arm's length. He had measured it with his eyes for six nights. It was nine paces from where he sat.

Tullus came down last.

He came with a lamp, the way he had come to the Chamber with a key, and he stood at the front of thirty men in the light with the prefect's stripe on his shoulder and a face that had stopped being anything, and looked at a Collar sitting on the rock at the turn with two dead Blanks beside him, and at the casks behind, and at cask thirty-one, at the road's end, with its lid off.

"Four four seven one," he said.

"Prefect."

"You're not dying."

"I've been told." Jonah did not get up. "I'd not put it past me. Six years is a long time to be wrong about a thing."

Tullus did not smile. He had never smiled; he was a decent man running an atrocity by the numbers and there was nothing in the numbers to smile at, and he stood with the lamp and looked at Jonah for a long time, the way he had looked at the sixteen slabs in the Chamber and at a Master of Readings telling him about a letter, and Jonah watched him count. He could see it. He had watched the old man do it for five years and Decimus for seven and Harrow with a lamp at a turn, and he watched a prefect with nothing left do it now, and it came out, and Tullus's face did not change.

"Pollio says you told a shackle no," the prefect said.

"Pollio's had a long day."

"Pollio says he read you six weeks ago and got nothing, and the Master called it void, and the woman from the College called it unstable, and wrote it in her own book. He says the mark won't close on thirty-one because there's a thing in it the mark can't shut. He says that thing is you." Tullus's voice was the voice for casks. "He's up the Throat now, on the cage, and he'll be on the surface in an hour, and he'll write to the College tonight, because that's what a Flamen does, and the letter'll go up on the freighter with the harvest. Beside mine. Beside the one about the lamp."

"Yes."

"So you'll understand that I've nothing to lose, and that I'm going to spend it."

"I'd counted that."

"I know you had. You've been counting since the Throat. I read a slate for twenty-three years that said one line about you and it was true, and I struck you off the tenth the day the old man died, and I should have struck you off the road." Tullus looked at the two boys. "What are you."

Jonah looked back at him.

He thought about it. He thought about the old man on the rock of the Cradle saying you are not a Stripe, you are the thing that eats Stripes, and about a woman on a stool saying void, and about Pell saying I've had the answer, and about a thing in the doorway of him that had just told a lie out of love and been found and had let go anyway when he asked. He had not had a word for it in six years and he did not have one now.

"A purser's pilot," he said. "Off Anvil. Citizen, for a week."

Tullus did not say anything for a moment.

"Come out," he said. "Off the Vein. To the road. You'll go up the Throat on the cage and you'll go on the slab, and the College'll read what you are, and it'll be in their letter and not in mine."

"No."

"You can't hold that gallery."

"I can hold it till the harvest's late. You know that. You counted it on the way down." Jonah kept his hands on his knees. "And I know what you do then. You go up to the Shallows and you start at decury six, at the post, one a day, at the turn, where I can see. Pell first. He's old and he's Marl and he sings, and you'll have him sing while they do it." He looked at the prefect. "And I'll come out. That's the count. We've both done it. I'm sitting here because I'd rather you said it than did it, and because I've not decided yet which of you I'm going to kill on the way to the road, and I want a minute."

Thirty men with rifles did not move.

Tullus looked at him with the face that had stopped being anything, and Jonah watched him, and thought: he'll not say it. He's a decent man. He'll do it and he'll not say it, because a decent man running an atrocity doesn't say the numbers aloud, he reads them off a slate, and that's the whole of the difference, and he knows it, and he's known it twenty-three years.

"Prefect," somebody said, up the road.

The line of guards moved. It moved the way a line moves when a thing comes down through it that it would rather not be near, and the lamps went to one side, and a man came down the Deep road at the pace a man walks when he is going somewhere, and stopped at the turn, nine paces from the casks, exactly, without looking, because he had a licence and his cutting knew the distance better than he did.

Sabinus.

He had the fang out. Not the way the guards had drawn, one high one low; he had it out the way a man carries a thing he has carried so long it is not a thing, at his side, point down, the plate on him grey and fitted and two bands past anything a chisel was made for, and there was a place at his left armpit, in the seam, where the plate was newer than the rest, and Jonah looked at it, and Sabinus watched him look.

"That's him," Sabinus said. To nobody. To Tullus. "That's the one. The cut one. The one with the white line, the day Vetch didn't come up the road." His voice had the bored thing in it and under the bored thing there was something else, and Jonah had kept it two years on the rock of the Cradle and knew it. "I looked at him in the line. Twice. I looked at him and I saw a dying Collar with a white line on his arm and there was nothing in him that wasn't beneath me." He looked at the two boys. "He did that in the time it takes to say a number, Pollio says. With a fang out of his wrist. On the Vein, where they couldn't follow."

"He's a Principal," Tullus said. "Pollio thinks."

"Pollio thinks." Sabinus did not take his eyes off Jonah. "Pollio read him at nothing. The woman read him at nothing. The Master read him at nothing. And he's sat there on the rock with his hands on his knees telling you he's not decided who to kill on the way to the road." He was quiet a moment. "I've been on this rock nine years, prefect. I've had a harvest every spring and a riot every third year and a man on a post once a month, and I've trimmed twice, on the surface, in a yard, legal, against men who came out to the Belt to trim a Centurion because it was cheap, and I've not had a fight since." He turned the fang over in his hand, once, and put the point down again. "Get your men back up the road. Past the Cradle. All of them."

"Centurion."

"He'll come out for me. He'll not come out for thirty rifles, because thirty rifles is a harvest and he's counted it. He'll come out for one man with a fang, because that's not a harvest, that's a thing he's been drilling in the dark for years on a rock with a stick, and I know that, prefect, because I've a place in my seam where an old Blank put a chisel, and I've thought about the old Blank every night since, and where he learned it, and who was standing twenty paces off in the light watching, and went into the dark when I hurried." He looked at Jonah. "Nobody taught you the Wall on Anvil. Nobody on Anvil's seen it. You did the Wall on that boy at the throat seam and then you did my step on the other one, badly, and you put the fang in the same seam the old Blank did." He said it flat. "Who taught you."

Jonah stood up.

He did it slowly, the way he had stood up in the cask, and the hunger came up with him, and the plate did not, because he held it, and he stood at the turn of the loading gallery with the casks at his back and thirty rifles going away up the road and a Centurion nine paces off with a fang, and said, in the old tongue, in the register the old man had beaten into him on a rock with a stick, for Stripes, for the Senate, for a thing that had a name:

"A man you never met, Gnaeus Sabinus. Who read every page. And found one about you."

The bored face did what it had done once, on the road, with a finger of chisel in it.

"Come out," Sabinus said.

Jonah came out.
