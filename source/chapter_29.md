# Chapter 29: Harvest

He was matter for four hours.

There was no dark, because dark is something a man sees. No cold. No count, no names, no heart he could find. A very long way down, Tacitus held something carefully, without letting it wear, as the wall held six hundred and forty-three men. The something was him. He was a slab. He was seventy-one slabs in a ring in a cask in a row. The row was the wall, and the wall breathed round him and did not reach.

He felt the lift.

Not with his body. Through the ring, through the cask: four men's hands on the glass, the tilt, the walk. Forty paces. The set-down. The scrape of the groove on the rock at the road's end. Another cask beside it, and another. Eight in a row where the Flamen stood with the rod. The deep men's hands going away.

Then the rod.

It came at arm's length, a young Flamen's arm held straight out because the Vein wanted the cutting in it. The rod touched the lid, and the mark went in. He had drilled the shape thirty times a night and it was the shape, the knot of closed lines that said this is shut. The lid took it. The mark went down through the lid into the ring, looking for the rest of itself, to close.

And it found him.

The mark said this is shut, this is counted, this is matter. It went down through seventy-one slabs of matter looking for the bottom of the cask. In the middle of the ring it found something that Tacitus was holding as matter, carefully, well, and it was a man. It was a lie. It was the best lie Tacitus had ever told, and it had told it out of love. The mark went into it and could not close. A shape has to be true from end to end. The plate will not hold on a lie. The old man had said it with a stick.

The mark came apart.

He felt the lines let go of each other in the lid. He felt the rod jerk back. He felt, through the ring, a young Flamen's heart go from slow to very fast.

"It won't take," Pollio said.

---

He heard that, from the bottom of the well, and understood what came next. He said, in the old tongue, to Tacitus: let go.

It did not, at once.

It had held him four hours. It had learned to hold from its mother, and learned well. Stopping meant the rod and the road. It stood at the bottom of him with its hands closed on his heart and hesitated, for the length of one breath he did not have.

Then it let go.

His heart came up too fast. On the floor of the Cradle it had come up slowly. Now it came up all at once, everywhere, and the dark was dark again and the cold was cold. His hands were flat against the slabs on either side and he could feel them. Above him, through the lid, the rod touched again.

"It won't take. I've put it twice." Pollio's voice was high. "There's something in this cask that isn't slabs."

"A hundred and twelve, Flamen." Harrow. Flat. The voice for a bite. "I packed it. Thirty-one. The seventeenth."

"Then something's shifted. Open it."

Nobody moved. Four hearts at the road's end going fast. Two more behind them, slow, young, plated: the guards who came down with the Flamen every day and stood where a lorica could stand.

"Open it," Pollio said again, louder.

"Flamen, we can't," one of the guards said.

"Then they can."

"Sir," Harrow said, and stopped, because there was nothing after it.

Jonah lay in the ring and did the count. It came out in the time it took Harrow to say sir. The cask was open in a minute whatever anyone said. A Flamen with a rod that had failed twice would lift the lid himself if he had to. When it was open there was a Collar in it. The Collar was on a slate in the deputy's hand at two hundred and forty. The deputy had written it. The foreman had packed it. The one who carries had gone into the wall the night before and the cut one had sat with him. Every man on the Deep road was in it. That was what a count did when it failed. It went through everyone who had touched it. There was one line on it he could still write.

"Harrow," he said, through the lid.

The road's end went silent.

"Open it. Do what he says. You didn't know."

---

Harrow's hands did it. The lid came up out of the groove and went back, and there was light. A lamp on the rock and the Flamen's face over it, white and young, with the rod held out in front of him. Behind him the two guards with their hands on their fangs. Harrow beside the cask with his face doing nothing at all.

Jonah sat up.

It was hard. His heart had come back and his hands had come back. The rest of him had been a slab for four hours and did not want to be anything else. He put his hands on the top course and looked at the Flamen over the rim. The Flamen looked at a shaved, scarred Collar with a white line on his arm, sitting in a cask of the harvest, and knew him.

"Four four seven one," Pollio said.

"Flamen."

"I read you. In the line, six weeks ago. I read you and I frowned and I wrote it down. She read you after me and said I was wrong, and the Master said I was wrong. I went up the Throat with it written down and I've thought about it every day since." His voice was high and steady. "What are you."

"Dying," Jonah said. "Six years. Everybody says so."

He put his hands on the rim and stood up in the cask. He did it slowly, so they would see what he was: a Collar in a cask, unarmed, with his hands shaking. While he did it he said, in the road's tongue, loud enough for the four deep men and the two guards and the whole of the road's end:

"He didn't know. Harrow. He packed a hundred and twelve on the seventeenth, the same as every cask. I came down on the sixteenth night with no lamp and moved forty-two of them. They're in twenty-nine and thirty, six deep, on top, under the lid. Count them. I'll wait." He looked at Harrow. "You packed right. I'm what was wrong with it."

Harrow did not look at him.

"Get him out," Pollio said. "Out of the cask and the iron on him. Now. Before it."

"Before it what," Jonah said.

The Flamen did not answer. He did not have to. He had felt the rod find a lie in the lid and come apart. A young Flamen who had read a void reading six weeks ago had it now, in his arm: what the void had been. There was something in the cask that was not slabs. It was standing up.

The guards came together, one for each arm. Young, plated, grey. They came to the rim of the cask and no nearer, because the cask was the Vein and their cuttings knew it. Jonah felt both loricae through the rock, pulling back from him. They can't put a hand in the cask, he thought. And they did not. One of them took a ring off his belt. Not iron. The College's dark glass, the width of a wrist, with a hinge. He held it out at the end of his arm and said:

"Hand."

Jonah looked at it.

He had worn one, six years ago, on his ankle. It had woken in the night and decided it owned the leg, and Tacitus had eaten it in the dark, one small dead want at a time. This one was not dead. It was the Vein, cut and shaped a hundred years ago to do one thing, and it was already saying so. Six years ago he had felt it. He heard it now, through the ring, through the plate under his skin: hold. Hold. With the whole of a want that had nothing else in it.

He put out his hand.

The guard closed the ring on his wrist. The hinge went in and it shut. It tightened, and it was not talking to his wrist. A garrison shackle held an ankle. A College shackle was for a man they took up the Throat to the slab. It held the cutting in his arm, whatever it was, and locked it. He felt it go through the skin into the plate and find Tacitus, fed, awake, with its hands just opened from his heart. It said to it: hold. Tacitus had said no to the wall six years ago through him. It had just told a lie out of love and been found. It heard the ring.

And Jonah said no.

Aloud, in the old tongue, to the ring on his wrist. He said it as the wall said come, to a piece of the wall. No. Not him. You'll not hold him. Let go.

The ring let go.

It did not die and burn. It stopped. He heard the want go out of it, told. The dark glass sat open on his wrist, the hinge undone. The young guard who had closed it looked at his own hand.

"It," the guard said.

"Yes," Jonah said. "It."

The plate came up.

He did not bring it. He had stopped pretending he brought it. It came up everywhere, fitted, dark on dark, in the crack of the Flamen's lamp. The ring on his wrist went into him. He ate it. Not slowly. He was standing in a cask at thirty-two with eleven slabs in him and a rod pointed at his face. A College shackle that had been told no was a piece of the wall nobody was using. It went in through his wrist in one bite. The hunger had been polite six years. It was not polite now, and he let it not be. He stood in full plate with the crooked fang out of the back of his right wrist and looked at the Flamen.

"Run," he said. "Up the road. Tell him."

Pollio ran.

---

The guards did not.

They were Tiro, boys of eighteen with grey plate and short fangs. They had watched a Collar say no to a College shackle and eat it, and they drew. Together, one high, one low. They came at the rim and stopped, because the cask was the Vein.

Jonah came out of it.

Over the rim, one hand, the whole weight. He landed on the rock between them, on the road side, off the Vein, where their cuttings would let them come. They came. High for the throat seam, low for the hip, both together.

He did not step. He was the wall.

The high fang came in at the throat seam and found plate there, because the plate had gone there, up, over the seam. The short grey fang skidded on it and went past his ear. The low one hit his hip and stopped, a finger in.

He took the high one's wrist. The pull, not the turn. He pulled the arm straight and stepped through, and the boy went past him. The crooked fang went into the seam at the boy's armpit as he passed, all of it. The boy's plate came off him in a shiver. He sat down on the rock and did not get up.

The low one had his fang back.

He was quicker than the first, and he had seen his friend sit down. He came in low and short, a feint at the belly and the real one for the throat. Not the Wall, Jonah thought. Not for this one. And did the half step.

It was late and it was ugly. The fang went past his hip close enough to skid. He turned inside it and put the crooked fang under the boy's jaw, where the chest plate met the face plate, a finger wide, without aiming.

The boy's plate came off. He sat down against the cask he had not been able to touch. He said something Jonah did not hear, and stopped.

Two cuttings in two dead arms lay three paces off. The hunger looked at them and said: those.

"No," Jonah said, aloud. "Not yet. Not here."

It did not argue. It settled. It did not stop leaning.

The deep men had not moved. Four of them at the casks with their gloves off, Harrow in front. They had watched a cut one stand up out of thirty-one in plate, eat a College shackle, and put two Blanks down in the time it took to say a number. Harrow's face did nothing. Then he said, very quietly:

"I've a girl on Marl."

"I know."

"Then you'd better be on the dock with a knife in you." Harrow looked at the two boys. "They'll come down. All of them, with the rifles. Tullus'll come himself. He's nothing left to lose and he knows it." He looked at the casks. "They can't come in here."

"No."

"A lorica can't come within an arm of a cask. That's why we pack. That's the only reason there's a Collar on this road. The only reason there's a road." His voice did not change. "Forty casks in a cut the size of the Cradle, with the Vein in every wall of it. They can stand at the turn and shoot down the gallery. But they can't come in, and a man in among the casks is a man they can't see to shoot." He looked at Jonah. "You could hold it a month."

"I could hold it till the harvest's late."

"Yes."

"A late harvest is forty casks a frightened man can't send up the Throat before the letter comes. A Deep that's not packing. A Shallows cutting for nothing." Jonah looked up the road. "He'll count that. He counts casks. Me in here a month against me dead on the road. He'll come out where I'd come out. Then he goes up to the Shallows and starts at decury six."

Harrow said nothing.

"Pell. Then the rest of the six. Then you. At the post, one a day, at the turn, in the light, where I can see. That's what a man with nothing to lose spends." He put the plate away. It went. "Get your men back in the slab galleries. Lie down with your gloves off and be asleep when they come. You packed a hundred and twelve. You opened it when the Flamen said. You never saw anything after the lid came off, because you were behind it."

"And you."

"I'll be at the turn," Jonah said. "In the light. Where they can see what I am."

---

They came down at the seventh bell.

He was sitting on the rock at the turn with his back to cask one and his hands on his knees. The two boys lay beside him with their eyes closed, because he had closed them. He felt the road fill with boots through the rock. Twenty. Thirty. Rifles. Lamps. They came past the Cradle, past the door, past the slab galleries where eleven men lay with their gloves off and their hearts going. They stopped at the turn, at exactly the distance a cutting would let a man stand from forty casks.

An arm's length. Nine paces from where he sat.

Tullus came down last, with a lamp, as he had come to the Chamber with a key. He stood at the front of thirty men with the prefect's stripe on his shoulder and a face that had stopped being anything. He looked at a Collar on the rock with two dead Blanks beside him. He looked at cask thirty-one with its lid off.

"Four four seven one."

"Prefect."

"You're not dying."

"I've been told." Jonah did not get up. "I'd not put it past me. Six years is a long time to be wrong about a thing."

Tullus did not smile. He never had. He was a decent man running an atrocity by the numbers, and there was nothing in the numbers to smile at. Jonah watched him count. It came out, and Tullus's face did not change.

"Pollio says you told a shackle no."

"Pollio's had a long day."

"Pollio says he read you six weeks ago and got nothing. The Master called it void. The woman from the College called it unstable and wrote it in her own book. He says the mark won't close on thirty-one because there's something in it the mark can't shut, and that something is you." The voice for casks. "He's on the cage now. He'll be on the surface in an hour. He'll write to the College tonight, because that's what a Flamen does. The letter goes up on the freighter with the harvest. Beside mine. Beside the one about the lamp."

"Yes."

"So you'll understand that I've nothing to lose, and that I'm going to spend it."

"I'd counted that."

"I know you had. You've been counting since the Throat. I read a slate for twenty-three years that said one line about you, and it was true. I struck you off the tenth the day the old man died. I should have struck you off the road." Tullus looked at the two boys. "What are you."

Jonah thought about it. The old man on the rock saying, you are the thing that eats Stripes. A woman on a stool saying void. Pell saying, I've had the answer. He had not had a word for it in six years and did not have one now.

"A purser's pilot," he said. "Off Anvil. Citizen, for a week."

Tullus said nothing for a moment.

"Come out. Off the Vein, to the road. You'll go up the Throat on the cage and you'll go on the slab. The College'll read what you are, and it'll be in their letter and not in mine."

"No."

"You can't hold that gallery."

"I can hold it till the harvest's late. You counted it coming down." Jonah kept his hands on his knees. "And I know what you do then. You go up to the Shallows and start at decury six, at the post, one a day, at the turn, where I can see. Pell first. He's old and he's Marl and he sings. You'll have him sing while they do it." He looked at the prefect. "And I'll come out. That's the count. We've both done it. I'm sitting here because I'd rather you said it than did it. And because I've not decided which of you I'm going to kill on the way to the road, and I want a minute."

Thirty men with rifles did not move.

He'll not say it, Jonah thought. He's a decent man. He'll do it and not say it. A decent man running an atrocity doesn't say the numbers aloud. He reads them off a slate. That's the whole of the difference, and he's known it twenty-three years.

"Prefect," somebody said, up the road.

The line moved. It moved as a line moves when something comes down through it that it would rather not be near. The lamps went to one side. A man came down the Deep road at a walk. He stopped at the turn, nine paces from the casks, exactly, without looking, because he had a licence and his cutting knew the distance better than he did.

Sabinus.

He had the fang out. Not drawn, as the guards had drawn. Carried, at his side, point down, an object he had carried so long it was part of the arm. The plate on him was grey and fitted and two bands past anything a chisel was made for. At his left armpit, in the seam, the plate was newer than the rest. Jonah looked at it, and Sabinus watched him look.

"That's him," Sabinus said. To nobody. To Tullus. "The cut one. The white line. The day Vetch didn't come up the road." The bored voice, and under it something else. Jonah had kept that something two years on the rock of the Cradle and knew it. "I looked at him in the line. Twice. I saw a dying Collar with a white line on his arm, and nothing in him that wasn't beneath me." He looked at the two boys. "He did that in the time it takes to say a number, Pollio says. With a fang out of his wrist. On the Vein, where they couldn't follow."

"He's a Principal," Tullus said. "Pollio thinks."

"Pollio thinks." Sabinus did not take his eyes off Jonah. "Pollio read him at nothing. The woman read him at nothing. The Master read him at nothing. And he's sat there on the rock telling you he's not decided who to kill on the way to the road." He was quiet a moment. "Nine years on this rock, prefect. A harvest every spring, a riot every third year, a man on a post once a month. I've trimmed twice, in a yard, legal, against men who came out to the Belt to trim a Centurion because it was cheap. I've not had a fight since." He turned the fang over in his hand once and put the point down again. "Get your men back up the road. Past the Cradle. All of them."

"Centurion."

"He'll come out for me. He'll not come out for thirty rifles. Thirty rifles is a harvest, and he's counted it. He'll come out for one man with a fang, because that's not a harvest. That's what he's been drilling in the dark for years on a rock with a stick. I know that, prefect, because I've a place in my seam where an old Blank put a chisel. I've thought about that Blank every night since, and where he learned it. And who was standing twenty paces off in the light, watching, and went into the dark when I hurried." He looked at Jonah. "Nobody taught you the Wall on Anvil. Nobody on Anvil's seen it. You did the Wall on that boy at the throat seam. Then you did my step on the other one, badly, and put the fang in the same seam the old Blank did." Flat. "Who taught you."

Jonah stood up.

Slowly, as he had stood up in the cask. The hunger came up with him, and the plate did not, because he held it. He stood at the turn with the casks at his back and thirty rifles going away up the road and a Centurion nine paces off. He said it in the old tongue, in the register the old man had beaten into him on a rock with a stick. The register for Stripes, and for the Senate, and for something with a name.

"A man you never met, Gnaeus Sabinus. Who read every page. And found one about you."

The bored face did what it had done once, on the road, with a finger of chisel in it.

"Come out," Sabinus said.

Jonah came out.
