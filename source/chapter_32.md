# Chapter 32: Hold

The bar went into the groove and the lid came up, and there was light, and he could see it.

That was the first thing. Not the lamp, not the face over it, not the hold; the seeing. He had gone into the cask blind with a white fever behind his eyes and he lay in the ring now with the lid coming off and looked up at a lamp and it was a lamp, yellow, hanging from a hook on a beam, swinging a little with the hull, and it was the first thing he had seen in thirty-one days and he looked at it the way he had looked at the Kestrel's yoke the first time, as a thing that answered.

Then the face came over the rim, and stopped.

It was an old face. Belt-brown, with a Halyard's flat nose and a beard cut short with a knife, and a bar in one hand and a lamp in the other, and it looked down into a cask of the College's sealed harvest and saw seventy slabs of the Vein standing in a ring and a man lying in the middle of them with a white line on his arm, and it did what a face does that has opened a great many casks and never once found that.

"Hal," the old man said. Not loud. "Hal. Get Kaspar."

Jonah said, in the old tongue, from the bottom of the well: let go.

It let go at once this time.

His heart came up. It came up the way it had on the floor of the Cradle, slowly, through the names of things, and it was a good heart, it was forty-one and settled and the coat fitted, and the rest of him was not. He found that out when he tried to move. He put his hands on the slabs to sit up and the hands did not do it. They shook. Not the six years' way and not the fever's way; they shook the way a thing shakes that has not been used for a month and has forgotten how, and his legs were not there at all, and his mouth was dry in a way that went all the way down, and he understood, lying in the ring with an old Halyard's lamp on his face, that a thing with a name had held him thirty-one days without letting him wear, and had held him well, and that a man who has not eaten for thirty-one days has still not eaten.

The hunger came up with the heart.

Not his. Tacitus's. It had held him as matter for a month and eaten nothing, because a thing holding does not eat, and it came up out of the well with him now the way it had come up in the Mouth, all at once, everywhere, and there were nine people on the ship and two of them were plated and it knew where both of them were before he did.

"Don't," he said, aloud, to it, in the old tongue. His voice came out of him in pieces. "Not yet."

The old man with the bar looked at him.

"What was that?"

"Praying," Jonah said, in the road's tongue. It hurt. "Give me a minute. I'm not going anywhere. I can't feel my legs."

---

He got the minute and not more.

Kaspar came down the ladder into the hold with a lamp and a fang out and the plate up on him to the elbows, grey, a Blank's plate, thin, and Jonah felt him coming before the boots hit the deck, the way he felt everything now; a Tiro six, a cutting in the right arm, Plate and Blade and one more, and a heart going fast, and behind him on the ladder two more, unplated, with knives, and the old man with the bar had stepped back from the cask and set the lamp on a cask beside it and was holding the bar in both hands now.

"What is it," Kaspar said. Belt accent, Halyard under it. Young. Thirty, and the mate, and afraid, and covering it with the plate.

"A man." The old one did not take his eyes off the cask. "In thirty-one. In a ring. Somebody packed him. He's got a collar line and he says he can't feel his legs."

"A convict."

"He was in a sealed cask, Kaspar. With a Flamen's mark on it." The old man's voice was flat. "I've opened casks for the skim thirty years and I've never once opened one with a mark on it that wasn't a mark, and this one's a mark, I looked, it's a knot, it's the College's, and it's the wrong way round. I looked at it a long time before I got the bar. It's backward. Like a thing seen in water." He breathed. "Somebody sealed that cask from inside."

Kaspar looked at the cask.

Jonah watched him look. He was sitting up now; he had got his hands to work by putting the plate into them, a little, the way he had put it into a slate, and the plate had come up on his palms and he had pushed with it, and he sat in the ring with seventy slabs standing round him and his back against the far course and looked at four men in a freighter's hold over the rim of a cask, and counted.

It came out badly. He knew it before it finished. Four men who skimmed a College harvest, on a contractor's freighter, thirty-one days out, with a stowaway in a sealed cask, and the seal broken, by them, with a bar. A College that never asked what happened to two casks in forty would ask, very carefully, what had happened to a cask that came in with its mark broken and a Collar's story in it. A crew that had found the cask had one thing they could do about it that did not end with all of them on a slab, and it was the thing a crew did with a stowaway in the Belt, which was the lock, and the dark, and forty casks on the dock with a story about a seal that had cracked in the cold.

"He goes out the lock," Kaspar said.

"He can't stand."

"Then he goes out the lock lying down." The mate came forward with the fang. "Pike, you found him, you don't have to watch. Hal. Dov. Get him up."

The two with knives came round the cask.

Jonah said, to the thing in the doorway, in the old tongue, very quietly: not the plated one. Not first.

And they took his arms and lifted, and his legs did not hold him, and they had him half out of the ring with his knees on the rim and his hands on the slabs when the plate came up.

---

It came up wrong.

He had known it would. The old man had said it with a stick: after a big jump you learn your own plate again, it is not a cutting, it does not settle like one, it settles faster and different every time. He had learned it again at twenty-four and at twenty-eight and at thirty-one on the floor of the Cradle with a month to do it in. He had thirty-one days of being matter and no floor and no month, and the plate came up on him in a Halyard freighter's hold with two men's hands on his arms and it was forty-one, and it was the wrong shape.

Not everywhere. It had always been everywhere. It came up now the way a thing comes up that has learned a new trick and does not know yet where the trick goes: it came up thick on the forearms where the fang had eaten strips on the road, thicker than it had ever been, and thin on the back, and over the seams it did a thing he had never felt it do, which was close. Not go up over them, the Wall way, because he was not lying about where he was afraid. Shut. The seam at the throat and the seam at the armpit and the seam at the hip shut the way a lid shuts in a groove, and he felt them shut and did not know what it was, and it was heavy, and the man on his left arm said, "What," the way the boy had said it in the cut, and let go.

Jonah went over the rim.

He went over it the way he had at the road's end, one hand, the whole weight, and the weight was wrong and the hand did not hold, and he went down onto the deck of the hold on his knees between two casks with a man with a knife on either side of him and a mate with a fang coming, and his legs were not there, and he put the fang out.

It came out of the back of his right wrist. It always had. And it went past the place he had put it.

He had put it a hand long, the way it came, the crooked one; and it came out a hand long and then it was an arm long, in the same motion, without his asking, and it went past the man on his right, who had been a hand out of reach, and through the meat of his thigh, high, and out, and the man sat down on the deck and looked at the leg, and Jonah looked at the fang.

Reach. He had the word from a page. The fang changes length mid-cut. It was a thing a licensed Centurion's cutting had, and did every day for twenty years without thinking, the way a hand goes to a bucket handle, and Jonah had eaten the cutting on the Deep road, all of it, a fifth of it into him in the time a man takes to sit down, and every gift a lorica holds has a chance to pass, the old man had said, and this one had passed, and it had come out wrong. He had not asked for an arm. He had asked for a hand. It had given him what Sabinus would have wanted.

That was the echo.

He felt it then, on his knees on the deck, and it was the first since Varro, and it was worse. Varro had been feet. A duellist's feet and a duellist's contempt, in his hands, and he had won three fights on them and hated it and the old man had spent a year getting them out. This was not feet. This was a way of looking at a hold full of Blanks with knives, bored, from a long way up, as slabs that would come or would not, and it was in his eyes, behind them, where the fever had been, and it looked at the mate coming with a thin grey fang and a Tiro six's plate and it thought, without words, in a voice that was not his: that one's nothing.

"No," Jonah said, aloud. "Not yours. Mine."

Kaspar came in.

He came in the garrison way, because there was only one way and every school in the Empire taught it; high, for the throat seam, and the throat seam was shut. Jonah felt the fang hit it and skid. Not into plate; off it, the way a knife skids off a lid, and the mate's face did the thing the boy's had done at the road's end, and Jonah was still on his knees, and did the only thing his legs would do, which was not stand.

He stepped.

Not up. Sideways, on his knees, half a pace, into the place the fang was not, and it was the water step, and it was right. It was the first time in his life. He had done it three hundred times on the rock and three times in a cut and twice on a road and it had been half every time, ugly, late, his, and on his knees on the deck of a Halyard freighter with a Tiro's fang going past his ear it was whole, and smooth, and early, and it was not his. It was Sabinus's. It was in his knees the way the wrist turn had been in his hands off a dead decurion, and he did it perfectly, and he wanted, on his knees in a hold with a fang out, to be sick.

He took the mate's arm instead.

Both hands. The wrist and the elbow, the way Varro had known and Livia had named, and the arm went the way arms do not go, and the fang went out of the fingers and clattered on the deck and was not a fang anymore, it was plate, gone back into the cutting, and Jonah had the arm, the right arm, the plated one, in both hands, and the hunger, which had been holding since the lid came off, stopped holding.

It went in.

It went in through his palms into the grey thin Blank's plate on a mate's forearm and found the cutting under it and did what it did. Not one bite. It had been thirty-one days without eating and a month before that on eleven slabs and a shackle and a Centurion, and it had held a man as matter the whole of that time with its hands closed on his heart and had been polite about it, and it was not polite now. It ate. He felt it eat the way he had felt it eat the shackle, want by want, hold, hold, and this was not a shackle. This was a lorica on a living man, a Tiro six, a cutting that had been in a Halyard boy's arm since he was fourteen and had grown with him and had gone up six grades in sixteen years on a freighter that never once had a fight, and it had wants, and Jonah heard them.

That was the thing that stopped him.

Not the mate screaming. Kaspar was screaming; he was on his knees on the deck with a Collar's hands on his arm and his plate going in strips into the Collar's palms and he was screaming the way a man screams whose arm is being taken off by something he cannot see, and Jonah heard that and it was a sound and he had heard a great many sounds. He heard the other thing under it. He heard it the way he had heard the College shackle say hold, through the plate, through the palm, in the tongue the wall spoke: a small thing, a cutting, six grades, that had never once been asked for anything but plate and a fang and had given them, saying, to the thing eating it, in the only word it had, the way a man says it who has nothing else to say:

Please.

He let go.

He let go the way Tacitus had let go of his heart when he said let go at the bottom of the well: not at once, and then at once. The hunger did not want to. It had half a lorica in it and the other half three fingers away and it was thirty-one days empty and it did the doorway thing, the deciding, and Jonah said, in the old tongue, aloud, on his knees, with a Halyard mate screaming in his hands:

"No. That one said please. Let go."

It let go.

Kaspar fell over. He lay on the deck of the hold with his right arm bare to the elbow, bare, not plated and not skin, the plate gone off it in a patch the shape of two hands and the skin under it grey and shining where a cutting had been half eaten and had not died, and he stopped screaming, because he had run out, and lay there breathing with his eyes open looking at the beam.

Jonah knelt beside him with his hands empty.

The hold was quiet. Pike stood by the cask with the bar in both hands and had not moved. Dov sat against a cask with his hands round his thigh and blood coming between the fingers and his face white. Hal was on the ladder, halfway, with a knife, looking down, and had been there since the plate came up and had not come down and had not gone up.

And the thing in the doorway settled.

Jonah felt it settle. Half a lorica, a Tiro six's, three grades of it, and it went in and did not fight and settled the way the shackle had, and he felt the coat pull in one more size, from forty-one, and it was forty-two, and it was not a thing he wanted. He had not once wanted it. He knelt on the deck of a Halyard freighter's hold with a boy's half-eaten arm beside him and felt himself get a grade for it and thought, this is what the old man meant. This is the Law. The strong eat the weak and get stronger and there's no line on the slate for what the weak said while it happened. I've a line for it. I'll write it.

And then he felt the other thing, and it was new.

The half of the cutting that had gone into him had had a gift. He had felt it pass the way he had felt Reach pass on the road, without asking, and it was Seal, the plate gift, the one that shut seams, and it was the thing his plate had done when it came up wrong. The seams shutting. He had felt them shut and not known what it was and it was this, it was a Halyard boy's cutting's one trick, the thing a lorica learns on a ship where the cold gets into the seams, and it had come across in a half and it was wrong. He felt it be wrong. It shut the seams and it shut them the way the harvest mark had closed from inside, backward, lumpy, a knot the wrong way round, and the plate over his throat was sealed and it was heavy and it would not open when he asked it to, and then it did, badly, and he knelt with his neck plated and unplated and plated again and thought, wrong-shaped. Both of them. The Reach and the Seal. I ate one whole and one half and I've got both of them wrong.

"Pike," he said.

The old man did not answer.

"Pike. Your mate's alive. His arm's not going to be what it was. Dov's leg'll heal; I went through the meat, I'm a foreman, I know where the meat is." He put his hand flat on the deck. He could feel the ship through it, the whole of it, the way he had felt the road, and there were nine hearts on it, and eight of them were going fast, and one, on the deck above, forward, had been going fast since the screaming started and had stopped, and was going slow now, on purpose, the way a heart goes slow when a man is making it. "That's your captain coming down. She's plated. She's Tiro nine, and she's got Hush and she's running it inward, which is a thing I've done six years, so I know what it costs her and I know she's not coming down to fight me." He looked at the ladder. "Tell her I've stopped. Tell her I stopped when he said please."

---

Yara came down the ladder the way a captain comes into her own hold, without hurrying, and she had a fang out, and it was not for him.

He saw that. He saw her see the hold from the bottom of the ladder, the mate on the deck with his arm bare, and Dov's leg, and Pike with the bar, and the Collar on his knees between the casks with his hands flat on the deck and his neck plated wrong, and the fang was in her hand the way Sabinus had carried his, point down, a thing she had carried so long it was not a thing, and she did not point it at him. She held it, and stood at the bottom of the ladder, and looked, and her heart was slow, and it was costing her.

She was forty. Halyard, flat-nosed, with a Blank's thin stripe on her wrist and the stripe was new, ten years, not more, and she had grey in her hair and a face that had never once in its life been surprised by anything it found in a hold, and it was not surprised now. It was counting. He watched her count and it was his count, the same one, all at once, without looking, and she came out where he had come out, and her face did nothing.

"You're the thing in thirty-one," she said.

"Yes."

"Pike says the mark's backward."

"I made it. From inside. With my hand." He did not move. "There's no Flamen on this ship and there's no Flamen on the dock you're going to, because you're not going to a dock the College watches, you're going to a yard in the Belt where the two casks you skim go, and you'll put thirty-eight on a College dock a week later with a story, and nobody was ever going to look at thirty-one's mark. Pike looked. He looks at everything. That's why he's alive at seventy on a skimmer."

Yara looked at Pike.

"He talks," Pike said.

"He's a Collar with a white line who came out of a sealed cask and took Kaspar's arm half off with his hands." She said it to the old man without any weight. "And he talks. And he's on his knees with his hands flat on my deck. Which of those do I count?"

"All of it," Jonah said. "That's the only way it comes out."

She looked at him then. Properly. The way the woman on the stool had, six weeks and thirty-one days ago, for longer than a number took, and he let her, and did not do anything with his face, because there was nothing on it that was not true. He was a man who could not stand. He was forty-two and settled and wrong-shaped and had a boy's cutting saying please in him and a Centurion's contempt behind his eyes that was not his, and he was hungry, and it was showing, and he was not going to eat anyone else on this ship, and he did not know how to say that in a way a Halyard captain with a fang would believe, so he did not say it. He put it on his face and let her read it.

"What are you," Yara said.

He thought about a purser's pilot off Anvil.

"I don't know," he said. "I've been asked three times in a month by people with the right to ask, and I've said the thing I was, and it's not true anymore, and I've not got the new one yet." He breathed. "I'm a man who's been in a box thirty-one days and hasn't eaten, and I've a letter on my chest from a girl to her father, and her father's dead, and I did it, and I'm going to find her. That's what I am tonight. Ask me again in a month."

Yara did not say anything.

She looked at the mate on the deck. Kaspar had turned his head and was looking at her, and his face was the face of a man who has had a thing taken off him that he had had since he was fourteen, and she looked at the arm, and at the grey shining patch where a cutting had been half eaten and had lived, and Jonah watched her put that with the rest, and it weighed, and she let it.

"He said please," she said. "Pike says you said that. That you stopped when he said please."

"Yes."

"Kaspar didn't say anything. He was screaming."

"Not him," Jonah said. "It."

The hold was very quiet.

Yara stood at the bottom of her ladder with a fang in her hand and looked at a Collar who had just told her that a lorica had asked him for something, and Jonah watched the thing go across her face that had gone across the Censor's on a shelf when a Collar said the wall had shown him something, and it was the face of a person who has spent a long time being the only one who knows a thing, and has just been told it by a stranger, and would rather not have been.

"Put your plate away," she said.

He tried. It went. Not clean; the seals let go last, wrong, the way they had shut, and the throat came open in a lump, and he knelt on the deck with his neck bare and his hands bare and looked at her.

"Hal. Come down off that ladder and get a bowl of something into him. Pike, Dov's leg. Kaspar." She stopped. She went across the hold and knelt beside her mate and put her hand on the bare arm, on the grey patch, and he made a sound, and she did not take the hand away. "Kaspar. It's still in there. I can feel it. It's half and it's alive and it's not coming back to what it was and you're not going to be a mate on a Halyard ship with half a cutting, and I'm sorry. I'll say that once. I'll not say it again because it won't help." She looked at Jonah over the mate's head. "You did that."

"Yes."

"And you'd do it again."

"Not to him."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Jonah said. "It's not." He did not look away. "Ask me the other thing. The one you came down to ask. It's not what am I. You'd not have come down the ladder for that with your heart slowed on purpose. You came down because Pike said backward, and a thing that seals a College cask from inside isn't a convict, and you wanted to see it before Kaspar put it out the lock, because you've a use for a thing like that, and you were counting what it would cost you before you got to the bottom of the ladder, and you've counted, and it's come out. So ask."

Yara knelt beside her mate with her hand on his arm and looked at him for a long time.

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight you eat, and you get your legs, and you sit in that cask with the lid off and Pike sits on a stool with a bar and watches you, and if you put your plate up he hits you with the bar and then we'll all find out what happens. Tomorrow I'll ask." She stood up. "It's thirty-one days to the yard. You've a month to get your new answer. I've a month to decide if I want to hear it."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's a Halyard ship." She went to the ladder. "Nothing on it's fair. It's mine." She stopped with her hand on the rail and did not turn round. "The letter. The girl's. Where is it?"

"Left side. Over my heart."

"Then eat," Yara said, "so that when you find her you're not a thing that came out of a box," and went up.

---

He ate.

Hal brought a bowl and it was fish, a Belt fish, in a broth, and he took it in hands that shook and got it to his mouth and it was the first thing he had tasted since Ullan's last morning and he did not taste it. He got it down. He got a second down. The hunger, the other one, Tacitus's, did not go down with it, because fish was not matter and a thing that ate loricae did not eat fish, and it sat in the doorway of him with three grades of a Halyard boy's cutting in it and the rest of that cutting alive on the deck ten paces off, and it looked, and did not lean, because it had been told no, and had heard why.

He sat in the cask with the lid off. Pike sat on a stool with the bar across his knees, the way the old man had sat with a stick, and watched him, and did not say anything for an hour, and then said:

"Thirty years I've opened casks."

"Yes."

"I've never once had one open me." The old Halyard looked at the bar. "That mark. Backward. You said with your hand."

"Yes."

"Show me."

Jonah looked at him. Then he put his palm flat on the inside of the cask, on the dark glass of the Vein that had held him a month, and asked, and the shape went out through the plate on his palm into the glass, the knot of closed lines, and it took, and it was the wrong way round, and Pike leaned over with the lamp and looked at it for a long time the way he had looked at the one on the lid before he got the bar.

"That's the College's."

"Yes."

"And you're a Collar."

"I was." Jonah took his hand off the glass. "A Flamen put a rod on my collar seven years ago on a dock and it came off and I went down a hole, and a man taught me on a rock for five years what every mark in the Empire was for, with a stick, and I've a bit of the stick here." He touched his chest. "That's what I was. What I am's the thing your captain's going to ask tomorrow, and I told her the truth. I've not got it yet."

Pike sat with the bar.

"Kaspar's my sister's boy," he said, after a while.

Jonah did not say anything.

"I'm telling you so it's said. She'll want to know what it was. When we're home." The old man looked at the mate, who was asleep, or something like it, with his arm bare on the deck. "I'll tell her it stopped. I don't know what else I'll tell her. I don't know what to call it." He looked at Jonah. "Have you a name for it? The thing that stopped?"

Jonah thought about a word at the bottom of a well.

"It has," he said. "It's called Tacitus. It stopped because I asked it and it asked me why and I said he said please, and it had heard that word once before, from a guard, on a road, and it knew what it was for." He closed his eyes. "That's the whole of it. Tell her that. Tell her it knew what the word was for."

Pike did not say anything for a long time.

Then he put the bar down on the deck beside the stool, and left it there, and Jonah heard it, and did not open his eyes.
