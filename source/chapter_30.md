# Chapter 30: The Trimmer

Sabinus walked him up the road the way a man walks a dog he has not decided about, behind and to one side, with the fang down.

The rifles had gone. Jonah felt them go, thirty pairs of boots up the Deep road past the slab galleries and the door and the Cradle, and stop there, beyond the Cradle, where a Centurion had told a prefect to put them, and the road between was empty, the lamps too far apart, the Vein breathing in the rock on the left hand and the whole dark on the right. Two men walking. He had walked it with buckets three years and with a slate and with the old man's blood on his hands and with a blanket, and he walked it now with nothing, and with a Centurion four paces behind him whose heart he could feel through the rock, and it was not going fast.

That was the thing about Sabinus. Jonah put it with the rest. Thirty rifles had come down the road with their hearts going and a prefect with nothing to lose had stood at the turn with his going steady because it had nowhere left to go, and Sabinus had come down through them with the fang out and his heart had been slow, and it was slow now, walking a Collar up a road to kill him, the way it had been slow on the night of the riot walking through men with chisels. He was not afraid. He had been afraid once, of a finger of steel in a seam, and he had thought about it every night for two years, and he had come down the Throat tonight to stop thinking about it.

"Here," Jonah said, and stopped.

The door was on his right. Steel, in the rock, with a lock in it that had one key, and the key was in the garrison, and the door had not been opened since the month's end when Tullus's men had come down with a lamp and found the room empty and the shelf empty and sixteen slabs of a dead man's count leaning on the rock. It had been opened since. It had been opened by a hand.

He put his hand on the lock.

Sabinus stopped four paces off and watched a Collar put his palm flat on a prefect's lock, and watched the plate come up under the palm, dark on dark, and go into the steel the way the old man's had gone into the Vein, and heard the lock do what a lock does when it has been asked in the only tongue it has, and the door came open on the whole dark of the Chamber.

"Ah," Sabinus said.

Jonah went in.

He did not need the lamp. The room was drawn in him the way the road was; he had spent three hours a night on its floor for a year and a half and he went across it in the dark to the shelf and put his hand out and the stick was there, across the empty rock where the old man had lain with it on his chest, ash, a metre long, worn pale where a hand had held it for twenty years. He took it. He weighed it once in his hand the way he had weighed it every night for five years from the wrong end, and went back out onto the road.

Sabinus looked at it.

"A stick."

"He taught with it."

"The old Blank."

"No." Jonah let the door swing to behind him. It did not lock; it did not need to. "The old Blank was Auger. You killed him with a backhand because he made you hurry. This was the other one. The one in the room. Twenty-three years behind that door with one key, and nine that I knew him, and you never saw him, Centurion, because nobody did, because a prefect doesn't show a Centurion the thing he does his arithmetic with." He put the stick on his shoulder. "He'd seen you. He'd read every page there was, before they cut him in the Forum, and he read the road through a wall for twenty years after, and there was a page about you. About a client of the Princeps' house who washed out of the Ludi because he could not bear to grind, and went to the Belt with a licence, and trimmed twice in a yard, against men who came out to trim a Centurion because it was cheap. And the page said: the Claudian Form without the Wall. A wall that can shed is a man pretending. And a man pretending can be made to hurry."

Sabinus's face did not do anything.

"He said it with this," Jonah said. "Every night. For five years. I've never won with anything else."

"You've never won."

"Once," Jonah said. "The shoulder. In the spring."

He took the stick off his shoulder.

---

He lost for two minutes and he knew it in the first ten seconds, and it was not the losing that he would remember. It was the knowing.

He had known, on the rock of the Cradle, with the old man's stick finding every place he was wrong, that there was a thing above the Forms, and it was grade, and the Forms were what a man did about it. Thirty-two against forty-five was thirteen grades and a licence. It was a plate two bands past anything a chisel was made for, and a fang that came from a forearm that had drawn it every day for twenty years and had never once had to think, and a speed that was not a thing Sabinus did, it was a thing he was, the way the fever was in Jonah, everywhere, evenly. Jonah had drilled the Wall for a year and the half step for two and the hand where the man is going for one, and in the first ten seconds on the Deep road he did the Wall, because there was nothing else to do, and the fang came through it.

Not through. Into. It came for the throat seam, the garrison way, and the plate went up over the seam because he did not lie to himself about where he was afraid, and the fang went into the plate a finger and stopped, and Sabinus turned his wrist and the fang came out and went in again, low, at the hip, before Jonah's plate had finished being where it was, and that was a finger too, and it hurt the way the cold had hurt on the nineteenth day, in the middle of him, and Sabinus stepped back a pace with the fang down and looked at him.

"That's the Wall," the Centurion said. "That's a Principal doing the Claudian Wall against a Centurion's fang. It holds. It'll hold twenty times. I've time."

He came in again.

Jonah used the stick. He would say that, later, to the one person he told, and she would say, a stick, and he would say, yes, ash, a metre long, against a licensed fang, because it was the only thing on the road that Sabinus had to look at. He put it up, and the fang took it, and the stick held, once, because ash holds once against anything, and the fang came off it and went past his ear and he stepped inside it, not the half step, the ugly one, nobody's, and put the stick across the Centurion's knee the way the old man had put it across his, and it landed, and Sabinus did not go down. He did not even shift. A stick across a Centurion's knee was a thing the plate did not notice, and the fang came round, the rope cut, the one that had opened Auger from shoulder to hip, and Jonah went under it by the width of a hand and felt it take the plate off the back of his shoulder in a strip, and the strip did not come back.

That was the second thing he would remember. The plate did not come back where the fang had been. It had always come back, on the derelict, on the road, in the cut, over the seam, everywhere, fitted, right, and on the Deep road at thirty-two against forty-five it went where the fang went and did not come back, and he understood, going under the third cut with the stick up, that a licensed fang did not cut plate. It ate it. A little. The way he ate. Only matter cuts matter, the old man had said on the first night in the Cradle with a stick across his knees, and the fang was matter and the plate was matter and the fang was two bands better, and the plate was going, a strip at a time, and he was thirty-two and settled and it was not enough, and he had known that on the rock at the turn with his hands on his knees, and had come out anyway, because of an old man from Marl who sang.

"He taught you the Wall," Sabinus said. He was not breathing hard. "He taught you my step; I saw it on the boy, badly. He taught you the old Blank's trick with the second hand, and you'll try it, because it's the only thing you've got that's put steel in me." He came in, high, and the stick took it, and held, and came away with a bite out of it. "He didn't teach you to win. He couldn't. He was a Blank in a room."

"He was a Master of the College."

Sabinus stopped.

It was not a step back. It was a stop, mid-road, with the fang up, for the length of a breath, and Jonah saw it and put it with the rest and did not use it, because it was not the thing he was waiting for.

"The struck one," Sabinus said. "The galleries."

"Aulus Terentius Pharus. Censor. He designed the galleries and told the Senate what the wall is and they cut him in the Forum for it and struck him and sent him to the Belt to do arithmetic behind a door." Jonah kept the stick up. "He read the Sertorian Form out of a book that doesn't exist anymore and he taught it to me with this, on a rock, and you're the first man to see it in a hundred years, Centurion, and you're not going to know what you saw."

Sabinus came in.

He came in the way he had come in on the road on the night of the riot, at the pace a man walks when he is going somewhere, and it was faster than anything Jonah had drilled against and he knew, going back, with the stick taking the first cut and the second and the plate taking the third in a strip off his forearm, that the talking was done, that a Centurion who had been made to stop once by a name would not be made to stop by anything else, and that he had perhaps a minute of plate left, and he went back up the road with it, a pace at a time, past the door, toward the Cradle, with the fang coming and the stick going to pieces in his hands.

It broke on the fourth cut.

Not the way a stick breaks, across the knee. The fang went through it. It went through ash a metre long and worn pale where a hand had held it for twenty years, and the stick was two sticks, and the near one was in Jonah's right hand and the far one was on the rock of the road, and Sabinus looked at the half in his hand and said, without any weight:

"That's the end of the old man."

"Yes," Jonah said.

He looked at the half stick. It was a hand long. It was a wind-up, a forearm, a tell, a thing a man had to look at, and he held it out to his right the way a man holds a thing he is about to hit with, and let his left hand hang.

Sabinus watched the stick.

He had watched it for two minutes. He was Claudian without the Wall; he was a man who had drilled a Form built to stand and take, and had never had the gift to stand, so he shed, and the shedding was a step, and the step was the water step, the one Jonah had never once done right, and it went where the blade was not. Always. Three hundred times on the rock and three times on the road and twice tonight. And it went, Jonah had seen tonight and put with the rest, to the right. Sabinus's right. Away from the left armpit, where the plate was newer than the rest, where a Blank had put a finger of chisel two years ago and the Centurion had thought about it every night since. A wall that can shed is a man pretending. A man pretending can be made to hurry. And a man who is afraid of a seam steps away from it, every time, without knowing that he does, because the fear is in the step before the step is in the man.

The hand where the man is going.

Jonah came in with the stick.

He came in the way Auger had come at Sabinus, low, short, a feint at the belly with the half stick and everything on his face saying the second hand, the old Blank's trick, and Sabinus read it, because it was written to be read, and did not hurry. He was better than that. He let the stick come and stepped, once, half a pace, the water step, to his right, into the place the stick was not, and put the fang through the place Jonah was going to be.

Jonah was there.

He did not step. He did not go under it. He had drilled for a year to let it in and it was the hardest thing the old man had ever put in his hands, harder than the Wall, harder than Bearing, because everything in a man says go away from the blade and the Sertorian Form said let it come. The plate went up over the throat seam, the Wall, and the fang came for the seam the way it had twenty times, and this time the plate did not stop it a finger in.

It opened.

He opened it. He would not be able to say how, later, any more than he could say how he had opened the lock; he stood in the dark of the Deep road with a Centurion's fang coming for his throat and he said to the thing in the doorway of him, in the old tongue, without words, the thing he had said to a shackle: that. And the plate over his throat, which was Tacitus, which was fed on eleven slabs and a College shackle and had not been polite for four hours, opened, the way it had opened for the slabs in the dark, and the fang went in.

Sabinus felt it go in. Jonah saw him feel it. A licensed Centurion's fang, drawn every day for twenty years, went into a Collar's throat the way it had gone into Auger's chest, and did not come out the other side, and did not stop on bone, and did not stop, and the Centurion's face, which had been bored on the road with a chisel in its seam and had stopped being bored once, did the thing again, and it was not fear this time. It was a man feeling his own arm being eaten.

Because the fang was not a thing. The old man had said it with a stick. A fang is not a sword; a sword is a thing a man carries; a fang is the lorica, put out, the way a hand is the man, put out, and Tacitus, which had eaten a shackle's want one small dead want at a time and had eaten a fang off Varro's dead arm on a derelict and had eaten eleven slabs of its mother slowly in the dark, closed on a live Centurion's fang three fingers into Jonah's throat and ate it, from the point back, the way the wall closed over a bite, and the fang went in and was not, and what it was made of went down into the middle of Jonah and arrived, and behind the fang, up the fang, along the arm it came from, the cutting in Sabinus's forearm felt a thing it had never felt in twenty years, and said so.

"Only matter cuts matter," Jonah said. It came out of a throat with a fang in it. It came out in the old tongue. "I eat matter."

And his left hand, which had been hanging, which had been nowhere near the stick, which had been useless the whole of the fight, was on Sabinus's left shoulder, because the shoulder had stepped into it, and the fang came out of the palm, a finger from the newer plate, at the seam where the Centurion's arm met his chest, and did not stop.

Sabinus said, "Oh."

He said it the way a man says it who has been told a thing he should have known. Not loud. Not to Jonah. To the road, to the newer plate, to a finger of chisel two years ago and a Blank in the light who had gone into the dark, and the plate came off him in a shiver from the shoulder down, all of it, the grey licensed plate two bands past anything a chisel was made for, the way a young guard's had come off in a cut, and Gnaeus Sabinus, client of the Princeps' house, sat down on the rock of the Deep road between two lamps with a Collar's hand on his shoulder, and looked at the hand, and stopped.

Jonah took the hand away.

He stood over him with the half stick in his right hand and a strip of plate gone from his shoulder and his forearm and a hole in his throat that was closing, because the plate that had opened was closing, slowly, the way the wall closed, and the hunger came up.

It did not ask. It had asked for six years. It had leaned toward two dead boys' cuttings in a cut and toward two more at the road's end and had been told no and had settled, and it had eaten a Centurion's fang three fingers into Jonah's throat and found out what a licensed cutting tasted like, and it did not ask now. It went. It went out of him through the hand he had taken away and into the dead man's forearm and found the cutting there, the licensed one, forty-five and trimmed twice, the thing that had put a fang through Auger and three men with chisels and had come down the Throat to remind the Deep, and it did to it what the wall did to a man who was ready.

He felt it go in.

Not the way the slabs had gone in, slowly, settling. Not the way the shackle had, one bite. It came in the way the cold had come on the nineteenth day, through everything, and it was not cold. It was the opposite of cold. It was a fifth of a Centurion's lorica arriving in a Principal at once, thirteen grades in the time it took a man to sit down on a road, and the settling that had fought him for a month from thirty-one to thirty-two did not fight now, it did not have time, it was over before it started, and he stood on the Deep road with a dead Centurion at his feet and was forty-one.

Unsettled. He knew the word. The old man had said it with a stick: a grade you have not slept in is a fever you have not had yet. He had it now. It came up through him from the middle out and it was in his hands and his teeth and behind his eyes, and behind his eyes it was white, and then it was nothing, and he stood on the road and could not see the lamps.

He could not see anything.

He stood very still and waited for it to come back and it did not, and he put his hand out and the rock was there, the Vein, warm, breathing, and he put his palm flat on it and felt the road the way he had felt it for two years, boots and hearts through stone, and there were thirty hearts past the Cradle, and eleven in the slab galleries, and one on the road.

Coming down.

---

He would tell the whole of it, once, to the one person, and she would stop him there and say, you couldn't see. And he would say, no. And she would say, then you didn't know. And he would say, I knew there was a man on the road with a rifle coming down alone toward a Collar who had just killed a Centurion, in the dark, past the point where the prefect had told the guard to stop, and I knew what a man does with a rifle when he comes round a lamp and sees that. I knew all of that. I'd counted it at the turn with my hands on my knees. I hadn't decided. And I was forty-one and blind and the thing in me had just eaten a Centurion and was not polite, and the man came round the lamp, and my hand went where he was going.

That was all. It was the Sertorian Form. It was a hand that went where a man was going before the man knew, and the man was going for the rifle, because he had been told to and it was not personal, and the hand was already on his shoulder, and the fang came out of the palm at the seam where an old guard's neck met a grey plate that was Tiro and had been Tiro for thirty years, and did not stop.

He heard the rifle hit the rock.

He heard the man sit down. He heard him breathe, once, and it was a sound he knew; he had heard it on the deck of the Kestrel on the first night and on the Throat with a list and in a tunnel with a lamp, a man breathing in before he said a number, and the number did not come, and Jonah stood in the whole dark with his hand on a shoulder he could not see and knew, before the breath was done, whose it was.

"Decimus," he said.

The old guard did not say anything. He was sitting on the rock of the Deep road with his back against the Vein and a Collar's hand on his shoulder and a fang in the seam of his neck, and his heart, which Jonah had listened for once in seven years, at a count, and had heard going slow and steady, was going slow, and was going.

"Four four seven one," Decimus said.

"I can't see you."

"No." The old guard's voice was the voice for things that were not personal. It was very quiet. "You'll not want to. Sit."

Jonah sat. He sat down on the rock beside him in the dark with the half stick still in his hand and his back to the wall, the way he had sat beside Ullan, and the old guard breathed beside him, and the breaths had a count to them, and the count was short.

"Orders," Decimus said. "Tullus. Go down and look. If the Centurion's dead shoot the Collar. If the Collar's dead come back and say." He breathed. "I said, send a young one. He said, they're afraid of him. You're not."

"You should have been."

"I'm a guard on a rock, boy. I've been afraid of the right things for thirty years and it's never once made a difference to the count." The lined face that Jonah could not see did something; he heard it in the voice, the thing from the tunnel, the almost-smile, the sixth or seventh time in seven years. "I came down slow. I'd my rifle down. I was going to say your number first. I'd got as far as the breath."

Jonah could not say anything.

"It's not personal," Decimus said. "Either way. I've said it to you seven years and you've never once believed it and it was always true. Tullus gave an order and I went, because that's the count, and you did what you did, because that's yours, and it's not on you, four four seven one. Don't put it on the slate. You've enough."

"It's on the slate."

"Then write it right." The old guard breathed. It took longer. "I've a letter. Inside. Left side. It's got a tree on it."

Jonah put his hand into the coat.

He found it by feel. Cheap garrison paper, folded many times, soft at the folds, in the inside pocket on the left side over the heart, and he took it out and held it in the hand that was not holding the stick, and could not see it, and knew what was on it, because Decimus had told him four years ago on the rock by the cage: the tree, marked MINE, and the seven hills, and the winters counted at the edge, and there would be more winters counted now, in a bigger hand.

"Sixteen," Decimus said. "Counting."

"I'll find her."

"You'll not. You're in a cask." A breath. "Or the wall. I wrote you into the wall this morning. That was the mark. I wrote, four four seven one, went in the night of the nineteenth, the one who carries went the night before, that's the Deep, and I'd not have had to change a word, whichever way tonight went." The old guard's voice was going. "I read numbers, boy. I don't read the old tongue. But I can count a man, and I counted you at the Throat, and you came out a thing that was going up it, one way or the other, and I wrote it so the road could say so."

Jonah sat with the letter in his hand.

"Decimus Alba," he said. "Of Etruria."

"That's it."

"Sula's father."

"Write that first," Decimus said. "Before the rest. They put the rest first on a slab and it's wrong." And breathed, once more, and Jonah felt it through the rock, through the plate, through a hand that was on a shoulder in the dark, the way he had felt the old man and Ullan, except that Tacitus did not stand between, because he had not asked it to, and he would not have, and it went through him, all of it, the count, the whole of a man going, and it was the fourth time, and it was the worst, and he let it.

Then it was quiet on the Deep road.

---

He sat for the length of a count.

He did not know how long a count was. It was as long as it took Decimus to read the Deep at dusk, all the numbers, in the voice for things that were not personal, and he sat in the dark with his eyes not working and said them, under his breath, in the old tongue, from the first to the last, all of them, and at the end of them he said four four seven one and answered himself, and made a mark on nothing with his finger.

Then he closed the old guard's eyes. He did it by feel. He had done it for two boys at the road's end by lamplight and he did it for a man of sixty in the whole dark with a hand that had killed him, and the lids were dry, and went down, and stayed.

"I'm not going to say it's not personal," he said, aloud, in the road's tongue, to the man beside him. "I've never once believed it."

He put the letter in his own shirt, on the left side, over the place under his ribs where the door was. He put the half stick beside it. He stood up, with his hand on the wall, and the Vein breathed against his palm, and the fever was white behind his eyes and the plate on him was forty-one and unsettled and had holes in it where a fang had eaten, and was closing, slowly, the way the wall closed, and would take a day a grade, nine days, in the dark, and he did not have nine days. He had until the cage came down for the harvest at dawn.

He thought about the old man, then. Not Ullan; the other one. Pharus, on the rock of the Cradle with the stick across his knees, on a night he could not have named, saying: you will do a bad thing, boy, and you will know it, and the knowing is the whole of what separates you from the Vibian, and it will not feel like enough, and it is not, and it is what there is.

"It's not enough," he said, to the dark.

The dark did not answer. It never had.

But the thing in the doorway of him, which had eaten a Centurion and was not polite and was forty-one and had put a fang through a man who said please, did the small thing, the thing with no hand, in the place where the seam had been and was a scar, and Jonah stood on the Deep road with his palm on the wall and said, the way he had said it twice in the Cradle:

"I know."

And then, because it had been said with a stick and it was the one line on the slate he could still write, and because Ullan had said write it with two and Decimus had said write that first: "Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father. He said my number seven years and it was never personal and I never once believed him, and he came down the road slow with his rifle down to say it first, and I didn't let him." He put the name in with the others, in its place, and it went in beside the old man's, because there was room. There was always room. It was the only thing about a count that was kind.

He went down the road.

Not up. Down, past the door, past the slab galleries where eleven men lay with their gloves off and did not breathe, to the turn, and the gallery, and the third row, and cask thirty-one with its lid off and the ring of seventy slabs standing dark inside it, and he found it by his hands, and he knew, standing beside it blind with a dead man's letter over his heart, what he had not known at the turn with his hands on his knees.

He knew who he had killed on the way to the road.
