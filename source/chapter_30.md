# Chapter 30: The Trimmer

Sabinus walked him up the road four paces behind, to one side, with the fang down.

The rifles had gone. Jonah felt them go, thirty pairs of boots up the Deep road past the slab galleries and the door and the Cradle, and stop beyond it, where a Centurion had told a prefect to put them. The road between was empty. The lamps were too far apart. Two men walking. He could feel the Centurion's heart through the rock, and it was not going fast.

Thirty rifles had come down the road with their hearts going. Tullus's had been steady because it had nowhere left to go. Sabinus had come down through them with the fang out and his heart slow, and it was slow now, walking a Collar up a road to kill him. He was not afraid. He had been afraid once, of a finger of steel in a seam. He had come down the Throat tonight to stop thinking about it.

"Here," he said, and stopped.

The door was on his right. Steel, in the rock, with a lock that had one key, and the key was in the garrison. He put his hand on the lock.

Sabinus stopped four paces off and watched a Collar put his palm flat on a prefect's lock. He watched the plate come up under the palm, dark on dark, and go into the steel. The lock did what a lock does when it has been asked in the only tongue it has. The door came open on the whole dark of the Chamber.

"Ah," Sabinus said.

Jonah went in.

He did not need a lamp. He had spent three hours a night on this floor for a year and a half. He crossed it in the dark to the shelf and put his hand out. The stick was there, across the empty rock where the old man had lain with it on his chest. Ash, a metre long, worn pale where a hand had held it twenty years. He took it, weighed it once, and went back out onto the road.

Sabinus looked at it.

"A stick."

"He taught with it," Jonah said.

"The old Blank."

Jonah said, "No. The old Blank was Auger. You killed him with a backhand because he made you hurry. This was the other one. The one in the room." He let the door swing to behind him. "Twenty-three years behind that door with one key, and nine that I knew him. You never saw him, Centurion. Nobody did. A prefect doesn't show a Centurion what he does his arithmetic with."

"And he saw me."

Jonah said, "He'd read every page there was, before they cut him in the Forum. He read the road through a wall for twenty years after, and there was a page about you." He put the stick on his shoulder. "A client of the Princeps' house who washed out of the Ludi because he could not bear to grind. Who went to the Belt with a licence and trimmed twice in a yard, against men who came out to trim a Centurion because it was cheap. The page said: the Claudian Form without the Wall. A wall that can shed is a man pretending. And a man pretending can be made to hurry."

Sabinus's face did nothing.

"He said it with this," Jonah said. "Every night. Five years. I've never won with anything else."

"You've never won."

"Once. The shoulder. In the spring," Jonah said. "I'm hoping to make it two, Centurion, purely to keep my own average respectable."

Sabinus did not laugh. Nobody had, in nine years.

"That land?" he said.

"No."

"Worth trying anyway."

He took the stick off his shoulder.

---

He lost for two minutes and knew it in the first ten seconds.

Thirty-two against forty-five was thirteen grades and a licence. A plate two bands past anything a chisel was made for. A fang from a forearm that had drawn it every day for twenty years. Speed that was not something Sabinus did but something he was. Jonah had drilled the Wall for a year, the half step for two, the hand where the man is going for one. In the first ten seconds he did the Wall, because there was nothing else to do.

The fang came for the throat seam. The plate went up over the seam. The fang went in a finger and stopped.

Sabinus turned his wrist. The fang came out and went in again, low, at the hip, before the plate had finished being where it was. A finger. It hurt in the middle of him. Sabinus stepped back a pace with the fang down.

"That's the Wall," he said. "A Principal doing the Claudian Wall against a Centurion's fang. It holds. It'll hold twenty times. I've time."

He came in again.

Jonah put the stick up and the fang took it. The stick held, once, because ash holds once against anything. The fang came off it and went past his ear. He stepped inside, not the half step, the ugly one, and put the stick across the Centurion's knee.

It landed. Sabinus did not go down. He did not shift.

The fang came round in the rope cut, the one that had opened Auger from shoulder to hip. Jonah went under it by the width of a hand and felt it take the plate off the back of his shoulder in a strip.

The strip did not come back.

The plate had always come back. On the derelict, on the road, in the cut, over the seam. On the Deep road at thirty-two against forty-five it went where the fang went and stayed gone. A licensed fang did not cut plate. It ate it. A little, a strip at a time. Only matter cuts matter, the old man had said on the first night with the stick across his knees. The fang was matter and the plate was matter and the fang was two bands better.

"He taught you the Wall," Sabinus said. He was not breathing hard. "He taught you my step; I saw it on the boy, badly. He taught you the old Blank's trick with the second hand. You'll try it, because it's the only thing you've got that's put steel in me." He came in high and the stick took it, and came away with a bite out of it. "He didn't teach you to win. He couldn't. He was a Blank in a room."

"He was a Master of the College."

Sabinus stopped.

Not a step back. A stop, mid-road, with the fang up, for the length of a breath.

"The struck one," he said. "The galleries."

"Aulus Terentius Pharus. Censor. He designed the galleries and told the Senate what the wall is. They cut him in the Forum for it and struck him and sent him to the Belt to do arithmetic behind a door." Jonah kept the stick up. "He read the Sertorian Form out of a book that doesn't exist anymore and taught it to me with this, on a rock. You're the first man to see it in a hundred years, Centurion, and you're not going to know what you saw."

Sabinus came in.

Faster than anything Jonah had drilled against. The stick took the first cut and the second. The plate took the third, in a strip off his forearm. The talking was done. A Centurion who had been made to stop once by a name would not be stopped by anything else. Jonah had perhaps a minute of plate left. He went back up the road a pace at a time, past the door, toward the Cradle, with the fang coming and the stick going to pieces in his hands.

It broke on the fourth cut.

Not across the knee. The fang went through it. Ash a metre long, worn pale by twenty years of one hand, was two sticks. The near half was in Jonah's right hand and the far half was on the rock.

"That's the end of the old man," Sabinus said, without weight.

"Yes."

Jonah looked at the half stick. A hand long. A wind-up, a tell, something a man had to look at. He held it out to his right and let his left hand hang.

Sabinus watched the stick.

He had watched it for two minutes. He was Claudian without the Wall. He had drilled a Form built to stand and take, and had never had the gift to stand, so he shed. The shedding was the water step. It went where the blade was not. Always. Three hundred times on the rock and three on the road and twice tonight. And it went to the right. Sabinus's right. Away from the left armpit, where the plate was newer, where a Blank had put a finger of chisel two years ago. A man who is afraid of a seam steps away from it every time, without knowing that he does. The fear is in the step before the step is in the man.

The hand where the man is going.

Jonah came in with the stick. Low, short, a feint at the belly, everything on his face saying the second hand, the old Blank's trick. Sabinus read it, because it was written to be read. He did not hurry. He let the stick come and stepped, once, half a pace, to his right, into the place the stick was not, and put the fang through the place Jonah was going to be.

Jonah was there.

He did not step. He did not go under it. Everything in a man says go away from the blade. The Sertorian Form said let it come. The plate went up over the throat seam, the Wall, and the fang came for the seam as it had twenty times. This time the plate did not stop it a finger in.

It opened.

He opened it. He said to Tacitus, in the old tongue, without words, what he had said to a shackle: that. The plate over his throat was Tacitus, fed on eleven slabs and a College shackle, and it had not been polite for four hours. It opened, and the fang went in.

Sabinus felt it go in. A licensed Centurion's fang went into a Collar's throat and did not come out the other side. It did not stop on bone. It did not stop. The Centurion's face, which had been bored on the road with a chisel in its seam and had stopped being bored once, did it again. Not fear this time. A man feeling his own arm being eaten.

A fang is not a sword. A sword is a thing a man carries. A fang is the lorica, put out, as a hand is the man, put out. Tacitus closed on a live Centurion's fang three fingers into Jonah's throat and ate it from the point back. What it was made of went down into the middle of Jonah and arrived. Up the fang, along the arm, the cutting in Sabinus's forearm felt something it had never felt in twenty years.

"Only matter cuts matter," Jonah said, in the old tongue, through a throat with a fang in it. "I eat matter."

His left hand, which had hung useless the whole fight, was on Sabinus's left shoulder, because the shoulder had stepped into it. The fang came out of the palm a finger from the newer plate, at the seam where the arm met the chest, and did not stop.

Sabinus said, "Oh."

Not loud. Not to Jonah. To the road, to the newer plate, to a finger of chisel two years ago and a Blank in the light who had gone into the dark. The plate came off him in a shiver from the shoulder down. Gnaeus Sabinus, client of the Princeps' house, sat down on the rock of the Deep road between two lamps with a Collar's hand on his shoulder. He looked at the hand, and stopped.

Jonah took the hand away.

The hole in his throat was closing, slowly, as the wall closed. The hunger came up.

It did not ask. It had asked for six years. It had leaned toward two dead boys in a cut and two more at the road's end and been told no. It had just found out what a licensed cutting tasted like. It went. Out of him through the hand he had taken away, into the dead forearm, to the cutting there. Forty-five, trimmed twice. It did to it what the wall did to a man who was ready.

It came in through everything, and it was the opposite of cold. A fifth of a Centurion's lorica arriving in a Principal at once. Thirteen grades in the time it took a man to sit down on a road. The settling that had fought him a month from thirty-one to thirty-two did not have time to fight. He stood on the Deep road with a dead Centurion at his feet and was forty-one.

Unsettled. The old man had said it with a stick: a grade you have not slept in is a fever you have not had yet. It came up through him from the middle out, into his hands and his teeth and behind his eyes. Behind his eyes it was white, and then it was nothing.

He could not see the lamps. He could not see anything.

He stood very still and waited for it to come back, and it did not. He put his hand out and the rock was there, the Vein, warm. He put his palm flat on it and felt the road, boots and hearts through stone. Thirty hearts past the Cradle. Eleven in the slab galleries. One on the road.

Coming down.

---

A man with a rifle, alone, coming down toward a Collar who had just killed a Centurion, past the point where the prefect had told the guard to stop. Jonah knew what a man does with a rifle when he comes round a lamp and sees that. He had counted it at the turn with his hands on his knees. He had not decided. He was forty-one and blind, and Tacitus had just eaten a Centurion and was not polite.

The man came round the lamp. Jonah's hand went where he was going.

That was all. The Sertorian Form. A hand that goes where a man is going before the man knows. The man was going for the rifle, because he had been told to. The hand was already on his shoulder. The fang came out of the palm at the seam where the neck met a grey plate that had been Tiro for thirty years, and did not stop.

The rifle hit the rock.

The man sat down. He breathed in, once, and Jonah knew the sound. He had heard it on the deck of the Kestrel the first night, and on the Throat with a list, and in a tunnel with a lamp. A man breathing in before he said a number.

The number did not come.

"Decimus," he said.

The old guard sat on the rock with his back against the Vein and a Collar's hand on his shoulder and a fang in the seam of his neck. His heart was going slow. And it was going.

"Four four seven one," Decimus said.

"I can't see you."

"No." The voice for things that were not personal, very quiet. "You'll not want to. Sit."

Jonah sat down beside him in the dark with the half stick still in his hand and his back to the wall, the way he had sat beside Ullan. The old guard's breaths had a count to them, and the count was short.

"Orders. Tullus. Go down and look. If the Centurion's dead, shoot the Collar. If the Collar's dead, come back and say." He breathed. "I said, send a young one. He said, they're afraid of him. You're not."

"You should have been."

"I'm a guard on a rock, boy. I've been afraid of the right things thirty years and it's never once made a difference to the count." Jonah heard the almost-smile in the voice, the sixth or seventh time in seven years. "I came down slow. Rifle down. I was going to say your number first. I'd got as far as the breath."

Jonah could not say anything.

"It's not personal," Decimus said. "Either way. I've said it to you seven years and you've never once believed it, and it was always true. Tullus gave an order and I went. That's the count. You did what you did, because that's yours. It's not on you. Don't put it on the slate. You've enough."

"It's on the slate."

"Then write it right." A breath. It took longer. "I've a letter. Inside. Left side. It's got a tree on it."

Jonah put his hand into the coat and found it by feel. Cheap garrison paper, folded many times, soft at the folds, in the inside pocket over the heart. He could not see it. He knew what was on it. Decimus had told him four years ago on the rock by the cage: the tree, marked MINE, and the seven hills, and the winters counted at the edge. There would be more winters now, in a bigger hand.

"Sixteen," Decimus said. "Counting."

"I'll find her."

"You'll not. You're in a cask." A breath. "Or the wall. I wrote you into the wall this morning. That was the mark. Four four seven one, went in the night of the nineteenth. The one who carries went the night before. That's the Deep. I'd not have had to change a word, whichever way tonight went."

"You knew."

"I read numbers, boy. I don't read the old tongue. But I can count a man. I counted you at the Throat. You came out a thing that was going up it, one way or the other, and I wrote it so the road could say so."

Jonah sat with the letter in his hand.

"Decimus Alba," he said. "Of Etruria."

"That's it."

"Sula's father."

"Write that first. Before the rest. They put the rest first on a slab and it's wrong."

He breathed once more. Jonah felt it through the rock, through the plate, through a hand on a shoulder in the dark, as he had felt the old man and Ullan. Tacitus did not stand between, because he had not asked it to, and he would not have. It went through him, all of it, the whole of a man going. It was the fourth time, and the worst, and he let it.

Then it was quiet on the Deep road.

---

He sat for the length of a count.

As long as it took Decimus to read the Deep at dusk, all the numbers. He said them under his breath, in the old tongue, from first to last. At the end he said four four seven one and answered himself, and made a mark on nothing with his finger.

Then he closed the old guard's eyes, by feel. He had done it for two boys at the road's end by lamplight. He did it for a man of sixty in the whole dark with the hand that had killed him. The lids went down and stayed.

Jonah said, "I'm not going to say it's not personal. I've never once believed it, and I'm not going to start lying to a dead man." He said it aloud, in the road's tongue.

He put the letter in his own shirt, on the left side, over the place under his ribs where the door was. He put the half stick beside it. He stood up with his hand on the wall. The fever was white behind his eyes. The plate on him was forty-one and unsettled and had holes in it where a fang had eaten. It was closing, slowly, and would take a day a grade. Nine days in the dark. He had until the cage came down for the harvest at dawn.

He thought of Pharus on the rock of the Cradle with the stick across his knees, saying: you will do a bad thing, boy, and you will know it. The knowing is the whole of what separates you from the Vibian. It will not feel like enough, and it is not, and it is what there is.

"It's not enough," he said, to the dark.

The dark did not answer. It never had.

But Tacitus did the small thing, the hand with no hand, in the place where the seam had been and was a scar. It had eaten a Centurion and was not polite. It was forty-one and had put a fang through a man who said please. It did that. Jonah stood on the Deep road with his palm on the wall and said, as he had said it twice in the Cradle:

"I know."

Then the rest, because it was the one line on the slate he could still write. Ullan had said write it with two. Decimus had said write that first. "Decimus Alba. Of Etruria. Sula's father. He said my number seven years and it was never personal and I never once believed him. He came down the road slow with his rifle down to say it first, and I didn't let him." He put the name in with the others. It went in beside the old man's, because there was room. There was always room. It was the only kind thing about a count.

He went down the road.

Not up. Down, past the door, past the slab galleries where eleven men lay with their gloves off and did not breathe. To the turn, and the gallery, and the third row. To cask thirty-one with its lid off and the ring of seventy slabs standing dark inside it. He found it by his hands. Standing beside it blind, with a dead man's letter over his heart, he knew what he had not known at the turn with his hands on his knees.

He knew who he had killed on the way to the road.
