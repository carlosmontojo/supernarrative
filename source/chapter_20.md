# Chapter 20: What the Censor Did

The count went to ten on the ninth day after the riot, by the piece, and it was the old man who did it, and Jonah understood that before anyone on the road did, and it did not help.

Decimus read it off the list at the dawn count in the voice for things that were not personal, and stopped, and read it again, because he had not read a number down in three years and did not trust his mouth with it. "Ten. Every decury. By the piece. Prefect's order." He did not look up. "The galleries, it says. The Deep's not to be pushed till it gives slabs again, and the Shallows is ten, because ten's what holds." He made a mark. "That's what it says."

The road did not make the sound. There were forty-one men on it and the eighteen had gone into the wall, and the five had gone into the wall after the post that was not the post, and Auger had gone into it last, carried by Ulli, who had carried him the length of the Deep road singing, and nobody on the road had anything left to make a sound with.

Ten. The wall's number. The number Auger had taken a cage for, and died on the rock for, and the number had come down the Throat nine days later on a slate, in an old man's hand, because a prefect who could not make the galleries hold without the Chamber's arithmetic had been told, in the same arithmetic, that the Shallows gave ten and the men who cut it had to be alive to cut it.

Auger had put his one card on the table so the other man would have to look at it. And the other man had looked at a different card, in a different hand, and folded.

Jonah stood at the wall of the seventh chamber with five men where there had been ten and cut one shard, and did not shake, and did not need to, and thought about the slate.

---

He did not go down the Deep road.

He'd told Decimus, the morning after, that the haul was done. He'd said it flat, at the count, in the road's tongue: "Six is five men and a foreman. I'm at the wall. The haul goes to whoever wants it." And Decimus had looked at him with the face from under the shelf, and had made a mark, and had said, "Nobody wants it," and had given it to a Deep man who had been touched on the shoulder in the line and had watched the hand move on, and who carried buckets past the Cradle every night for a month and never once heard the rock.

Jonah slept. That was the thing he did with the third hour, and the second, and the first. He lay on the shelf in the sleeping gallery with the lamp at the end that never went out and Pell above him, singing to the wall some nights and not others, and he said the names, because the thing under his ribs expected them and a thing that expected did not wander, and he said all of them, out of order, and Corr, and Auger, and he did not say the first one, and he slept.

The thing under his ribs did not like it.

He had known it would not. It had been fed at the face and had grown by five sizes and had stood in a cut in full plate and been unafraid, and it had learned from that, the way it learned from everything, that the body it was in was a body that went places and did things and was, most nights, on a road with a stick coming at it. And now the body lay on a shelf. Nobody hit it. It did not stand in a doorway with the wall on the other side. It did not bring plate up on the back of a hand thirty times in the dark. It cut one shard and ate one bowl and lay down, and the thing under his ribs went through it every night, reading, looking for the new bruises and the new torn places and the new way the legs had learned to stand, and there were none.

So it ate the old ones.

He felt it start in the second week. Not the arm; the arm had come back after the face, the muscle above the ring filled in over a month of buckets. This was everywhere. It was the weight coming off him the way it had on the transport, a little each day, from nowhere he could point to, and it was the fever that came now without any reason, every fourth night, then every third, the coat getting no bigger and the body inside it getting less. It was hunger with a size, and the size going the wrong way, fast, because the thing had learned at the face what full was and would not go back to not knowing.

"You're going," Pell said, in the third week, to the wall, at the change of shift. "I can see it. I said I'd say it the other way and I'm saying it. You're going, cut one. Whatever you did in the Cradle, you've stopped doing it, and the wall's got you."

"The wall hasn't got me."

"Then something has. You've the look. I've seen it on my brother, I expect, if I'd been there." The old man from Marl was quiet a moment. "You don't go down the road."

"No."

"You went down it every night for three years. You came back up it with your legs shaking and a look on you that wasn't the wall. And then Corr went to the post and you stopped." Pell turned on his shelf; Jonah heard him. "I'm not asking what's down there. I sat by the wall in the Cradle with you three days and I heard the rock and I know there's something. I'm asking if it's the something that's got you, or the not going."

Jonah lay with his hands on his chest.

"The not going," he said.

"Then go."

"He sold Corr."

"I know what he did. I was in the line. I watched the hand stop and I watched Tullus read a slate and I watched Corr step out, and I've been on this rock long enough to count what a slate is." Pell's voice did not change. "He sold Corr. And the day after he sold Tullus ten, and forty-one men are alive on this road cutting the wall's number who'd be cutting fourteen and dying of it, and Auger's in the wall having got what he took a cage for. Both. That's what a slate is. You want one or the other and there isn't one or the other. There's a slate."

"That's what Decimus said. About the Empire."

"Decimus is a fool with a rifle who brought you a bandage. He's right about most things." The old man turned back to the wall. "Go down the road, cut one. Or don't, and I'll sing over you when they carry you, and I'll get the words right, because I'll have learned them from the wall. It's got a great many."

---

He did not go in the third week, or the fourth.

He went to the Deep instead, on the fourth week's second day, because the cage came down with a new guard in it who was Vetch with a different face and a paper that said the Deep's count needed a Shallows foreman who could cut a slab, by the Chamber's numbers, and Jonah understood that the old man had written that too, and went.

The face was different. The Vein had closed over the bitten place the way skin closes, and the pale lines that had run everywhere ran now in a knot around a place the size of a table, drawn tight, and the deep-diggers cut around it and did not go near it, and Harrow, who was fair and afraid, showed him where the slabs came from now and said, "Not there. Nobody cuts there."

"Why?"

"It doesn't come off. And it's warm. Warmer than the rest." The deep foreman looked at the knot. "It's like it's holding something."

Jonah cut. It came off for him. It always would. And he stood at the face with a slab at his feet and looked at the knot, and the thing under his ribs, hungry, less every day, leaned at it, and he thought, that's the shape. That's where it found out. And he did not touch it, and did not want to, and that was new.

Ulli was at the end of the gallery on his bucket.

He had carried forty-two men out of the Cradle now, Auger the last, and he sat with his eyes closed and sang under his breath, and when Jonah's boots stopped in front of him he opened them.

"He asks," Ulli said.

"What?"

"The rock. Every night. I sit at the end of the road and he taps, and it's not to the dying, there's no dying in the Cradle, it's ten, nobody's dying. He taps to the road. Your number. Four, four, seven, one. Then he waits. Then he taps it again." The deep man's face did nothing. "Six years I never tapped back to him. I'm not going to start. But I'm telling you it's been three weeks and he taps every night, and he's seventy, cut one, and he tapped to dying men for twenty years and none of them heard, and now he's tapping to a man who can and won't."

"He sold Corr."

"He did. And he sold Tullus ten, and he wrote the paper that put you at this face so you'd eat, because you're going, everybody on the road can see it, and the only thing in this Pit that'll feed that thing without killing a man is the wall, and he'd rather you cut a slab beside Harrow than put your hand on it in the dark." Ulli closed his eyes. "That's what he did. All of it. You can carry the Corr part and not the rest if you like. Most men would. I'd think less of you."

Jonah stood in the gallery.

"Why do you care?"

"Because I've carried forty-two men out of a room he drew," the deep man said, "and sung over every one of them, and he heard it through a wall, every night, twenty years, and when you told him he said tell Ulli I heard it. Nobody in this Pit has ever said that to me. A thing I did was heard." He did not open his eyes. "He drew the room. I sing in it. You turned around in it. That's the three of us, cut one. That's what we've got. Go down the road."

---

He went on the forty-second night.

He counted it. He'd stopped counting nights and he counted these, because they were a thing he had done and not a thing that had been done to him, and there was a difference, the old man had said, and he had learned it. Forty-two nights. He picked up the buckets that the Deep man had put down at the cage, because the Deep man was glad to be rid of them, and walked down the road past the lamps that were too far apart, and past Ulli, who did not open his eyes, and into the Cradle.

He put the buckets down. He did not tap. He stood at the back, where the rock met the Vein, and waited, and the thing under his ribs, which had leaned at every wall in the Pit for three years and had not, for six weeks, leaned at this one, leaned.

The rock opened.

The old man came out with the lamp turned to a thread and the stick on his shoulder, and stopped, and looked at Jonah in the light.

He was thinner. Jonah saw that first and put it with the rest. Six weeks on one bowl a day was six weeks on one bowl a day at seventy, and the straight back was straight and the chin was level and the pale eyes went over Jonah from boots to face the way they always had, finding out where the weight was, and there was less of him. The rag of a robe hung differently. The hand on the stick was a duellist's hand and the knuckles were larger than they had been.

"You have lost a stone," the Censor said.

"So have you."

"I have lost a stone every year for twenty years. It is a habit. Yours is the thing in you eating what it cannot spare, and it will be your heart by winter, and Harrow tells me you stood at the face for three weeks and cut beside the knot and did not touch it." The old man's voice was the stone and it was not quite the stone. "Why?"

"I didn't want to."

"That is new."

"Yes."

The Censor looked at him for a long moment.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever you have carried down that road. Say it now, in whatever tongue you like, and then we will see what is left."

Jonah stood with his back to the wall the old man had drawn.

"You sold Corr," he said. "You wrote a line on a slate and Tullus read it and the hand moved, and Corr stepped out, and Sabinus put a fang through him at the post, and I watched, and Corr looked at me, and said nothing personal, and he meant it, and that's the worst of it. He meant it. He liked me. He said so. He said, you don't talk." His voice was level. He kept it level. "And you drew the room he died in. And the room I turned around in. And the road, and the Throat, and the slab where they cut Aulus of Marl out of himself, and you put the sick against the thickest wall and wrote kindness in the margin. And you told me none of it for three years while you beat a decurion out of my legs with a stick and taught me to count, and you told me on the night you'd spent Corr, so I'd have it all at once, so I'd go."

"Yes."

"And I went."

"Yes."

"And I've come back," Jonah said, "and I want you to understand why, because it's not what you think. It's not that I've forgiven it. I haven't. I don't know how. I've said his name every night for six weeks and I can't say yours, and I'm not going to be able to for a long time, and you're going to have to teach me with that on the rock between us." He looked at the old man. "I've come back because the alternative is dying ignorant. That's all. There's a thing in me that's eating my heart and a Centurion up the Throat who's afraid of seams now, and a College coming down it with glass, and I know a hand and a tongue and a count and four names on a stick and I don't know the fifth, and you do. So I'm going to use you. That's what this is. I'm going to stand on this rock and take everything you've got and walk up the Throat with it, and I want you to know that I know that's what I'm doing. Don't forget it."

The Censor stood with the stick on his shoulder and the lamp in his hand and looked at him, and the thing went across his face that Jonah had seen three times, the grief with the other thing under it, and it was there for longer than it had been on any of the three.

"It is what I am counting on," the old man said.

Jonah did not say anything.

"You think I do not know what I am. I told you what I am on the night of the line and you left, which was correct. I am a man who does arithmetic and it comes out to Corr. It came out to Corr, and it came out to ten, and it came out to a paper that put you at the face so you would eat slabs and not the wall, and it came out, twenty-two years ago, to a mine that gave ten a day and did not fall, and I have been in it since." He set the stick down on the rock between them, carefully, the way he had on the night of Tom's death. "I have never once asked you to forgive it. I have never asked anyone. I asked the Senate for something once and they cut the stripe out of my arm, and I learned. I ask you for nothing, Jonah Vale. I am asking you to take. That is what I have wanted since you tapped your number on my wall. A man who would take everything I have and use it and not carry me up the Throat with him like a debt. You have just said you will. It is the first thing you have said to me in three years that I believed entirely."

Jonah looked at the stick on the rock.

"Corr's name."

"Written. The night of the line. Anvil, north yards, four years, said a month. It is in the seventeenth column of the sixteenth slab, beside Auger, who said his own name in the Cradle to Ulli before he went, which nobody knew he had." The old man's voice was even. "And Hollen. And Bricks, who was eleven years and had a name I did not know until Pell told me through the wall, which was Marcus, and which he never used. And the boy from the transport, who was fifteen and called Tallow. And the Deep man, whose name I do not have and have written as such."

"I said I didn't know it."

"I heard. Through the rock. You said I'm sorry, I don't know it, on the road, on the night you left, to a thing that does not know what a name is." The Censor looked at the lamp. "That is the whole of what a count is. You say it when you have it and you say you do not when you do not. You have been doing it for three years on a road at night and calling it saying the names, and Bricks thought you were praying, and you were not. You were counting. You learned it from a rock before the rock taught you anything else, and it is the only thing I have ever taught anyone that I am not ashamed of."

He picked up the stick.

"So. You are twenty-six, near enough, and going, and I have six weeks to put back and a fifth Form to give you and a College coming down the Throat in eleven months, and Sabinus, whom you watched Auger make hurry, and whom you will make hurry, because you kept his face and I saw you keep it. You know the four. Say them."

"Vibia. Claudia. Fulvia. Aemilia."

"In the old tongue."

He said them in the old tongue.

"Your endings," the Censor said, "have not been a disgrace in a year, and I have not said so, because it did not need saying. It is said." He set his feet. "The fifth is called Sertoria. It is the house of a man who lost a war against the Empire and won every battle in it, and made a Senate of his own on a rock at the edge of everything, and was murdered by his friends at dinner, which is what happens to men who win every battle. His house fought with a fang that was not there until it was. I will show you what that means. Get up."

"I'm standing."

"You are standing on a rock I drew," the old man said, "with a thing in you I hid in a box and a stone off you that I put there by selling a man you liked. Stand better." And the stick came out of the dark.

---

He said the names that night on the road, after, with the buckets, in the dark between the lamps, all of them, in the order he had them, out of order, and Corr, and Auger, and Hollen, and Bricks, Marcus, who never used it, and Tallow, fifteen, and the Deep man, whose name he did not have and said so.

And Decimus Alba, of Etruria, whom he'd put under a shelf.

And at the end of it, standing in the dark with the thing under his ribs fed on nothing but a stick and listening, the way it always listened, he said the first one. Three words in the old tongue, tapped once on a wall by a man who had not said it in twenty years and had wanted it said. He said it with the endings right. He did not say it easily and he did not say it soft, and he did not say anything after it, and it was on the list again, at the top, where it had been, and it would stay there.

The thing under his ribs heard it, and added it back to the things it expected, and did not wander.
