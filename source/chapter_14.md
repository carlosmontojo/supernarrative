# Chapter 14: Quota

"Twelve," Decimus said.

He read it off the list after the count, in the voice for things that were not personal. Spring of the first year. Decury six stood in the seventh chamber and looked at him.

"Twelve," Bricks said. He said it again, which he never did. "Twelve."

"Twelve a day. Every decury. From today. Prefect's order. I don't know why and I wouldn't tell you if I did."

"We've never made twelve. Nobody's ever made twelve. Ten's the wall's number. Ten's what it gives."

"Then it'll have to give twelve," the old guard said, "or I'll be coming down the road at dusk to watch you choose." He went on down the road saying numbers.

Corr said, quietly, to nobody, "That's a war, that is. Twelve. That's plate for a legion."

Nobody asked him how he knew. Corr had been on the wall four years. He knew what the wall's numbers meant the way Pell knew what a field's did.

---

They made eleven the first day and Bricks chose Hollen for the post. Hollen took it without a sound, ten across the back at the dusk count with the road watching. The second day they made eleven again and Bricks chose Pell.

Bricks had not wanted to. But a foreman whose decury missed chose a man. Choose the same man twice and the road said you had a favourite. Choose the strong ones and the decury went from eleven to nine. Pell was sixty, farmer-built even now, and had made one shard in two days. Everybody could count.

Pell took it to the floor. Ten strokes. He got up after and went to the wall and put his chisel to the dark. His hands shook so badly the steel rang on the Vein.

"Give it here," he said.

"No," Pell said, and cut.

The third day they made eleven and a half. Jonah made the half. He cut his one shard slowly, shaking. Then he cut another, fast, and broke it in two on the rock, so it went into the bucket as a shard and a piece. Bricks looked at the piece, and at him, and said nothing, and counted it.

It was not enough. The wall gave ten.

"It gives ten because ten is what the Shallows grows back in a day," the Censor said that night, in the old tongue. Jonah understood every word now. "I did the arithmetic on this rock twenty-two years ago, and again when they built the galleries. I told the man who ordered them that ten was the number, and he said, then ten. Now a prefect who has never done a sum says twelve, because a legate on the far Limes needs plate for a legion by autumn."

"And the wall?"

"The Shallows will give twelve for a season. Then it will give eight for ten years. Every man on that road will be flogged for the difference. That is what a quota is. A man in the City deciding that a wall grows faster if you hit the men in front of it."

"Then what do I do?"

"You cut your one shard slowly, and you shake. You take your turn at the post when it comes, because it will come, and you do not die of it." The Censor picked up the stick. "The rest is not tonight."

---

The rest was the ninth night, and it was a guard called Vetch.

He was new. Twenty-two, maybe. A Blank in garrison grey with a line on his arm and plate on both forearms that he kept out all the time, so everyone could see it. He brought his fang out at the cage for no reason, a hand's length of dull grey, and put it away, and brought it out. Tullus had sent three new guards down with the new number, because a prefect who raises a quota knows what a road looks like a week later. Vetch had decided in his first week that the way to make a road give twelve was to be what the road was afraid of.

He had done Hollen on the second night. Not the post. Before it. He had come into the seventh chamber at the end of shift with the bucket showing eleven, put the fang against Hollen's throat, and said, one more, cutter. Hollen had cut one more in a quarter of an hour with a fang at his neck. Vetch had laughed and gone up the road. Bricks had not had to choose that night. The road understood what that meant. Vetch had found a way to make twelve.

On the ninth night he found Pell.

Jonah was there because he had stayed. The chamber had emptied at the end of shift, Corr and Bricks and the rest gone to the road for the bowl. Pell had not gone, because Pell had made nothing that day. Not a shard, not a piece. He stood at the wall with the chisel in both hands and the point on the dark, not pushing. Jonah had stayed because the haul did not start until the second bell, and because he had thought, I'll cut it for him. One shard. Fast. Nobody's here.

He had the chisel on the wall and the Vein opening for it when the lamp came into the chamber behind them.

"Two of you," Vetch said. "And one shard between you. Which one's the shard?"

Jonah took the steel off the wall slowly and let his arms shake, and turned. Vetch stood in the mouth of the chamber with the lamp in his left hand. Plate on both forearms, grey. Fang out on the right. He was smiling. He had never in his life been told no by anything.

"Cut one," Vetch said. "The one the Vein wants. I've heard about you. Sweats and shakes and then can't. Corr says you're going." The lamp swung to Pell. "And the old one. Marl. Made nothing today, foreman says. Nothing. In a decury that owes twelve." He came into the chamber. "So I thought I'd come down and help. Like the north-side cutter. He made twelve with a little help. Didn't he."

"He did," he said.

"So." Vetch stopped in front of Pell. He put the fang under the old man's chin and lifted. Pell's head came up off the wall and his eyes came round, slowly, to the young guard's face. "One shard, Marl. In a quarter hour. Or I take the difference out of the count myself, and it's not the post."

What he wanted to say was that he could put the fang hand on the floor before Vetch finished the sentence. What he said instead tried to end this without anyone's hands leaving the ends of their arms.

"He's decury six's best count when his hands aren't shaking," Jonah said. "If he goes down tonight, the number goes down with him. That's on your report, not mine."

Vetch looked at him for the first time since the lamp had come into the chamber, and decided Jonah was not worth the extra sentence. "Then he'd better not shake." The fang did not move.

Pell looked at him.

"Nothing personal," the old man said. It was Corr's line. Pell had picked it up. "You'll do what you do."

"That's right."

Pell put the chisel to the wall.

He tried. He put the steel on the dark and pushed with both hands and all sixty years. The wall did not open for him. It never had. The steel scored the surface and skidded. His hands slipped, and the fang at his chin drew a line. He made a sound, and pushed again.

"Quarter hour," Vetch said, pleasantly, and looked at Jonah. "You're not cutting."

"I'm watching."

"Watch, then. Faster, Marl."

Jonah did the arithmetic. He would tell the old man later that he had, and the old man would nod, because that was what made it different from Hask and from Varro. He had time, and he used it, in the old tongue.

Tiro. Eight, maybe. Grey plate, thin; it comes off him when he sleeps. Weight on the front foot. Lamp in the left hand, fang at Pell's chin with the right. Right hip toward me. There is a seam there, where the thigh plate meets the side; there is always a seam there. The old man showed it to me with the stick forty times.

If I do this, he does not go up the road. Tullus counts guards. There will be a search. There will be Sabinus, whom I have not seen and the old man has. There is nowhere in this rock to put a Blank in garrison grey.

There is one place.

Pell counted four for me. Pell pushed his bowl across the rock. Pell is going to make one shard in a quarter hour or die with a fang under his chin in front of the only man in the Pit who could stop it. And I am standing here doing arithmetic.

The old man will be angry.

The arithmetic said no. Then it stopped mattering, because the quarter hour was not going to come. Vetch was not the kind who waited. The fang had already moved from under Pell's chin to the side of his neck.

Jonah crossed three paces of rock and put the chisel into the seam at Vetch's hip.

---

It went in.

Not easily. Plate was plate, even grey, even thin. The seam was a finger wide and the chisel was a finger wide. His hand knew where the seam was and did not miss. It went past the plate and into the man. Vetch made a sound and turned with the fang, fast, and Jonah was not there.

He had stepped right.

Not left. Not the Fulvian step off the line. Right, and back, the ugly step the old man had put in his legs over ninety nights. The step that was nobody's. Vetch's fang went through the place where a decurion would have been. Jonah pulled the chisel out of the hip as he went past and put it in again, higher, at the armpit seam. Vetch dropped the lamp.

It rolled on the rock and threw its light up the breathing wall. Vetch's face in it was not surprised. Only angry, the anger of a man whose thing has stopped working. He came again, low, fang at Jonah's belly, the same line the cadet had used in the sand.

Jonah let it come. He turned his hip and let the grey fang go past close enough to feel. He took the wrist in both hands and did not turn it. Never turn it, the old man had said; it is a Vibian turn, Auger has seen it, and you are done with what is not yours. He pulled instead. He pulled the arm straight and stepped through. Vetch's own weight took him past Jonah's hip and onto the rock, face down, fang arm stretched under Jonah's boot. The chisel came down to the back of his neck, to the seam where the skull plate met the spine.

Jonah held it there.

Vetch was breathing. The plate was still on him and the fang was still out. He said into the rock, thickly, "You can't."

"I've been told."

"You're a Collar. A cut one. You can't. It's not." His voice went up. "It's not a thing that."

Jonah pushed.

---

The plate came off him in a shiver, all at once, back into the line on his arm. Underneath was a boy of twenty-two in a grey coat, face down on a rock. Jonah stood over him with a wet chisel and breathed.

Pell had not moved. He stood at the wall with the chisel in both hands and a line of blood under his chin, looking at the guard on the rock, and then at Jonah.

"Five," Pell said.

"Yes."

"That's a guard."

"Yes."

"They'll count him."

"Yes." Jonah crouched and put his hand on Vetch's arm, on the line. The genius had come up in the first second and had not been told no this time. It went down through his palm into the boy's arm and found the cutting there.

It was small. Grey, thin, stupid. Two years in Vetch. It had watched its man die with a chisel in his neck and it did not understand. It went into Jonah in pieces, hold, strike, obey, faster than Varro's, because there was so much less of it and because the genius had learned in the hold how to take a thing apart without tasting every piece. Twenty counts. Jonah felt it go down and be folded, and felt the coat get bigger by a size he could almost name. The fever started, warm, from the middle. He did not count it.

Something came with it. It always did. A way of standing with the weight forward. A way of holding a lamp in the left hand so the right was free. And under those, faint, the feeling of walking into a chamber with a fang out and two men in it and being, for the first time in your life, what the room was afraid of.

He knew that one. He had it already, from a hold, and it had liked the sand. He put the new one in with it and did not let it look through his eyes.

"Help me," he said.

"With what?"

"He's going in the wall."

Pell looked at the cut place. The size of a table, wet, moving, where decury six had taken eleven and a half shards that day and the wall had already begun to close.

"It takes them," the old man said slowly. "In the Cradle. When they're done. Ulli says. It takes them in."

"It takes what it wants. It wanted me. It'll want him. He's got a hole in his arm where a piece of it was."

"He's a guard."

"He's meat, Pell. The wall doesn't know what a coat is."

They carried him. He was not heavy. Jonah put the boy's arm against the cut place, the arm with the line, the way he had once put his own hand in a box. The wall was warm, and it did what it did. Not fast. Not the mouthful; that had been the genius, hungry, taking. This was the wall, kind, wanting. It took Vetch the way it took the sick in the Cradle, from the arm in. Jonah watched with Pell beside him and did not look away. You do not look away from a thing you have done, the old man had said. That is the whole of what separates you from a Stripe.

By the second bell there was a grey coat on the rock, a lamp, a chisel, and nothing else.

He put the coat in the bucket under a shard. He took the lamp. He picked up Vetch's chisel, garrison steel, a good one, and gave it to Pell.

"Cut with that tomorrow. It's sharper."

Pell took it. He turned it over once, testing the balance, the way he might have tested a scythe forty years ago on a field that no longer existed.

"Better than your usual," Pell said.

"Everything's better than my usual," Jonah said.

Pell made a sound that started as a laugh and came out as a cough.

"Five," the old man said again, to the floor. Then, after a moment: "He'd have done it. In a quarter hour. He wasn't waiting. I've seen the ones that wait, and he wasn't one."

"I know."

"I'm just saying. So it's said."

---

The search came at dawn, and it came down the Throat with Sabinus in it.

Jonah had never seen him. Everybody in the Pit had heard the name and nobody in the Shallows had seen the man, because Sabinus did not come to the Shallows. He came to the Deep when the Deep needed reminding, and to wherever Tullus sent him when a thing had gone wrong. A guard who had not come up the road at the end of shift had gone wrong.

He came down the road with four guards behind him and did not hurry.

He was not big. Jonah had built him big, from the name. He was a man of middle height with a narrow face and grey at the temples and a coat that fitted. The stripe on his forearm was narrow and old, the purple gone almost brown. Above it and below it, where a stripe was not, were two thin white lines. Scars. Jonah looked at them and did not know what they were. The genius looked at them and went very still.

Sabinus walked the road and looked at every man on it. Not the way Auger looked, or Decimus, or the Flamen with the glass. He looked with an opinion already formed about where the weight was, and the opinion was that none of it mattered. He was bored. He had been sent down a road to look at sixty Collars for a missing Blank, and he had done it before, and it was beneath him.

He stopped at decury six. He looked at Bricks's hand. He looked at Hollen's back. He looked at the line of blood under Pell's chin, and at the garrison chisel in Pell's farmer's grip. Pell looked at the floor. Sabinus looked at Jonah.

"That one," he said, to the guard behind him. Not a question.

"Four four seven one, Centurion. Cut one. The Vein's got him. Been going since winter, the foreman says. Sweats, shakes."

What he did not say to Sabinus was the only true sentence in the chamber: that the guard the centurion had come looking for was already in the wall, and that Jonah had put him there. What he let Sabinus see instead was a dying Collar shaking on cue.

Sabinus looked at Jonah's face. Jonah let it shake. He let the fever from the night show, which was not hard, and let his eyes go to the floor as Pell's had. He held the genius as still as he had ever held anything. Sabinus looked at a dying Collar with a white line on his arm and found nothing in it that was not beneath him.

"He'll be in the Cradle by summer," Sabinus said. "Waste of a count."

He went on down the road.

They found the coat in the bucket at the cage, under the shard. Jonah had meant them to. A guard who walked off the road into the Deep and was taken by the wall was a thing that happened, the old ones said, once or twice in a decade. A young one who went where he was not put and touched what he should not. Sabinus looked at the coat for a long time.

"Fool," he said, and handed it to the guard behind him, and went up the cage. The road breathed.

Decimus came down at the dusk count. He said the numbers. He came to Jonah and said four four seven one, and Jonah said it back. The old guard looked at his face, at the shake in his hands, at the line under Pell's chin, at the garrison chisel in Pell's grip.

"Twelve," Decimus said.

"Twelve."

"Vetch is gone."

"So I heard."

"Went into the Deep. Touched the wall. Young. They do." He made a mark on his list. He did not look up. "There'll be a new one down tomorrow with the new count. Tullus doesn't stop for one. It'll be personal to somebody, I expect. It always is." He went on down the road.

---

The Censor did not beat him that night. He sat across from him with the stick on his knees and heard the whole of it, in the old tongue, from the chamber to the coat. Then he said, in the common speech, which he saved now for things that mattered:

"That is one."

"One what?"

"One thing you have done in this rock that they would recognise. Everything else, the bowl, the haul, the shard, is a Collar being strange. They have a word for that, and the word is dying. This is not that. This is a man who saw a seam and put a chisel in it, in the dark, without a decurion in his legs, because there was an old man with a fang at his throat. The arithmetic said no and he did it anyway." The pale eyes did not move. "That is the Hand. You have it. I did not give it to you. I only took away what was in the way."

"You said I'd die in the first year without you."

"You would have. You would have done it left and forward. Sabinus would have looked at the coat and then looked at you, and he would not have been bored." A pause. "He was bored?"

"He said waste of a count."

"Good. Hold onto that. It is the most valuable thing anyone has said about you in your life, and he will not say it twice." The Censor picked up the stick. "Now the other thing. Twelve. The wall gives ten. You have a foreman with one hand. You have an old man with a garrison chisel who will make two shards a day if the Vein is kind, and it is not. You have a guard coming down tomorrow who is Vetch with a different face. You have made one thing they would recognise. Now make the other."

"Which?"

"Count. You have been counting men on this road since the day I told you to. Bricks. Decimus. Now count the ones above the road. The prefect is a number. His deputy is a number. Who owes. Who fears. Where is the gap." He stood. "When you find it, go and stand in it. Say what you have to say in the right tongue. Not this one. The one they use. You have it now. Use it."

---

The gap was the deputy prefect. Jonah found him in four days.

The deputy was a Thinstripe called Rennick. He ran the cage count, and he was afraid of Tullus. That was the whole of him. Forty years old, a narrow stripe he had done nothing to earn but be born a client of the right house, posted to Metalla Seven to count shards in a rock. Above him a prefect who had never made a licence and hated every man who had one. Every dusk Rennick took the count up the Throat and stood while Tullus read it. Every dusk since the twelve, the count had said eleven, eleven, eleven and a half. Every dusk Tullus had looked at him.

Jonah stood in the road at the cage on the fourth day, with the bucket, when Rennick came down for the count. He spoke in the old tongue, in the register the quaestor's clerks used. The register of a man about to make somebody's number look better.

"Deputy. A word about the count."

Rennick looked at him. He had never in his life been addressed in the old tongue by a man with a shackle on his ankle.

"The Shallows gives ten. That's the wall's number. It was the wall's number twenty years ago and it's the wall's number now. Every decury on this road is making eleven by flogging the men who make nine. It'll be ten again by summer whatever the prefect says. You'll carry ten up the Throat every dusk and stand while he reads it." He kept his eyes on the deputy's stripe, not his face, the way a clerk looked at a Stripe. "But the Deep gives slabs. Not shards. A slab is six shards' weight, and it goes in the bucket as one, because the count is by the piece and nobody ever thought about it. Count by weight. One slab is six. The Deep's decuries make twenty a day by weight and the Shallows makes ten. The average across the road is fifteen. Fifteen is what you carry up the Throat. It's true, and it's more than twelve, and the prefect stops looking at you."

Rennick stood very still.

"The prefect counts pieces," he said.

"The prefect counts what's on the slate you hand him. Hand him weight. Tell him the old count was a Collar count, by the piece. Tell him the College counts by weight and you've corrected it." Jonah looked, for the first time, at the deputy's face. "He doesn't know how the College counts. Neither do I. But he'll believe a man with a stripe who says so in the right voice. You've got the stripe, and you've had the voice since you were five."

The deputy prefect looked at him for a long time.

"Who are you?"

"Four four seven one. Cut one. The Vein's got me, ask Corr." Jonah let his hands shake, once. "I was a purser's pilot, before. I sat behind a man who counted things for six years. It's not hard. It's just that nobody down here ever had a reason to."

Rennick took the count up the Throat that dusk by weight. Tullus read fifteen, and looked at him, and said nothing. The next morning Decimus read the count and then read something else off the list.

"Twelve stands. But it's by weight now. Deputy's order. Slab's six." He did not look up. "And decury six has a new foreman, because Bricks has one hand and the deputy wants a man who can count. Four four seven one. Foreman."

The road looked at him.

Sixty men, and Auger at the edge of the light with his arms folded. Bricks, who had been foreman eleven years and had said nothing when the deputy told him, said nothing now. Corr. Hollen with his back still striped. Pell with a garrison chisel. All of them looking at the cut one who was dying and had been made foreman.

"Cut a shard," he said to them. "Don't make me guess."

---

Decury six made eleven the next day. By weight it was fourteen. The count was made, and nobody chose a man for the post.

The day after, the Vein gave nine, because the wall gave what it gave. The Shallows did not cut slabs, so by weight nine was nine. At the dusk count Decimus came down the road with his list. He stopped at decury six and said, "Foreman," and waited.

Jonah stood in the road.

He had known. He had done the arithmetic on the shelf the night before, and it came out to this. A wall that gave what it gave. A deputy who would carry fifteen up the Throat as long as the Deep held. A decury that would still, some days, make nine. A foreman who chose a man. He had stood in the gap and said the thing in the right tongue, and it had worked, and this was the price.

He looked at them. Bricks, one hand. Hollen, back still striped. Pell, sixty, the garrison chisel. Corr.

"Corr," he said.

Corr looked up. He had been looking at the floor; he had known too. He looked at Jonah for a moment with the wall's dark on his face. Then he nodded, once, and walked to the post at the cage end of the road and put his hands on it. Decimus handed Jonah the strap.

It was leather, and it had been on a great many backs. It was heavier than it looked. He stood behind Corr, who had said a month to him on the first day, and had stopped saying it, and had said, I'm sorry, I like you, you don't talk. He lifted the strap.

"Ten," Decimus said.

He gave him ten. Not slowly and not soft. If you do this, do it, the old man had said. A Stripe would do it soft and let the road see he was soft, and you are done with what a Stripe would do. He gave them hard, and counted them out loud in the common speech, because that was what the road spoke. Corr made a sound on the seventh and none on the rest. On the tenth Jonah put the strap in Decimus's hand and walked back to his place at the wall.

Corr came off the post. He walked back down the road past sixty men and sat down at his place beside Jonah's with his back to the rock, carefully, and picked up his bowl. After a while he said, to the floor, not unkindly:

"Nothing personal."

"No."

"Twelve's a war, cut one. That's all it is. You didn't make it."

"I know."

"Just so it's said."

Jonah sat beside him with the strap's weight still in his hand and last night's fever still in his teeth. The genius lay behind his heart, fed, folded, a young guard's way of holding a lamp put away where it could not look through his eyes. That was the other thing. That was the second thing they would recognise. He had made it.

Auger, at the edge of the light, unfolded his arms and looked at him for a long moment. He folded them again and said nothing. The lamp at the end of the road did not go out.
