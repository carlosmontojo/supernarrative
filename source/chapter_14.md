# Chapter 14: Quota

The quota went up in the spring of the first year, and it went up because of a war that nobody in the Pit had heard of, and Jonah learned about the war from a guard he killed.

That was how things came down the Throat. Not as news. As weight. One morning Decimus read the count and then read something else off the list, in the voice for things that were not personal, and the something else was twelve.

"Twelve," Bricks said. He said it the way he said everything, and then he said it again, which he never did. "Twelve."

"Twelve a day. Every decury. From today." Decimus did not look up from the list. "Prefect's order. I don't know why and I wouldn't tell you if I did. Twelve."

"We've never made twelve. Nobody's ever made twelve. Ten's the wall's number. Ten's what it gives."

"Then it'll have to give twelve," the old guard said, "or I'll be coming down the road at dusk to watch you choose," and went on down the road saying numbers, and decury six stood in the seventh chamber and looked at the dark breathing wall, and Corr said, quietly, to nobody, "That's a war, that is. Twelve. That's plate for a legion."

Nobody asked him how he knew. Corr had been on the wall four years. He knew what the wall's numbers meant the way Pell knew what a field's did.

---

They made eleven the first day and Bricks chose Hollen for the post and Hollen took it without a sound, ten across the back at the dusk count with the road watching, and the second day they made eleven again and Bricks chose Pell.

Jonah watched the foreman's face when he did it. Bricks had not wanted to. There was nothing in the wide face that wanted to. But the foreman of a decury that missed chose a man, and if he chose the same man twice the road would say he had a favourite, and if he chose the strong ones the decury would go from eleven to nine, and Pell was sixty and cut like a farmer and had made one shard in two days, and everybody could count.

Pell took it the way he took everything. To the floor. Ten strokes. He got up after and went to the wall and picked up his chisel and put it to the dark and his hands were shaking so badly that the steel rang on the Vein like a bell, and Jonah, beside him, said, "Give it here," and Pell said, "No," and cut.

The third day they made eleven and a half. Jonah made the half. He'd cut his one shard slowly, shaking, the way he did, and then he'd cut another, not slowly, and broken it in two on the rock so that it went into the bucket as a shard and a piece, and Bricks had looked at the piece and at him and said nothing and counted it.

It was not enough. The wall gave ten. The Censor said it, that night, in the Cradle, in the old tongue, and Jonah understood every word.

"It gives ten because ten is what the Shallows grows back in a day. I did the arithmetic on this rock twenty-two years ago and I did it again when they built the galleries and I told the man who ordered them that ten was the number, and he said, then ten, and now a prefect who has never done a sum in his life says twelve, because a legate on the far Limes has said he needs plate for a legion by autumn, and the Shallows will give twelve, boy, it will give twelve for a season and then it will give eight for ten years, and every man on that road will be flogged for the difference." The old man's face in the lamp was very still. "That is what a quota is. It is a man in the City deciding that a wall will grow faster if you hit the men in front of it."

"Then what do I do?"

"You cut your one shard slowly and you shake and you take your turn at the post when it comes, because it will come, and you do not die of it. That is what you do." The Censor picked up the stick. "The rest is not tonight."

---

The rest was the ninth night, and it was a guard called Vetch.

He was new. Jonah had seen him twice: a Blank in the garrison grey, young, with a line on his arm and a plate on his forearms that he did deploy on purpose, all the time, so that everyone could see it, and a fang that he brought out at the cage for no reason, a hand's length, dull grey, and put away, and brought out. He had come down with the twelve. Tullus had sent three new guards down the Throat with the new number, because a prefect who raises a quota knows what a road looks like a week later, and Vetch was one of them, and Vetch had decided in his first week that the way to make a road give twelve was to be a thing the road was afraid of.

He'd flogged Hollen himself, on the second night. Not the post. Before it. He'd come into the seventh chamber at the end of shift with the bucket showing eleven and had put the fang against Hollen's throat and said, one more, cutter, and Hollen had cut one more, in a quarter of an hour, with a fang at his neck, and had made twelve, and Vetch had laughed and gone up the road, and Bricks had not had to choose that night, and the road had understood what that meant, which was that Vetch had found a way to make twelve.

On the ninth night he found Pell.

Jonah was there because he'd stayed. It was the end of shift and the chamber had emptied, Corr and Bricks and the rest gone to the road for the bowl, and Pell had not gone, because Pell had made nothing that day, not a shard, not a piece, and was standing at the wall with the chisel in both hands and the point on the dark, not pushing, just standing, the way a man stands at a thing he cannot do. And Jonah had stayed, because the haul did not start until the second bell and because he had thought, I'll cut it for him. One shard. Fast. Nobody's here.

He had the chisel on the wall and the Vein opening for it like a door and the thing under his ribs leaning, when the lamp came into the chamber behind them.

"Two of you," Vetch said. "And one shard between you. Which one's the shard?"

Jonah stopped cutting.

He did it slowly. He took the steel off the wall and let his arms shake, and turned, and Vetch was in the mouth of the chamber with the lamp in one hand and the other hand bare, and the plate was on both his forearms, grey, and the fang was out on the right, and he was smiling, and he was twenty-two, perhaps, and he had never in his life been told no by anything.

"Cut one," Vetch said. "The one the Vein wants. I've heard about you. Sweats and shakes and cuts like a foreman and then can't. Corr says you're going." The lamp swung to Pell. "And the old one. Marl. Made nothing today, foreman says. Nothing. In a decury that owes twelve." He came into the chamber. "So I thought, I'll come down and help. Like with the north-side cutter. He made twelve with a little help. Didn't he."

"He did," Jonah said.

"So." Vetch stopped in front of Pell. He put the fang under the old man's chin, the way he had with Hollen, and lifted, so that Pell's head came up off the wall and his farmer's eyes came round, slowly, from the floor to the young guard's face, and stayed there. "One shard, Marl. In a quarter hour. Or I take the difference out of the count myself, and it's not the post."

Pell looked at him.

"Nothing personal," the old man said. It was Corr's line. Pell had picked it up. "You'll do what you do."

"That's right."

Pell put the chisel to the wall.

He tried. Jonah watched him try. He put the steel on the dark and pushed with both hands and his whole sixty years, and the wall did not open for him the way it opened for Jonah, it had never opened for him, he had made one shard in two days on a wall that gave ten, and the steel scored the surface and skidded and Pell's hands slipped and the fang at his chin drew a line, and he made a sound, and pushed again.

"Quarter hour," Vetch said, pleasantly, and looked at Jonah. "You're not cutting."

"I'm watching."

"Watch, then." The guard turned back to Pell. "Faster, Marl."

And Jonah thought about it.

He would say that, later, when he told it, and the old man would nod, because it was the thing that made it different from Hask and from Varro. He had time. He stood in the seventh chamber with a chisel in his hands and a young Blank with a fang at an old man's throat three paces away, and he thought about it, clearly, in the old tongue, because that was the tongue he thought in now when he needed to think slowly.

He thought: he is Tiro. Eight, maybe. The plate is grey and thin and it comes off him when he sleeps. He is standing with his weight on the front foot and the lamp in his left hand and the fang at Pell's chin with his right, and his hip is toward me, the right hip, and there is a seam there, where the plate on his thigh meets the plate on his side, because there is always a seam there, the old man showed it to me with the stick forty times.

He thought: if I do this, he does not go up the road. A guard does not go up the road and Tullus counts guards the way Decimus counts us. There will be a search. There will be Sabinus, whom I have not seen and the old man has, and there will be men with glass, maybe, and there is nowhere in this rock to put a Blank in the garrison grey.

He thought: there is one place.

He thought: Pell counted four for me. Pell pushed his bowl across the rock. Pell is going to make one shard in a quarter hour or he is going to die with a fang under his chin in front of the only man in the Pit who could stop it, and I am the only man in the Pit who could stop it, and I am standing here doing arithmetic.

He thought: the old man will be angry.

Then he stopped thinking, because the quarter hour was not going to come, Vetch was not the kind who waited for it, the fang had already gone from under Pell's chin to the side of his neck, and Jonah crossed three paces of rock with a chisel in his right hand and put it into the seam at Vetch's hip.

---

It went in.

Not easily. Not the way the fang had gone into Varro or the steel into Hask. Plate was plate, even grey, even thin; the seam was a finger wide and the chisel was a finger wide and it went in because his hand knew where the seam was and did not miss, and it went in past the plate and into the man, and Vetch made a sound and turned, with the fang, fast, and Jonah was not there.

He'd stepped right.

Not left. Not the Fulvian step off the line. Right, and back, the ugly step the old man had put in his legs with a stick over ninety nights, the step that was nobody's, and Vetch's fang went through the place where a decurion would have been and Jonah took the chisel out of the guard's hip as he went past and put it in again, higher, in the seam at the armpit, and Vetch dropped the lamp.

It did not go out. It rolled on the rock and threw its light up the wall and across the dark breathing surface of the Vein, and in the light Jonah saw the young guard's face, and it was not surprised. That was the thing he would keep. Varro had been surprised. Hask had looked at something, finally. Vetch's face was only angry, the anger of a man whose thing has stopped working, and he came again with the fang, low, at Jonah's belly, exactly the way the cadet had come in the sand, and Jonah did not need Varro's feet for that one. He'd had that one taught to him properly.

He let it come. He turned his hip and let the grey fang go past it close enough to feel, and took the wrist, both hands, and did not turn it. The old man had said never turn it, it is a Vibian turn and Auger has seen it and you are done with things that are not yours. He did the other thing. He pulled. He pulled the arm straight and stepped through, the way you'd step through a door, and the young guard's own weight took him past Jonah's hip and onto the rock, face down, with his fang arm stretched out under Jonah's boot and the chisel, when Jonah brought it down, already at the back of his neck where the plate on his skull met the plate on his spine.

He held it there.

Vetch was breathing. The plate was still on him, grey, thin, and the fang was still out, and he was on his face on the rock of the seventh chamber with a chisel in the seam at the base of his skull, and he said, into the rock, thickly, "You can't."

"I've been told."

"You're a Collar. A cut one. You can't. It's not." The young man's voice went up. "It's not a thing that."

Jonah pushed.

---

The plate came off him the way it had come off Varro, in a shiver, all at once, back into the line on his arm, and underneath there was a boy of twenty-two in a grey coat on his face on a rock, and Jonah stood over him with a wet chisel and breathed.

Pell had not moved.

The old man was standing where he'd been, at the wall, with the chisel in both hands and a line of blood under his chin, and he was looking at the guard on the rock, and then at Jonah, and his farmer's face was doing nothing at all.

"Five," Pell said.

"Yes."

"That's a guard."

"Yes."

"They'll count him."

"Yes." Jonah crouched. He put his hand on Vetch's arm, on the line, and the thing under his ribs, which had come up in the first second and had been told nothing this time, had not been told no, came down through his palm the way it had in the Kestrel's hold and went into the boy's arm and found the cutting there.

It was small. It was grey and thin and stupid and it had been in Vetch two years and it had watched its man die with a chisel in his neck and it did not understand, and it went into Jonah in pieces, hold, strike, obey, fast, faster than Varro's, because there was so much less of it and because the thing under his ribs had learned since the hold how to take a thing apart without tasting every piece. It was done in a count of twenty. Jonah felt it go down and be folded, and felt the coat get bigger by a size he could name now, almost, and felt the fever start, warm, from the middle, and did not count it.

And something came with it. It always did. He'd known it would. A way of standing with the weight forward. A way of holding a lamp in the left hand so the right was free. And under those, faint, folded in, the feeling of walking into a chamber with a fang out and two men in it and being, for the first time in your life, the thing the room was afraid of.

He knew that one. He had it already, from a hold, and it had liked the sand. He put the new one in with it and did not let it look through his eyes.

"Help me," he said to Pell.

"With what?"

"He's going in the wall."

Pell looked at him. Then he looked at the Vein, at the cut place, the size of a table, wet, moving, where decury six had taken eleven and a half shards that day and the wall had already begun, the way it did, to close.

"It takes them," the old man said slowly. "In the Cradle. When they're done. Ulli says. It takes them in."

"It takes what it wants. It wanted me. It'll want him. He's got a hole in his arm now where a piece of it was."

"He's a guard."

"He's meat, Pell. The wall doesn't know what a coat is."

They carried him. He was not heavy. They carried him to the cut place and Jonah put the boy's arm against it, the arm with the line, the way he'd put his own hand in a box, and the wall was warm, and it did what it did. It was not fast. It was not the mouthful; that had been the thing under his ribs, hungry, taking. This was the wall, kind, wanting, and it took Vetch the way it took the sick in the Cradle, slowly, from the arm in, and Jonah stood and watched it happen with Pell beside him and did not look away, because the old man had said you do not look away from a thing you have done, that is the whole of what separates you from a Stripe, and by the time the second bell rang for the haul there was a grey coat on the rock and a lamp and a chisel and nothing else.

He put the coat in the bucket under a shard. He put the lamp in his own hand. He picked up Vetch's chisel, which was a good one, garrison steel, and gave it to Pell.

"Cut with that tomorrow. It's sharper."

Pell took it.

"Five," the old man said again, to the floor, and then, after a moment, in the voice he used for things he had seen before, "He'd have done it. In a quarter hour. He wasn't waiting. I've seen the ones that wait and he wasn't one."

"I know."

"I'm just saying. So it's said."

---

The search came at dawn and it came down the Throat with Sabinus in it.

Jonah had not seen him. Everybody in the Pit had heard the name and nobody in the Shallows had seen him, because Sabinus did not come to the Shallows; Sabinus came to the Deep when the Deep needed reminding, and to the surface, and to wherever Tullus sent him when a thing had gone wrong, and a guard who had not come up the road at the end of shift was a thing that had gone wrong.

He came down the road with four guards behind him and did not hurry.

He was not big. That was the first thing. Jonah had built him big, from the name, the way you build a thing from a name, and he was a man of middle height with a narrow face and grey at the temples and a coat that fitted, and the stripe on his forearm was narrow and old, worn, the purple gone almost brown, and on either side of it, above and below, where a stripe was not, there were two thin white lines. Scars. Old ones. Jonah looked at them and did not know what they were and the thing under his ribs looked at them and went very still.

Sabinus walked the road. He looked at every man on it. He did not look at them the way Auger did, or Decimus, or the Flamen with the glass. He looked at them the way Jonah had looked at Hask across the transport hold, with an opinion already formed about where the weight was, and the opinion was that none of it mattered, and he was, Jonah understood, in the same moment, bored. He was a man who had been sent down a road to look at sixty Collars for a missing Blank, and he was doing it, and he had done it before, and it was beneath him.

He stopped at decury six. He looked at Bricks's hand. He looked at Hollen's back. He looked at the line of blood under Pell's chin and at Pell's new chisel, garrison steel, and Pell held it the way a farmer holds a tool and looked at the floor, and Sabinus looked at Jonah.

"That one," he said, to the guard behind him. Not a question.

"Four four seven one, Centurion. Cut one. The Vein's got him. Been going since winter, the foreman says. Sweats, shakes."

Sabinus looked at Jonah's face for a moment. Jonah let it shake. He let the fever from the night show, which was not hard, and let his eyes go to the floor the way Pell's did, and held the thing under his ribs as still as he had ever held anything, and Sabinus looked at a dying Collar with a white line on his arm and found nothing in it that was not beneath him.

"He'll be in the Cradle by summer," Sabinus said. "Waste of a count." And went on down the road.

They found the coat in the bucket at the cage, under the shard. Jonah had meant them to. A guard who had walked off the road into the Deep and been taken by the wall was a thing that happened, the old ones said, once or twice in a decade, a young one who went where he was not put and touched what he should not, and a coat in a bucket said that better than nothing would have. Sabinus looked at the coat for a long time. Then he said, "Fool," and handed it to the guard behind him, and went up the cage, and the road breathed.

Decimus came down at the dusk count.

He said the numbers. He came to Jonah and said four four seven one and Jonah said it back, and the old guard looked at him, at his face, at the shake in his hands, at the line under Pell's chin beside him, and at the garrison chisel in Pell's farmer's grip, for a long moment, in the way that had waited in the tunnel.

"Twelve," Decimus said.

"Twelve."

"Vetch is gone."

"So I heard."

"Went into the Deep. Touched the wall. Young. They do." The old guard made a mark on his list. He did not look up. "There'll be a new one down tomorrow with the new count. Tullus doesn't stop for one. It'll be personal to somebody, I expect. It always is." He went on down the road.

---

The Censor did not beat him, that night. He sat across from him in the lamp with the stick on his knees and heard the whole of it, in the old tongue, from the chamber to the coat, and did not say anything until it was done, and then he said, in the common speech, which he saved now for things that mattered:

"That is one."

"One what?"

"One thing you have done in this rock that they would recognise. Everything else, the bowl, the haul, the shard, is a Collar being strange, and they have a word for that and the word is dying. This is not that. This is a man who saw a seam and put a chisel in it, in the dark, without a decurion in his legs, because there was an old man with a fang at his throat and he had done the arithmetic and the arithmetic said no and he did it anyway." The pale eyes did not move. "That is the Hand. You have it. I did not give it to you. I only took away what was in the way."

"You said I'd die in the first year without you."

"You would have. You would have done it left and forward, and Sabinus would have looked at the coat and then looked at you, and he would not have been bored." The old man was quiet a moment. "He was bored?"

"He said waste of a count."

"Good. Hold onto that. It is the most valuable thing anyone has said about you in your life, and he will not say it twice." The Censor picked up the stick. "And now the other thing. Twelve. The wall gives ten. You have a foreman with one hand and an old man with a garrison chisel who will make two shards a day with it if the Vein is kind, and it is not, and a guard coming down tomorrow who is Vetch with a different face. You have made one thing they would recognise. Now make the other."

"Which?"

"Count," the old man said. "You have been counting men on this road since the day I told you to. Bricks. Decimus. Now count the ones above the road. The prefect is a number. His deputy is a number. Who owes. Who fears. Where is the gap." He stood. "And when you find it, go and stand in it, and say what you have to say in the right tongue. Not this one. The one they use. You have it now. Use it."

---

The gap was the deputy prefect, and Jonah found him in four days, and it cost him what the old man had said it would.

The deputy was a Thinstripe called Rennick, and he ran the cage count, and he was afraid of Tullus. That was the whole of him. He was a man of forty with a narrow stripe he had done nothing to earn but be born a client of the right house, and he had been posted to Metalla Seven to count shards in a rock, and he had a prefect above him who had never made a licence and hated every man who had one, and every dusk Rennick took the count up the Throat to Tullus and stood while the prefect read it, and every dusk since the twelve the count had said eleven, eleven, eleven and a half, and every dusk Tullus had looked at him.

Jonah stood in the road at the cage on the fourth day, with the bucket, when Rennick came down for the count, and said, in the old tongue, in the register that the quaestor's clerks used, which was the register of a man who is about to make somebody's number look better:

"Deputy. A word about the count."

Rennick looked at him. He had never in his life been addressed in the old tongue by a thing with a shackle on its ankle.

"The Shallows gives ten," Jonah said. "That's the wall's number. It was the wall's number twenty years ago and it's the wall's number now, and every decury on this road is making eleven by flogging the men who make nine, and it's going to be ten again by summer whatever the prefect says, and you're going to carry ten up the Throat every dusk and stand while he reads it." He kept his voice level and his eyes on the deputy's stripe, not his face, the way the old man had said a clerk looked at a Stripe. "But the Deep gives slabs. Not shards. A slab is six shards' weight and it goes in the bucket as one, because the count is by the piece and always has been, because nobody ever thought about it. Count by weight. One slab is six. The Deep's decuries make twenty a day by weight and the Shallows makes ten, and the average across the road is fifteen, and fifteen is what you carry up the Throat, and it's true, and it's more than twelve, and the prefect stops looking at you."

Rennick stood very still.

"The prefect counts pieces," he said.

"The prefect counts what's on the slate you hand him. Hand him weight. Tell him the old count was a Collar count, by the piece, that the College counts by weight, that you've corrected it." Jonah looked, for the first time, at the deputy's face. "He doesn't know how the College counts. Neither do I. But he'll believe a man with a stripe who says so in the right voice, and you've got the stripe, and you've had the voice since you were five."

The deputy prefect looked at him for a long time.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Four four seven one. Cut one. The Vein's got me, ask Corr." Jonah let his hands shake, once. "I was a purser's pilot, before. I sat behind a man who counted things for six years. It's not hard. It's just that nobody down here has ever had a reason to."

Rennick took the count up the Throat that dusk by weight, and Tullus read fifteen, and looked at him, and said nothing, and the next morning Decimus read the count and then read something else off the list.

"Twelve stands," the old guard said. "But it's by weight now. Deputy's order. Slab's six." He did not look up. "And decury six has a new foreman, because Bricks has one hand and the deputy wants a man who can count. Four four seven one. Foreman."

The road looked at him.

He felt it. Sixty men and Auger at the edge of the light with his arms folded, and Bricks, who had been foreman eleven years and had said nothing when the deputy told him and said nothing now, and Corr, and Hollen with his back still striped from the post, and Pell with a garrison chisel, all of them looking at the cut one who was dying and had been made foreman, and he understood what Auger had meant about a rule.

"Cut a shard," he said to them. "Don't make me guess."

---

Decury six made eleven the next day, by the piece, and by weight it was fourteen, and the count was made, and nobody chose a man for the post.

On the day after that the Vein gave nine, because the wall gave what it gave, and by weight nine was nine, because the Shallows did not cut slabs, and the count was short, and at the dusk count Decimus came down the road with his list and stopped at decury six and said, "Foreman," and waited.

Jonah stood in the road.

He had known. He'd done the arithmetic on the shelf the night before and it had come out to this, to a wall that gave what it gave and a deputy who would carry fifteen up the Throat as long as the Deep held and a decury that would still, some days, make nine, and a foreman who chose a man. He had counted the men above the road and stood in the gap and said the thing in the right tongue, and it had worked, and the price of it was standing in the road with sixty men watching while Decimus waited.

He looked at them. Bricks, one hand. Hollen, back still striped. Pell, sixty, the garrison chisel. Corr.

"Corr," he said.

Corr looked up. He'd been looking at the floor; he'd known too. He looked at Jonah for a moment with the wall's dark on his face, and then he nodded, once, and walked to the post at the cage end of the road and put his hands on it, and Decimus handed Jonah the strap.

It was leather. It had been on a great many backs. He took it, and it was heavier than it looked, and he stood behind Corr, who had said a month to him on the first day and had stopped saying it and had said, I'm sorry, I like you, you don't talk, and he lifted the strap.

"Ten," Decimus said.

He gave him ten. He did not give them slowly and he did not give them soft, because the old man had said, if you do this, do it, a Stripe would do it soft and let the road see he was soft, and you are done with what a Stripe would do. He gave them the way a foreman gave them, and counted them out loud in the common speech, because that was what the road spoke, and Corr made a sound on the seventh and none on the rest, and on the tenth Jonah put the strap in Decimus's hand and walked back to his place at the wall and stood there.

Corr came off the post. He walked back down the road past the sixty men and sat down at his place beside Jonah's with his back to the rock, carefully, and picked up his bowl, and after a while he said, to the floor, not unkindly:

"Nothing personal."

"No."

"Twelve's a war, cut one. That's all it is. You didn't make it."

"I know."

"Just so it's said."

Jonah sat beside him with the strap's weight still in his hand and the fever from the night before still in his teeth and the thing under his ribs behind his heart, fed, folded, a young guard's way of holding a lamp put away where it could not look through his eyes, and thought, that's the other thing. That's the thing they'd recognise. And it was, and he had made it, and Auger, at the edge of the light, unfolded his arms and looked at him for a long moment and folded them again and said nothing, and the lamp at the end of the road did not go out.
