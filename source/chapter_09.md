# Chapter 9: Decury

The shackle died on the first night, and Jonah felt it go, and did not sleep.

Decury six slept in a side gallery off the Shallows road, a cut the length of a barge hold with rock shelves along both walls and a lamp at the end that never went out, and the ten of them lay on the shelves under grey blankets that had been on a great many men, and Jonah lay on his with his knees drawn up and his left ankle in both hands.

The shackle was awake. He'd felt it wake at the Throat and it had not gone back to sleep since. It sat on his ankle and looked at his ankle, and every so often, when he shifted, it tightened a fraction, the way a hand tightens on a thing it means to keep, and he understood that it would do that for the rest of his life unless the rest of his life was short.

And the thing under his ribs was looking at it.

Not the way it had looked at Varro. Not hungry. It had not been hungry since the slab. It lay at the bottom of him where it had gone flat, with the raw place on it where the piece had been torn off, and it looked at the shackle on his ankle the way a man with a wound looks at the knife.

"It's a lorica," he said, very quietly, into the blanket. "It's like the collar. It's a stupid one. It only knows one thing."

The thing under his ribs did not care what it knew.

He felt it move. Slowly. Down his left leg, through the muscle, the way it had gone up his arm on the derelict, reading as it went, finding the old torn ligament from the yards and stopping at it, interested, and going on. It reached his ankle. It reached the place where the shackle sat on the skin, and it stopped there, on the inside, and it looked at the shackle from underneath.

The shackle tightened.

It had felt something. It was stupid but it was not blind. It tightened on his ankle the way it would have tightened if he'd run, and the burn started, a heat under the iron, small, a warning, and Jonah put his teeth in the blanket.

Then the thing under his ribs went through the skin, and into the shackle, and ate it.

Not fast. It had been fast with Varro's lorica because Varro's lorica was warm and dying and there was a lot of it. This was small and cold and it did not want to be eaten, and it fought, if a thing that knew one thing could fight, and the fighting was the burn, and the burn went from a warning to a fact and Jonah lay on the rock shelf with a blanket in his teeth and his ankle on fire and did not make a sound, because Pell was on the shelf above him and Corr was across the gallery and the lamp never went out.

It took most of the night. The thing under his ribs went through the shackle the way it had gone through the collar, taking it apart, one small dead want at a time, and there was only one want, and it was hold, and it was very strong, and it came up his leg over and over, hold, hold, hold, and was folded away, and each time it was folded away the burn went down a little, and the raw place at the bottom of him, where the piece had been, felt a little less raw.

Near dawn it was done.

The shackle was still on his ankle. He put his hand on it in the dark. It was iron, or the thing that looked like iron. It was cold. It was a ring of dead metal around his leg that any guard who looked at it would see as a shackle, and it would never tighten again, and it would never burn, and if he ran, nobody would know until they counted.

He lay with his hand on it and felt the thing under his ribs settle back to where it lived, fed, not full, and he thought, that's two. The collar and this. Two of the Empire's hands off me. And then he thought, and nobody can see either one, and that's the whole of what I've got, and he did not know if that was a lot or nothing.

The lamp at the end of the gallery did not go out. Somewhere up the road a bell rang, flat, and Decimus's voice came down the rock saying numbers, and Jonah got up.

---

The Shallows ran on shards.

He learned it the first week, the way he learned everything now, by keeping his mouth shut and his eyes open and getting hit when he forgot. A shard was a piece of the Vein the length of a thumb, cut off the wall with a chisel and dropped in a bucket, and a decury of ten owed ten shards a day to the bucket, and a foreman carried the bucket to the cage at dusk, and a guard counted the shards, and if there were ten the decury ate, and if there were nine the decury ate less, and if there were eight the foreman chose a man and that man was flogged at the count, and if there were seven the foreman chose a man and Decimus did not come down for what happened after.

The foreman of decury six was a Blank called Bricks.

He had been a Blank. He had a white scar on his arm where the line had been and he had been in the Shallows eleven years, which nobody else in the decury could say or could imagine, and he was short and wide and had a chisel he had ground down at one end to a point, and he carried it in his belt the way Hask had carried his steel, and the first thing he said to Jonah, on the road, on the first morning, was, "You're the cut one."

"Four four seven one."

"Corr says the Vein wants you."

"Corr says a month to everyone."

Bricks looked at him. He had small eyes in a wide face and they were not stupid.

"So he does," the foreman said. "Cut a shard today. Cut a shard tomorrow. When you can't cut a shard, tell me, and I'll pick somebody else for the post. Don't make me guess."

That was the whole of Bricks. Jonah would come to think it was the whole of the Shallows. Cut a shard. Don't make me guess.

He cut a shard.

That was the problem. He'd stood at the wall on the first day with a chisel in his hands and the thing under his ribs looking through him at the dark breathing depth of it, and he had pushed, and the wall had let him. Corr had said it, on the third day. Corr had stopped cutting and stood with his own chisel hanging and watched Jonah put steel to the Vein and take a shard off it the length of a hand in the time it took Corr to score a line, and had said, quietly, "It comes off for you."

"It's soft here."

"It's not soft anywhere. I've been on this wall four years. It comes off for you, cut one." Corr had looked at him, close, with the wall's dark on his face. "It does that. Before. When it wants a man. It gets easy for him. He cuts like a foreman for a month, and then he can't lift the chisel, and then he's on the far side with the deep ones." He'd gone back to his own line. "Enjoy it."

Jonah had stood with the shard in his hand and understood that the wall was not being soft. It was being polite. Something on the other side of it had turned to look at him on the first day, and had looked at the thing under his ribs, and whatever it had seen, it had decided that this one could cut. It let the chisel in the way you'd let a hand in that you knew.

Which meant he could have taken twenty shards a day. Thirty. He could have made decury six the richest decury in the Shallows and eaten like a foreman and never seen the post. And every guard on the road would have looked at the bucket and asked how, and Corr would have said, it comes off for him, and one day somebody with a glass would have come down the Throat to see what the Vein liked so much about four four seven one.

So he cut one. Slowly. He stood at the wall with the thing under his ribs going through him and the Vein opening for his chisel like a door, and he took one shard a day off it with his arms shaking, the way a new man's arms shook, and dropped it in the bucket, and Bricks counted ten, and decury six ate.

It was the hardest work he had ever done. Not the cutting. The not.

---

The challenge came on the third night.

He'd known it would. Pell had told him, on the first night, before the shackle, in the flat voice he used for things he'd seen before. There's a law down here, the old man had said, to the shelf above. Not theirs. Ours. Auger's. You eat what you're given unless somebody wants it, and if somebody wants it he says so, and you fight for it, chisels, in the road, with Auger's man watching so it's fair. Nobody dies. Mostly. You lose, you go hungry, and they know you'll go hungry again. You win, they leave you alone for a while. New men get it on the third night. Always the third. It's a thing they do.

"Who's Auger?"

"King of the Shallows," Pell had said, as if it were a field. "Been here longest. Unstripped, like you. Holds the top road and the rations come through him first. He's the reason nobody starves, down here, and the reason nobody gets fat. They say he's fair." He'd been quiet a moment. "They say it the way you say a wall's straight."

On the third night, at the meal, which was a bowl of something grey ladled out of a cauldron at the cage end of the road by a Collar with one eye, Bricks took his bowl and Jonah's and walked into the middle of the road and set Jonah's down on the rock, and said, "Mine."

The road went quiet.

There were perhaps sixty men on it, four decuries eating on the rock with their backs to the walls, and they all stopped, and Jonah stood with his empty hands and looked at his bowl in the middle of the road and at Bricks standing over it with his ground-down chisel in his belt, and thought, of course. Of course it's him. He's the one who has to know.

"You don't want it," he said. "You eat first. You've eaten."

"I want to see what the cut one does when a man takes his food." Bricks had not drawn the chisel. His small eyes were on Jonah's hands. "Corr says you cut like a foreman and then shake like you didn't. Corr says a lot. I want to see. Come and get your bowl."

Somebody moved, at the edge of the light, and Jonah looked, and it was a man he had not seen before. Big, slow, old, with a white scar on a forearm as thick as Jonah's leg and a face that had been broken and set wrong more than once, and a chisel in his hand that he was not holding like a weapon. He was holding it like a thing he'd had for so long he'd forgotten it was there. He leaned against the rock at the edge of the road and folded his arms and looked at Bricks and at Jonah and said nothing, and every man on the road looked at him and then looked away, and Jonah understood who he was.

Auger nodded, once, slightly. At Bricks. Fair.

Jonah walked into the road.

---

He'd thought about it. That was the difference, this time. In the transport hold he'd sat against a wall for a day and a night and argued, and found the argument already over. Here he'd had three nights and he'd used them, lying on the shelf with his dead shackle under his hand, and what he'd decided was this. He was not going to let the feet do it.

Not because they wouldn't win. They would. He could feel them, in his legs, looking at Bricks the way they'd looked at Hask, and the opinion was that Bricks was better than Hask, that Bricks was a man who'd been in a great many fights in a narrow road and knew where a chisel went, and that it did not matter, because a Fulvian step off the line and a wrist-turn would have Bricks on the rock with his own point under his ear in the time it took to say it.

And every man on the road would see a Collar barge pilot do a thing that garrison schools taught. And Auger would see it, and Auger had been a Blank and had been stripped for something and did not look like a man who forgot what he'd seen. And Bricks would be dead, or crippled, and decury six would have no foreman, and the post would find a new one, and the new one would be the man who'd done it.

So he wasn't going to let the feet do it. He was going to do it himself, with what he had, and what he had was six years of yard fights and three weeks in the sand on Founding Day and a dead man's opinion about where Bricks's weight was, which he was going to listen to and not obey.

Bricks drew the chisel.

He came in low. He came in the way Hask had, straight, but he was not Hask; the point came in fast and short, at the belly, and pulled back before it landed, a feint, and his other hand was already coming for Jonah's throat, and it was good. It was eleven years of narrow roads. The feet said left and forward and Jonah said no, and stepped back instead, the way a man steps back, and the grabbing hand missed him by a finger and Bricks's weight came a half step too far and Jonah hit him.

He hit him in the face. With his fist. It was the least clever thing he could have done and Bricks had not expected it, because nobody in the Shallows hit a man in the face, they went for the chisel hand, and the foreman's head went back and the point came round, wild, and opened Jonah's forearm from wrist to elbow.

He felt it go. He felt the thing under his ribs come up at the cut, fast, the way it had come up in his father's room, and felt it start to close the wound from the inside, and he thought, no, not here, and it stopped, and the blood ran.

Bricks came again.

This time Jonah let the feet do one thing. One. Not the step. Not the turn. When the chisel came in he did what Varro had known and what Livia had named and what a garrison school taught in the first month, which was that a man's wrist is a hinge and a hinge only goes one way, and he caught the wrist, not with a turn but with both hands, the way a yard man catches a falling tool, and he bent it back.

Not sideways. Back. Toward the elbow. It was ugly and slow and it took his whole weight and Bricks fought it with eleven years, and the road watched the two of them stand in the middle of it locked together with the blood running off Jonah's arm onto the rock, and then something in the foreman's wrist went, with a sound, and the chisel came out of his fingers and rang on the stone, and Bricks made a noise through his teeth and went down on one knee.

Jonah let go.

He stepped back. He picked up the chisel off the rock, because leaving it was a thing he'd done once and been told nobody did, and he held it, point down, and looked at the foreman on one knee holding his wrist against his chest with his small eyes gone very wide, and he looked at Auger against the rock at the edge of the light with his arms folded, and he looked at his bowl.

He picked it up. It had been in the middle of the road the whole time and nobody had touched it.

He carried it to Bricks. He crouched, with the blood running off his arm, and set the bowl down on the rock in front of the foreman's knee.

"It's yours," he said. "You said mine. I heard you."

The road did not breathe.

Bricks looked at the bowl. He looked at Jonah's face, close, the way Corr had, and his small eyes were not stupid, and they were doing a sum, and the sum was not coming out.

"You broke my hand," he said.

"You cut my arm. Eat your soup."

"Nobody gives it back."

"I've been told." Jonah stood up. His arm hurt. He was glad of it. It was the only thing in him that was doing what it was supposed to. "Somebody's got to carry the bucket tomorrow, and it's not going to be you with that hand, and it's not going to be me, because I'm new and I'm cut and the Vein wants me, Corr says. So pick somebody. Don't make me guess."

He walked back to his place at the wall and sat down with his back to the rock and his bleeding arm in his lap and no bowl, and after a moment Pell, beside him, without looking, pushed his own bowl across the stone until it touched Jonah's knee, and went back to looking at the floor.

"Four," the old man said, to nobody.

"Nobody died."

"Four times nobody's understood you. I'm counting that now. It's easier."

---

Auger came over when the road had gone back to eating.

He came the way he'd leaned, slowly, without any weight in it, an old broken man with a chisel he'd forgotten he was holding, and he stood over Jonah and Pell and looked down at Jonah's arm, which Jonah had wrapped in a strip off his blanket, and at the bowl, and at Jonah.

"Cut one," he said. His voice was low and had gravel in it and he did not raise it, because he had not needed to raise it in a long time. "You'll do that once."

"Do what?"

"Give it back. Once, they'll think you're strange. Twice, they'll think you're soft. Soft's a thing they can use. You'll have a man at your bowl every night until one of them gets the point in." He looked at the wrapped arm. "You let him cut you."

"He was quick."

"He wasn't quick. He's Bricks. I've watched Bricks eleven years. You stepped back when you should have gone in and you took a cut you didn't need, and then you did a thing with his wrist that I've seen done twice in my life, both times by men with a stripe, and you did it slow and ugly on purpose so nobody'd see what it was." He crouched. His knees made a sound. His broken face came level with Jonah's and it was not unfriendly, which was worse. "I've been down here nineteen years, cut one. There's nothing on this road I haven't seen. I'm telling you what I saw. I'm not telling anybody else. Yet."

Jonah looked at him.

"What do you want?" he said.

"Nothing." Auger stood up. "I want the road to run. I want ten shards a day out of six and I want nobody starving and I want nobody getting ideas, and a cut one who gives his bowl back and breaks a foreman's hand like a Stripe is an idea. So I'm going to watch you. And you're going to cut one shard a day, slow, with your arms shaking, like you have been, and you're going to lose the next one." He looked down at the bowl. "Three times, and they'll think it's a rule. I don't want a new rule. I've got enough."

He went back to the edge of the light and leaned on the rock and folded his arms, and did not look at Jonah again that night, and every man on the road looked at him and then away.

---

Decimus was at the dusk count.

He came down the road with his lamp and his list and his pale grey plate on his forearms that he never seemed to notice, and he said the numbers, and the men said them back, and when he came to Jonah he stopped and looked at the arm.

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"That'll want washing."

"It's washed."

"With what? There's nothing down here to wash a thing with but the wall, and I wouldn't." The old guard looked at him a moment longer, and then reached into the pocket of the grey coat and took out a strip of clean cloth, real cloth, a bandage, and held it out. "Wrap it in that. Give the blanket back to the blanket."

Jonah took it. He didn't say thank you. You didn't. But he looked at the guard, and the guard looked back, and there was nothing in the old lined face but the thing that had waited in the tunnel while Jonah knelt, and Jonah thought, he's not supposed to have that in his pocket. Nobody hands a cut one a bandage on his third night. That's a thing he had to bring down.

"Bricks says you gave the bowl back," Decimus said.

"Bricks says a lot."

"Bricks says nothing. That's why I listen when he does." The guard made a mark on his list. "Don't do it again. It won't be personal." And he went on down the road saying numbers, and Jonah stood with a bandage in his hand that had been brought down ten kilometres of rock for a man whose name was a number, and put it with the rest.

---

He wrapped the arm. He lay on his shelf. The lamp did not go out.

Under the bandage, the cut was closing. He could feel it. The thing under his ribs had come up at the cut and he'd said no and it had stopped, and now, in the dark, with nobody looking, he let it, and it went into the arm and did what it did, slowly, the way it had put the ring under his skin, and the edges of the wound found each other and held. By dawn there would be a line. By the next dawn there would be nothing. He'd have to keep the bandage on for a week anyway, and remember to wince.

He lay and thought about Auger. About being watched. About a bowl in the middle of a road and a rule he was not allowed to make.

And he felt it start.

It started the way it had in the cage, small, a lean. The thing under his ribs, fed on the shackle, settled, closing his arm, turned. Not toward him. Away. Toward the rock wall of the gallery, and through it, toward the road, and past the road, toward the seventh tunnel and the chamber at the end of it and the dark breathing wall he'd stood in front of for three days with a chisel in his hands, cutting one shard, slowly.

It leaned. Then it pulled.

It was not the lunge. It had lunged once and hit the inside of him and put him on his knees, and it had not done that again; it had lain flat since the slab, hurt, hungry, careful. This was worse. This was the pull of a thing that had been careful for three days and was tired of it, that had lain at the bottom of a man in a rock ten kilometres under a garrison and had listened, every hour of every one of those days, to something on the other side of a wall breathing, and had had enough of listening.

Mother, it said. Not in a word. In the pull.

Jonah put his hand flat on his chest.

"No," he said, into the blanket. "Not yet. I don't know what happens. I don't know what you are and I don't know what it is and I'm not going through a wall in the dark to find out."

The pull did not stop.

It had never not stopped. He'd said no in the hold and it had gone still. He'd said no in the cage and it had not heard him, and he'd told himself that was the wall, the first sight, the shock of it. It was not the shock. It was this. It was lying on a shelf in the dark with a dead shackle on his ankle and a bandage on his arm and a thing under his ribs that had given up a piece of itself for him on a slab, and that was now, quietly, with everything it had, pulling toward a door, and did not care that he had said no, because it had not been asking.

He held it. The way he'd held it on the glass. The way he'd held it kneeling in his father's room. He put both hands on his chest and held it back from the wall with his whole self, and felt the seam pull, the place that had torn when he knelt, and felt it hold, and felt the thing under his ribs press against the seam, patient, homesick, strong.

He held it until the bell.

Then he got up, and said his number, and went to cut a shard, slowly, with his arms shaking, and did not have to pretend.
