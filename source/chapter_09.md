# Chapter 9: Decury

The shackle tightened on his ankle in the dark, and Jonah put his teeth in the blanket.

Decury six slept in a side gallery off the Shallows road, a cut the length of a barge hold, with rock shelves along both walls and a lamp at the end that never went out. Ten men under grey blankets that had been on a great many men before. Jonah lay on his shelf with his knees drawn up and his left ankle in both hands.

The shackle had been awake since the Throat. It sat on his ankle and looked at it. When he shifted, it tightened a fraction, a hand closing on something it meant to keep. It would do that for the rest of his life, unless the rest of his life was short.

And under his ribs, it was looking at the shackle.

Not hungry. It had not been hungry since the slab. It lay at the bottom of him where it had gone flat, with the raw place where the piece had been torn off, and it looked at the shackle the way a man with a wound looks at the knife.

"It's a lorica," he said into the blanket. "Like the collar. A stupid one. It only knows one thing."

It did not care what the shackle knew.

It moved. Down his left leg, through the muscle, reading as it went. It found the old torn ligament from the yards, stopped at it, interested, and went on. It reached his ankle, and the place where the shackle sat on the skin, and stopped there, and looked at the shackle from underneath.

The shackle tightened.

It was stupid but not blind. It gripped as it would have gripped if he'd run. Heat under the iron, small. A warning.

Then it went through the skin, into the shackle, and started to eat.

Not fast. Varro's lorica had been warm and dying and there had been a lot of it. This was small and cold and it did not want to be eaten. It fought, if something that knew one thing could fight. The fighting was the burn. The burn went from a warning to a fact. Jonah lay on the rock shelf with a blanket in his teeth and his ankle on fire and made no sound. Pell was on the shelf above. Corr was across the gallery. The lamp never went out.

"You're shaking the shelf," Pell said, from above, to the dark.

"Cramp."

"Cramp." A pause. "Walk it off, then."

"Can't."

Pell said nothing more.

It took most of the night. It went through the shackle as it had gone through the collar, one small dead want at a time. There was only one want. Hold. It came up his leg over and over, hold, hold, hold, and was folded away. Each time, the burn went down a little. The raw place at the bottom of him felt a little less raw.

Near dawn it was done.

The shackle was still on his ankle. He put his hand on it. Cold. A ring of dead metal that any guard would see as a shackle. It would never tighten again. It would never burn. If he ran, nobody would know until they counted.

That's two, he thought. The collar and this. Two of the Empire's hands off me, and nobody can see either one. He did not know if that was a lot or nothing.

Somewhere up the road a bell rang, flat. Decimus's voice came down the rock saying numbers, and Jonah got up.

---

"Ten a day," Bricks said. "That's the whole of it."

The foreman of decury six was a Blank, or had been. White scar where the line had been. Short and wide, with small eyes in a wide face, and a chisel in his belt that he had ground down at one end to a point. He'd been in the Shallows eleven years, which nobody else in the decury could say or imagine. He said it on the road, the first morning, to the ten of them, without any weight.

"A shard is a piece of the wall the length of your thumb. You cut it off with a chisel and drop it in the bucket. Ten of you, ten shards, one bucket. I carry the bucket to the cage at dusk and a guard counts. Ten, we eat. Nine, we eat less. Eight, I pick a man and he's flogged at the count."

"Seven?" someone said.

"Seven, I pick a man." Bricks let that sit. "And Decimus doesn't come down for what happens after."

He looked along the line and stopped at Jonah.

"You're the cut one."

"Four four seven one."

"Corr says the Vein wants you."

"Corr says a month to everyone."

Bricks looked at him. The small eyes were not stupid.

"So he does. Cut a shard today. Cut a shard tomorrow. When you can't cut a shard, tell me, and I'll pick somebody else for the post. Don't make me guess."

That was the whole of Bricks. Jonah would come to think it was the whole of the Shallows. Cut a shard. Don't make me guess.

He cut a shard.

That was the problem. He'd stood at the wall on the first day and pushed, and the wall had let him. On the third day Corr stopped cutting and stood with his own chisel hanging. He had watched Jonah put steel to the Vein and take a hand's length off it in the time it took Corr to score a line.

"It comes off for you," Corr said quietly.

"It's soft here."

"It's not soft anywhere. I've been on this wall four years. It comes off for you, cut one." Corr looked at him, close, with the wall's dark on his face. "It does that. Before. When it wants a man. It gets easy for him. He cuts like a foreman for a month, and then he can't lift the chisel, and then he's on the far side with the deep ones." He went back to his own line. "Enjoy it."

Jonah stood with the shard in his hand. The wall was not soft. It was polite. Something on the other side of it had turned to look at him on the first day, and looked at what he carried, and decided this one could cut. It let the chisel in.

He could have taken twenty shards a day. Thirty. Decury six could have eaten like foremen and never seen the post. And every guard on the road would have looked at the bucket and asked how. Corr would have said, it comes off for him. One day somebody with a glass would come down the Throat to see what the Vein liked so much about four four seven one.

So he cut one. Slowly. He stood at the wall with the Vein opening for his chisel and took one shard a day off it with his arms shaking, a new man's shake, and dropped it in the bucket. Bricks counted ten. Decury six ate.

It was the hardest work he had ever done. Not the cutting. The not.

---

"There's a law down here," Pell had said, on the first night, to the shelf above. "Not theirs. Ours. Auger's."

"Who's Auger?"

"King of the Shallows. Been here longest. Unstripped, like you. Holds the top road, and the rations come through him first. He's the reason nobody starves down here, and the reason nobody gets fat. They say he's fair." Pell had been quiet a moment. "They say it the way you say a wall's straight."

"What's the law?"

"You eat what you're given unless somebody wants it. If somebody wants it, he says so, and you fight for it. Chisels, in the road, with Auger's man watching so it's fair. Nobody dies. Mostly. You lose, you go hungry, and they know you'll go hungry again. You win, they leave you alone for a while."

"When?"

"New men get it on the third night. Always the third. It's what they do."

On the third night, at the meal, Bricks took his own bowl and Jonah's. The meal was grey, ladled out of a cauldron at the cage end of the road by a Collar with one eye. Bricks walked into the middle of the road, set Jonah's bowl down on the rock, and said, "Mine."

The road went quiet.

Sixty men, four decuries, eating with their backs to the walls. They all stopped. Jonah stood with his empty hands and looked at his bowl in the middle of the road, and at Bricks standing over it with the ground-down chisel in his belt. Of course it's him. He's the one who has to know.

"You don't want it," Jonah said. "You eat first. You've eaten."

"I want to see what the cut one does when a man takes his food." Bricks had not drawn the chisel. His eyes were on Jonah's hands. "Corr says you cut like a foreman and then shake like you didn't. Corr says a lot. I want to see. Come and get your bowl."

Somebody moved at the edge of the light.

Big, slow, old. A white scar on a forearm as thick as Jonah's leg. A face that had been broken and set wrong more than once. He held a chisel he seemed to have forgotten he was holding. He leaned against the rock at the edge of the road and folded his arms and looked at Bricks and at Jonah and said nothing. Every man on the road looked at him and then looked away.

Auger nodded once, slightly, at Bricks. Fair.

Jonah walked into the road.

---

He'd had three nights to think, lying on the shelf with the dead shackle under his hand, and he'd used them. He was not going to let the feet do it.

Not because they wouldn't win. They would. He could feel them in his legs, looking at Bricks as they had looked at Hask. Bricks was better than Hask. Bricks had been in a great many fights in a narrow road and knew where a chisel went. It didn't matter. A Fulvian step off the line and a wrist-turn would have Bricks on the rock with his own point under his ear before anyone finished breathing in.

And every man on the road would see a Collar barge pilot do what garrison schools taught. Auger would see it, and Auger did not look forgetful. Bricks would be dead or crippled. Decury six would have no foreman, and the post would find a new one, and the new one would be the man who'd done it.

So. Six years of yard fights, three weeks in the sand, and a dead man's opinion about where Bricks's weight was, which he would listen to and not obey.

Bricks drew the chisel.

He came in low. Straight, but not Hask. The point came fast and short at the belly and pulled back before it landed. A feint. His other hand was already coming for Jonah's throat, and it was good. Eleven years of narrow roads.

The feet said left and forward. Jonah said no, and stepped back.

The grabbing hand missed him by a finger. Bricks's weight came half a step too far. Jonah hit him.

In the face. With his fist. The least clever thing he could have done, and Bricks had not expected it. Nobody in the Shallows hit a man in the face. They went for the chisel hand. The foreman's head snapped back. The point came round wild and opened Jonah's forearm from wrist to elbow.

He felt it go. He felt it come up at the cut, fast, and start to close the wound from the inside. No. Not here. It stopped. The blood ran.

Bricks came again.

This time Jonah let the feet do one thing. Not the step. Not the turn. When the chisel came in he did what Varro had known, what Livia had named, what a garrison school taught in the first month. A man's wrist is a hinge, and a hinge only goes one way. He caught the wrist with both hands, a yard man catching a falling tool. He bent it back.

Not sideways. Back, toward the elbow. Ugly and slow, and it took his whole weight. Bricks fought it with eleven years. The road watched two men stand locked in the middle of it with blood running off Jonah's arm onto the rock. Then something in the foreman's wrist went, with a sound. The chisel dropped and rang on the stone. Bricks made a noise through his teeth and went down on one knee.

Jonah let go.

He stepped back and picked up the chisel, because he'd left one on a barrel once and been told nobody did. He held it point down. He looked at the foreman on one knee with his wrist against his chest and his small eyes gone wide. He looked at Auger with his arms folded. He looked at his bowl.

It had been in the middle of the road the whole time and nobody had touched it.

He picked it up. He carried it to Bricks, crouched, with the blood running off his arm, and set it down on the rock in front of the foreman's knee.

"It's yours. You said mine. I heard you."

The road did not breathe.

Bricks looked at the bowl. He looked at Jonah's face, close. His eyes were doing a sum, and the sum was not coming out.

"You broke my hand."

"You cut my arm. Eat your soup."

"Nobody gives it back."

"I've been told." Jonah stood. His arm hurt. He was glad of it. It was the only part of him doing what it was supposed to. "Somebody's got to carry the bucket tomorrow. Not you, with that hand. Not me. I'm new and I'm cut and the Vein wants me, Corr says. So pick somebody. Don't make me guess."

He walked back to his place at the wall and sat down with his back to the rock, his bleeding arm in his lap, and no bowl. After a moment Pell, without looking, pushed his own bowl across the stone until it touched Jonah's knee.

"Four," the old man said.

"Nobody died."

"Four times nobody's understood you. I'm counting that now. It's easier."

---

Auger came over when the road had gone back to eating.

Slowly, without weight, an old broken man with a chisel he'd forgotten. He stood over Jonah and Pell and looked at Jonah's arm, wrapped in a strip off his blanket. He looked at the bowl, and at Jonah.

"Cut one." Low voice, gravel in it. He didn't raise it. He hadn't needed to in a long time. "You'll do that once."

"Do what?"

"Give it back. Once, they'll think you're strange. Twice, they'll think you're soft. Soft they can use. You'll have a man at your bowl every night until one of them gets the point in." He looked at the wrapped arm. "You let him cut you."

"He was quick."

"He wasn't quick. He's Bricks. I've watched Bricks eleven years. You stepped back when you should have gone in and you took a cut you didn't need. Then you did something with his wrist that I've seen done twice in my life, both times by men with a stripe. You did it slow and ugly on purpose so nobody'd see what it was."

He crouched. His knees made a sound. His broken face came level with Jonah's, and it was not unfriendly, which was worse.

"Nineteen years I've been down here, cut one. There's nothing on this road I haven't seen. I'm telling you what I saw. I'm not telling anybody else. Yet."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing." Auger stood. "I want the road to run. I want ten shards a day out of six. I want nobody starving and nobody getting ideas. A cut one who gives his bowl back and breaks a foreman's hand with a Stripe's trick is an idea. So I'm going to watch you. And you're going to cut one shard a day, slow, with your arms shaking, like you have been. You're going to lose the next one." He looked at the bowl. "Three times, and they'll think it's a rule. I don't want a new rule. I've got enough."

He went back to the edge of the light and leaned on the rock and folded his arms. He did not look at Jonah again that night.

---

Decimus came down the road at dusk count with his lamp and his list and the pale grey plate on his forearms. He said numbers, and the men said them back. At Jonah he stopped and looked at the arm.

"Four four seven one."

"Four four seven one."

"That'll want washing."

"It's washed."

"With what? There's nothing down here to wash with but the wall, and I wouldn't." The old guard looked at him a moment longer. Then he reached into the pocket of his grey coat and took out a strip of clean cloth. Real cloth. A bandage. "Wrap it in that. Give the blanket back to the blanket."

Jonah took it. You didn't say thank you. But he looked at the guard, and the guard looked back. Nobody hands a cut one a bandage on his third night. Nobody has one in his pocket ten kilometres down. He brought it.

"Bricks says you gave the bowl back," Decimus said.

"Bricks says a lot."

"Bricks says nothing. That's why I listen when he does." He made a mark on the list. "Don't do it again. It won't be personal."

He went on down the road saying numbers.

---

He wrapped the arm and lay on his shelf. The lamp did not go out.

Under the bandage the cut was closing. He'd said no in the road and it had stopped. Now, in the dark, with nobody looking, he let it. It went into the arm and did what it did, slowly, the same as it had put the ring under his skin. The edges found each other and held. By dawn there'd be a line. By the next dawn, nothing. He'd keep the bandage on a week anyway, and remember to wince.

He lay and thought about Auger. About being watched. About a rule he was not allowed to make.

And he felt it start.

Small, at first. A lean. Fed on the shackle, settled, closing his arm, it turned. Not toward him. Away. Toward the rock wall of the gallery and through it, toward the road and past the road, toward the seventh tunnel and the chamber at the end of it and the dark breathing wall he'd stood in front of for three days, cutting one shard, slowly.

It leaned. Then it pulled.

Not the lunge. It had lunged once, hit the inside of him, and put him on his knees. It had not done that again. It had lain flat since the slab, hurt, hungry, careful. This was worse. This was the pull of something that had been careful for three days and was tired of it. It had listened, every hour, to something on the other side of a wall, breathing. It had had enough of listening.

Mother, it said. Not in a word. In the pull.

Jonah put his hand flat on his chest.

"No," he said into the blanket. "Not yet. I don't know what happens. I don't know what you are and I don't know what it is. I'm not going through a wall in the dark to find out."

The pull did not stop.

He'd said no in the hold and it had gone still. He'd said no in the cage and it had not heard him, and he had told himself that was the shock of the first sight. It was not the shock. It was this. It had given up a piece of itself for him on a slab, and now, quietly, with everything it had, it was pulling toward a door. It did not care that he'd said no. It had not been asking.

He held it, as he had on the glass, as he had kneeling in his father's room. Both hands on his chest, holding it back from the wall with his whole self. He felt the seam pull, the place that had torn when he knelt, and felt it hold. It pressed against the seam, patient, homesick, strong.

He held it until the bell.

Then he got up, said his number, and went to cut a shard, slowly, with his arms shaking. He did not have to pretend.
