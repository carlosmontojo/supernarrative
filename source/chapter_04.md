# Chapter 4: Interlude. Three Men and a Letter

Silas Marchand had never in his life written a number he had not checked, and he was not going to start with a letter.

That was what he told himself, at the corner table of the Halyard, with the festival still going on in the square above and coming down through the rock as a kind of weather, a thud and a roar and a thud. He had a sheet of manifest paper in front of him, good paper, the kind the yard bought for the inspectors, and a pen, and a glass of Marl wine he had not touched, and he was checking.

Item. On the fourteenth day of the month, in the inner rings of Quirinus, the barge Kestrel was boarded by the cutter Vigilant under a lawful claim. He had seen the cutter. He had seen the officer. There was no question of that item.

Item. The officer, a decurion of the equestrian order, put the captain of the barge to death in the course of the claim. Marchand had watched it happen from six feet away and had dropped a manifest on the deck, which he had never done before in fourteen years and had thought about since more than he had thought about the death. There was no question of that item either. There was, if he was precise, no fault in it. A decurion may put a peregrine to death in the course of a lawful claim. It was not pleasant. Neither was a hull strike.

Item.

He put the pen down.

"You've been looking at that page for an hour," Corin Aldane said, across from him. "Either write it or drink."

"I'm being precise."

"You're being frightened. I've seen it on men in the sand. They stand there deciding, and then they get cut." Corin had the cutter's blood still on his shirt, gone brown, and a bandage on his forearm that somebody in the square had tied for him, and his name was on a roll in the quaestor's house, and he was drinking like a man who had earned it. "Write it. You've been talking about writing it for three weeks."

"I've been talking to you about it for three weeks. You've been agreeing with me for one day. What changed?"

Corin drank.

"I watched him," he said.

---

The third man at the table was asleep, or near enough. Gaspar Roake had come down from the square at the second bell with the festival ration inside him and a bottle of something from the north yards on top of that, and had sat down at their table because it was the nearest one, and had been told to go away, and had said, I know that boy's father, and had not gone. He was a big soft man with a welder's hands and a welder's cough and he had his head on his arms now and his eyes half open, and every so often he said, quietly, into the tabletop, Tom's boy, as if it were the answer to something.

Marchand had considered moving tables. He had decided against it. A man who moved tables was a man who had something to hide from a drunk, and Gaspar Roake would remember that, in the way drunks remembered things, sideways and at the wrong moment. Better to let him sleep. Better to let him hear. A witness who had heard nothing was a witness who could say he had heard nothing, and that was a thing Marchand might need said.

"What did you see," he asked Corin, "when you watched him?"

"You were there."

"I was watching the Stripe. I watch the Stripes. It's what a purser's for." Marchand turned the pen over. "Tell me what you saw."

Corin looked into his glass.

"I've been in the sand nine times," he said. "Four Ludi, five sharpenings. I've been cut in six of them. I'm the best knife in the south yards and everybody knows it and it's why I'm on the roll, and I'm telling you, Marchand, I'd have lost to that cadet. Not fast. But I'd have lost. He was quick and he was taught and he had a year of a garrison school in him, and I've got what I've got." He put the glass down. "And Vale put him on his back in the time it takes to say it. Vale. Who's never held a knife. Who flies a barge. He went left and forward on the thrust like it was a thing he'd done a thousand times, and I've never seen him do it once, and I've known him since we were six."

"So he learned it somewhere."

"Where? On the Kestrel? From Ochoa?" Corin's laugh had no fun in it. "There's nowhere. There's nowhere on this moon a Collar learns that. I've looked. I've looked my whole life. There's the sand and there's watching the Stripes and there's nothing else, and I've done both, and I'm what that gets you." He looked at Marchand, finally. "You were on the barge. You tell me where he learned it."

Marchand said nothing for a moment.

He was thinking about the hatch.

He had been in the cabin hatch when the decurion said the word. He'd gone up the ladder because he was the citizen of record and if there was going to be a claim there was going to be paper, and he'd wanted the paper in his hands. And he'd turned at the top and looked down, and the peregrine had been on his knee with his hand in the box, and the decurion had said the word to the collar, and the boy had gone still the way they all went still, and Marchand had felt, for one clean moment, that the world was in order.

And then it wasn't.

He had watched it come out of the boy. He did not have words for it and had stopped looking for them. He had watched it come out of him like sweat and cover him and move, and he had watched a bar of glass come out of the wrong side of the boy's arm, and he had watched a decurion of the equestrian order with fourteen years' plate on him go over on his back with the glass in his throat, and he had watched the boy pull it out.

That was not the part that frightened him. That was a horror, and horrors were things that happened, hull strikes, drive fires, a man's hand in a cutter's jaws. He had seen horrors. He had inventoried them.

The part that frightened him was after. When the plate had gone back in, and the boy had stood there in the hold with a dead Stripe at his feet, and had not screamed, and had not run, and had not fallen down. He had stood there and looked at what he'd done, and then he had knelt down beside the captain and said, don't talk. In an ordinary voice. And nine hours later, in the dark, with the cutter going around them, he had said, we're going to go home, and had laid out the whole of it, the strike, the torch, the manifest, the reading, in the same ordinary voice, and every piece of it had been right.

Marchand had lived his whole life among men who were frightened of him a little, because he knew where the numbers were. He had never been frightened of one of them.

"I'll tell you where he learned it," he said. "He didn't. Something in him did."

---

He wrote it then.

He did not hurry. He wrote in the hand he used for manifests, small and even, and he made each item a line, and he did not use a word he could not stand behind in a room with a Stripe in it. He did not write what he had seen in the hold. That was the thing he had decided in three weeks of not writing. He had seen it, and if he wrote that he had seen it, then he had seen a decurion die and had said nothing for three weeks, and had put his name to a manifest that said otherwise, and there was a knife for that and he knew its name.

So he did not write the hold. He wrote the sand. He wrote that the peregrine Jonah Vale, called to sharpen under the law, had fought with the footwork of the equestrian order, which no peregrine could have learned on Anvil, and that the peregrine stood, in daily life, in a manner consistent with a bearer of plate, and that he, Silas Marchand, citizen, had reason to believe the peregrine was in unlawful possession of a lorica or of some object of the same nature, taken from a founding-era hull in the rings on the fourteenth day of the month in the course of a salvage that had not been reported.

That was true. Every line of it. He checked it twice.

And then he wrote the other thing.

He had not been sure, until the pen was moving, that he would. It had come out of the captain's locker, where he had gone on the second day to inventory the effects, because that was what a citizen of record did, and it had not been there. The packet. He knew it had existed because he had seen Ochoa put things in that locker for six years that were never on a manifest, small sealed things that went aboard on Anvil and came off in the Interior and were never spoken of, and he had never spoken of them either, because a purser who spoke of such things did not remain a purser. And on the second day the locker had been clean. Someone had been there first.

And in the hold, over the captain, with the blood coming, the boy had leaned in close, and the captain had said something, and Marchand had been six feet away and had heard one word of it. It had sounded like a name.

He wrote: and further, that the said peregrine has in his possession a sealed packet, taken from the effects of the deceased captain, addressed to a person of the senatorial order, the name Vibius having been spoken in my hearing, which packet I believe to be correspondence of an unlawful faction, the deceased captain having for some years carried such correspondence unmanifested.

He looked at it for a long time.

It was the line that would do it. He knew that. The lorica was a horror, a thing that could not be, and the quaestor would read it and see a purser's fancy, a Collar with quick feet and a jealous Blank behind him, and might do nothing at all. But a sealed packet for a senator was sedition, and sedition was a page in a book, with a punishment printed under it, and a quaestor did not get to be a praetor by leaving that page unread.

"That'll bury him," Corin said. He had been reading upside down. He'd gone a bad colour. "Not the sand. The other. That's the mines, Marchand. That's not the post. That's a hole in a rock for the rest of his life."

"You wanted him gone."

"I wanted him." Corin stopped. "I wanted him not here. In a month I'm on a ship to somewhere and she's still on this moon with him, and every day of it she'll be watching him do things I can't, and I'll be in a uniform on some rock getting shot at so that one day I can stand on a dais. I wanted him not here. I didn't."

"You didn't what?"

Corin didn't answer.

Marchand turned the paper around and pushed it across the table, and put the pen on top of it.

"He killed a decurion," he said, quietly, so the drunk would not hear, though the drunk was saying Tom's boy into his arms and hearing nothing. "I watched it. I've said nothing for three weeks and put my name to a manifest that says the man was never aboard, and if the Flamens find what's in that boy at investiture, and they will, Corin, they will, they'll put a wand on him and it will scream, then they'll ask who was on the barge, and they'll ask who signed the manifest, and I'll be in the hole beside him, and so will Bram, and Hollis, and Morrow." He tapped the paper. "Or I'm the man who reported it. Before the wand. In writing. With a witness."

"That's not why you're doing it."

"No," Marchand agreed. "It's why I can."

Corin looked at the paper for a long time. Then he picked up the pen. His hand, Marchand noted, with the part of him that noted things, was not steady at all. It was the hand of a man in the sand who had decided, and was about to be cut, and knew it.

He signed. Corin Aldane, peregrine, of Anvil, enrolled this day. Witness.

Gaspar Roake lifted his head off his arms.

He looked at the two of them with his wet half-open eyes, and at the paper, and at the pen, and it was not clear how much he had heard or how much of what he had heard he would keep, and he opened his mouth, and Marchand waited.

"Tom's boy," Gaspar said.

"Yes."

"He's a good boy."

"Yes."

Gaspar looked at the paper a while longer. Then he put his head back down on his arms and closed his eyes, and after a moment he was snoring, and he had not said anything else, and Marchand understood that he would not, that this was a man who had decided long ago that the safest thing a Collar could do in any room was nothing, and had been right often enough to keep doing it.

He folded the letter.

---

He carried it to the quaestor's house himself, at the sixth bell, with the festival dying overhead and the sun-lamps going down to their night setting, and put it through the slot in the door where petitions went. It was addressed on the outside in his manifest hand. To the Quaestor of the Province, Marcus Vibius Priscus, from Silas Marchand, citizen, purser of the barge Kestrel, in the matter of the peregrine Jonah Vale. Urgent, and concerning the Flamens.

He walked home through the tenement roads with his hands in his coat and thought about nothing, precisely, item by item, the way he had learned to.

He had done it for the captaincy. He knew that. He had wanted the Kestrel for six years and had been promised it and had watched it go to a Collar on the yard office steps that afternoon with the whole moon cheering, and he had felt the thing he always felt, which was that the sum had been added wrong and nobody would ever check it.

He had done it because he was afraid, too. Of the knife, and of the hole, and of the wand at investiture, and of the boy.

But under both of those, in the place where he kept the numbers he did not show anyone, there was a third item, and he had checked it, and it was true, and it was the one he would never write down.

He had done it because the boy had stood in the hold with a dead Stripe at his feet and had not been sorry. Not for one second. He had looked at the stripe on the dead man's arm and something had gone across his face, fast, that Marchand had seen and would not name, and it had not been horror, and it had not been grief. And a Collar who could feel that, standing over a Stripe, and then go up a ladder and fly, was not a thing that Silas Marchand could share a moon with, or a barge, or a world.

He let himself into his rooms and lit the lamp and sat down at his own table and took out his own ledger, and wrote the day's figures, and checked them, and they came out.

Outside, the square went quiet at last, and the moon turned toward the sun, and somewhere in the tenements a boy with a ring on his finger lay awake with a blanket over the vent and did not know that he had six days.
