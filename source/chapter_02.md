# Chapter 2: The Rings

The Vigilant fired on nineteen.

Jonah had the drive lit on twenty. He'd had his hand on it since he came through the hatch. The countdown had gone on in the cabin speaker in that flat Blank voice. He knew what a cutter's gunner did in the last five seconds of a count. He'd talked to one once, in a yard bar, a Blank on leave who'd wanted to tell a Collar how it felt. You stop listening to the count, the gunner had said. You watch the target. If it so much as twitches before zero, you fire, and you're covered, because it twitched.

So Jonah didn't twitch. He lit the drive on twenty with the jets already turning the barge. The Kestrel's two hundred metres swung on its nose. On nineteen the first round went through the space where the cabin had been.

He felt it go. A pressure in the hull, a shiver in the deck under his boots, and a light on the panel that said the aft cargo ring had lost something it would rather have kept.

"Strap in," he said again, to the cabin, to whoever was in it. "Strap in, strap in, strap."

The second seat creaked. Marchand. Not Ochoa. It was going to be a while before that seat was anybody but Ochoa.

He put the Kestrel into the rings.

---

He had done it once already that day and it had been the best flying of his life. This time it was worse. He did it anyway.

The debris came at him slow and enormous and turning. But now there was a cutter behind him putting rounds into it. Every round that missed the barge hit something else, and everything it hit became more debris, smaller and faster, coming from directions the ring plane had never sent anything from before. He took the barge under a hull slab. Something the size of a house came off the top of it and went past the cabin close enough to see rivets. He took the barge over a drive cowling and the drive cowling was gone. Where it had been there was a spray of glass and metal expanding in every direction. He went through it because there was nowhere to go around, and heard it rattle on the hull.

And he was sick.

Nobody would ever believe that part, later, when it was a story. He'd flown the run of his life with a fever. It had come on him on the ladder. By the time he'd got the drive lit his hands were shaking on the yoke. Not with fear. He knew what fear did to his hands. This was coming up from inside them.

Plate was coming out of his hands.

Not all of it. Not the flood from the hold. It came out in patches, on the backs of his fingers and the heels of his palms. Dark glass formed and held for a second and slid back in, over and over, trying to work out how a hand went. Where it formed on the yoke it stuck. He'd move his hand and it would come away with a small wet click and leave a smear on the grip that wasn't sweat.

His vision doubled. Not blurred. Doubled, cleanly, two of everything and both of them sharp. He had to pick one. He picked the one that was where things actually were, and mostly he was right.

"Vale," Marchand said. His voice sounded far away. "Vale, your face."

"Busy."

"Your face is doing something."

Jonah said, "So's the cutter, Silas. Pick one to watch."

The legion transport was ahead. Half a warship, broken across the middle four hundred years ago and still the largest single thing in the rings, tumbling so slowly it looked still. Its open end faced them. Inside was a cathedral of dead decks and drive housings. It was dark. It was the only place in the ring plane big enough to hide a barge.

His hands had already started the turn.

Behind his heart, what had come into him on the hold deck sat up.

It had been lying there since the fight, hungry and waiting. Now it moved. It wasn't afraid. He understood that suddenly and completely. It didn't know what a cutter was, or a barge, or a round going through a hull. It had noticed that the body it was in was moving very fast and doing something difficult and doing it well. It was interested, as it had been interested in the old break in his wrist. It wanted to see.

So he let it see.

He didn't give it his hands. He didn't give it anything. He just stopped holding the door shut for a moment. It looked out through his eyes at the open end of the legion transport coming up at them. The two images in his vision became one, and he saw the way through.

Later, in the Chamber, an old man would give him words for it. Sense and Edge and the number a Flamen writes next to them. He would say, that's not what it was. What it was: the transport's dead interior stopped being darkness with things in it and became a shape. A whole shape, all at once, every deck, every housing, every hanging cable, and the width of every gap.

The Kestrel went in.

He took it through a gap between two drive housings that Bram would swear, for the rest of his life, had not been wide enough. He took it around a hanging mass of cable that would have taken the cabin off, and down, into the belly of the dead ship. He cut the drive. He cut the jets. He cut the lights. The barge drifted on into the dark and stopped against something with a bump everybody in the cabin felt in their teeth.

Then it was quiet.

Outside, far off, through the hull, they heard the Vigilant put three more rounds into the ring plane at nothing, and stop.

---

They waited nine hours.

The cutter went around the transport twice. A patrol cutter has a drive you can hear through a hull at a kilometre, if the hull is quiet enough. The Kestrel was as quiet as a barge gets, drive cold, heaters off, four people breathing as little as they could. The second time around it slowed and sat off the open end of the transport for what Marchand's watch said was fifty minutes. Jonah sat in the pilot's seat with his hands in his lap, not on the yoke, because he wasn't sure what his hands would do.

Then it went. They heard it go up out of the ring plane and away, and after that there was nothing.

"They'll be back," Marchand said.

Jonah said, "Yes. Not here. Not for a while."

"They'll sit at the edge and wait. Ochoa said it. They can sit for a month."

"Ochoa said they'd sit for a month if they knew we were coming out. They think we're dead, Silas. They put a round through the aft ring and watched us go into the biggest wreck in the plane with a cutter on our tail and no way out. Nobody comes out of that. That's what their gunner's writing down right now. Barge destroyed, presumed, all hands."

"Presumed."

"It's a lot of ring. They're not going to search it for a Collar barge. They'll write it up and go home. The report will say a decurion boarded a salvage barge and there was a hostile lorica aboard. It'll say the barge was destroyed with all hands. And that'll be that."

"A hostile lorica." Marchand was looking at him. He'd been looking at him for nine hours. "On a Collar barge."

"That's what the marines saw."

"That's what I saw."

Jonah didn't answer.

"I saw it, Vale. I was in the hatch. I saw you go down on your knee. I saw him say the word and I saw you stop. Then I saw." Marchand stopped. His hands were folded on the manifest, which he'd picked up off the hold deck at some point and had not let go of since. They were very steady, which was how Jonah knew he was frightened. "What are you?"

Jonah said, "I don't know. I'm nineteen, Silas. There's a dead man's feet in my legs and something behind my heart that wanted to eat him, and I'm making the rest of this up as fast as you are."

"Don't."

"I'm not making up what happened. I put my hand in the box because the captain told me not to let anybody touch it. The man who'd just killed him was about to touch it. That's all I decided. Then it decided the rest."

"It."

"Whatever was in the box."

"Where is it now?"

Jonah looked at his hands. The plate had stopped coming out of them around the third hour. The fever hadn't. He could feel it in his teeth.

"In me," he said.

Marchand didn't say anything for a while.

"He was a decurion," he said at last. "Equestrian order. Fourteen years' service, it'll be in his record. Do you know what they do to a peregrine who kills a Stripe?"

"I've an idea."

"You don't. You have an idea of what they do to a peregrine who hits a Stripe. There's no page for the other thing. There's no page because it doesn't happen. A collar doesn't let it happen. It's not a crime, Vale, it's not on the list. It can't happen. When it happens anyway they don't have a punishment for it. They have Flamens, and the Flamens have a knife." He unfolded his hands, looked at them, and folded them again. "And it won't only be you. It'll be the crew. It'll be Morrow. It'll be the yard."

"I know."

"So what are we going to do?"

Jonah had been sitting with that question for nine hours. At some point he had stopped thinking about the cutter and started thinking about that.

"We're going to go home," he said. "We're going to go home with a dead captain and a hole in the aft ring. The hole's going to look like a debris strike, because Bram's going to get out on the hull with a torch and make it look like one. The captain died in the same strike. Nobody boarded us, because who boards a barge in the rings. There was no decurion, and there was no box." He looked at Marchand. "And you're going to write that down, because it's your name on the report."

"They'll read us."

"They'll read a barge crew. Two Collar cutters, a Collar pilot, and a Blank purser. They read us every time we dock. They'll get exactly what they get every time."

"Not from you."

Jonah said, "Yes. From me. Ochoa trusted me with the yoke for six years. He can trust me with a lie for one afternoon."

He didn't know if it was true. He was about to find out.

---

He went down into the hold alone.

Ochoa was where he'd left him. Bram had put a blanket over him, one of the grey ones from the crew locker, and had folded his hands on his chest, which Ochoa would have found sentimental. The blood had gone dark. The hold smelled of it.

Varro was where he'd left him too. Nobody had put a blanket on Varro.

Jonah crouched beside the decurion. The face was still surprised. The fang had gone back into the arm as the plate came off him. The arm was just an arm, with the narrow stripe on the inside of the forearm.

Behind Jonah's heart, it leaned toward the stripe.

He'd known it would. He'd felt it looking at Varro nine hours ago. He'd said no, out loud, and it had stopped doing anything about it and not stopped wanting. For nine hours in the dark it had wanted. Not as a man wants a meal. As a fire wants what's next to it. There was nothing cruel in it. It was hungry, and there was a lorica on the deck in front of it that belonged to nobody now.

"He was going to kill me," Jonah said, to the hold. "He'd have taken the hand off and then he'd have decided I wasn't worth the paperwork."

It didn't care. He hadn't expected it to.

"And he's going out the airlock either way. Into the rings. Him and his stripe. Nobody's going to find him."

He put his hand on Varro's arm.

He hadn't meant to. He would tell the old man with the stick that, years later, and the old man would nod. He'd meant to argue with himself a while longer. But the hand went down on the stripe before the deciding was done, the same as it had gone into the box. The thing behind his heart went out through his palm and into the dead man's arm, and Jonah felt it begin to eat.

It was not fast. It went into the stripe and found the lorica there, the cutting, the dog-brained loyal thing that had lived in Varro for fourteen years and put a blade in his hand on command. It had watched its man die and had nothing to do about it. Jonah's genius did not tear it. It took it apart. The pieces came up his arm one at a time. Each was a small dead want: a wish to stand, a wish to strike, a wish to be told what to do. Each came into him and was folded away, and the folding felt like being fed.

Something else came with it.

He'd stood in Founding Square at fourteen and watched a Thinstripe from a visiting cutter sharpen against Corin Aldane, steel knives, unplated, for the crowd. He'd watched the Stripe's feet and never forgotten them. The man had not seemed to move his feet at all and had never once been where Corin's knife went. Now Jonah had that. It was in his legs and the small of his back, patient and certain, a way of standing he had never learned. Under it, faint, folded in with the rest, was a feeling he recognised from the wrong side. The feeling of looking at a Collar. Contempt, easy and unexamined, the kind you don't know you're carrying because you've never had to put it down. It was in him now, and it was looking at him.

He took his hand off the arm.

The stripe was gone. There was pale skin where it had been, and a faint smudge in the shape of a stripe.

Jonah sat back against the hold bulkhead and breathed for a while.

The fever had changed. It had gone from his teeth to all of him, evenly. Under the heat something had settled. He knew, without looking, where the plate would come out if he asked it to. He knew it would come out where he asked, mostly. He knew the crooked fang was on the wrong side of his wrist and would stay there, because that was where it had learned to be. For the first time since the hold deck, the thing in him and the body it was in were arguing about the same house.

Above him, the cabin hatch was open. He hadn't heard it open. A hand's width, and nobody in it. Then he heard the second seat creak.

He didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that would make it unseen.

---

Bram went out on the hull with a torch. The fever went with Jonah into the pilot's seat. Between them they took the Kestrel out of the transport, out of the rings, and across eleven hours of nothing toward Anvil, a grey rock with a black stain of yards on the sunward side.

Varro went into the rings before they left the transport. Jonah did it himself. He took the decurion to the airlock, put him in it, and cycled it. He watched through the port as the body went out into the dark inside the dead ship, turning slowly, one more piece of a ring plane full of pieces. He thought he should say something over it and found he had nothing. Then he found that he did, and that it was what the crowd said when a Stripe won a bout in Founding Square. He didn't say that either.

Ochoa stayed. Ochoa was going to the melt, because he'd been born a Collar and had died one, whatever the paper said. The melt was where Collars went. Bram wrapped him properly. Hollis stopped saying "he just did it" around the fourth hour and started crying instead, which was better.

Nobody talked to Jonah. Marchand sat in the second seat and wrote in the manifest for a long time in his small clerk's hand. When he'd finished he read it back to himself twice, silently, moving his lips. Then he closed it and held it and looked out at the dark. The three of them who had been in the hold when Jonah went down there did not look at Jonah at all.

He didn't blame them. He didn't much want to look at himself.

The collar was the last thing.

He'd forgotten it. Somewhere in the eighth hour he'd put his hand up to his throat without thinking and felt the ring there, cold, and remembered that it was nothing. That it had been eaten in the first second of the bond, leaving the shell. Every inspector on Anvil would look at it and see a collar. Any one of them could say the word, and nothing would happen.

That was going to be a problem. Not this docking. Maybe not the next. But one day somebody with a stripe was going to say the word to him, in a room with other people in it, and his knees were going to stay straight.

He put the thought away. There were a lot of things in there now.

---

Anvil came up on the screen. The yards first, on the sunward side, a hundred kilometres of broken ships laid out on grey rock. Then the lights of the dock ring, then the docking beacon. Then, on the open channel, a dock inspector asking for the Kestrel's manifest and the citizen of record.

Marchand gave it. His voice was steady and dull and exactly right. Debris strike in the outer rings. Captain lost. Aft cargo ring damaged, request a yard slot. No salvage. No boarding. Four aboard, three peregrine, one citizen, the citizen speaking.

"Reading on docking," the inspector said, and cut the channel.

Jonah brought the barge in.

The reading was a Blank with a grade you could count on one hand and a wand he waved at each of them in turn on the dock, bored. The wand was the small reading. It found a lorica on a man or it did not. The big reading was the glass, a slab of dark stone in the Flamens' house that gave a number, the gifts, and the colour of the plate. The College wrote all of it down and kept it for ever. A Collar coming off a barge got the wand. Nobody had ever been on the wrong side of a wand. Jonah stood in the line with Bram on one side and Hollis on the other and thought, very hard, about nothing.

He hadn't known that would work. He'd thought about it for eleven hours. In the hold, when Varro said the word to the collar, the genius had gone still. It had gone still because it was listening. Maybe it could be asked to be still now. So he asked. Not with words. He held it, and it held. It was the strangest feeling he'd ever had: a second heart, held still inside the first.

"Service?" the Blank asked, not looking up from his slate, when the wand reached Jonah.

Jonah said, "Six years, sir. Ten fingers. I count them every morning."

The Blank didn't look up. Whatever the joke had been worth, it wasn't worth writing down. Jonah decided it had been for himself, and let it go.

The wand came past his throat, his chest, his hands, and went on to Hollis.

"Peregrine, peregrine, peregrine," the Blank said, writing. "Citizen. Clear. Sorry about your captain." He didn't sound sorry. He didn't sound anything. He went away.

Jonah let go, and nearly sat down on the dock.

Morrow was there.

He'd been there the whole time, at the back, behind the inspector, where a yard owner had to stand when the Empire was reading his crew. Now he came forward, big and grey and tired in a coat that had been good once. He looked at the wrapped shape Bram and Hollis were carrying between them. He put his hand on it, and kept it there.

"Debris," he said.

"Debris," Marchand said.

Morrow looked at Jonah. Jonah looked back. Neither of them said anything, and Morrow's hand stayed on the blanket. After a moment he nodded once, slowly.

"Take him to the yard house," Morrow said. "I'll speak to his people. Silas, the manifest, my office, now. Jonah."

"Sir."

"Go home. You look like death. You'll come to my office tomorrow and we'll talk about the petition. I'm not stopping it for this, do you hear me, I'm not stopping it for this, the man would've cut my ears off." His voice caught, and he let it, and went on. "Tomorrow. Go home."

Jonah went.

Tom was at the dock gate. He'd have been there since the beacon; he was always there, in his good coat, with his hat in his hand for the inspector. His face when he saw Jonah come through the gate on his own feet did something Jonah could not, just then, afford to look at for long.

Mara was beside him.

She came forward. She had her hair tied back for the yards and burn marks on both forearms from the torches, and she was smiling with her whole face. She came at him to put her arms around him, and he stepped back.

He didn't mean to. His body did it. Something in him had come out of his hands on a yoke that morning with no warning at all, and it was hungry. He did not know what it would do if she touched him. His body put a step between them before he could think.

Mara stopped.

Her smile stayed where it was, but something behind it went careful.

"Jonah?"

"I'm all right." His own voice sounded wrong to him. "I'm all right. Ochoa's dead. I'll tell you. I'll tell you all of it, I just." He looked at his hands. "Not yet. Let me. Not yet."

She looked at his hands too. Then at his face. After a moment she nodded, and did not come closer. That was when he knew she was going to be the hardest one to lie to.

Tom put a hand on his shoulder. It was fine. Nothing came out.

"Come home," his father said. "Come home, son. Whatever it is, come home first."

They walked. Jonah had a stone box in his kit that was empty and a sealed packet inside his suit that was not. He had a fever, a dead man's feet, and something behind his heart that had eaten a Thinstripe and was already, faintly, hungry again.

At the top of the dock ramp he looked back once.

Marchand was standing where the inspector had left him, with the manifest under his arm, watching the three of them go. When he saw Jonah look, he didn't look away. He raised his voice just enough to carry.

"I didn't see anything," he said. "Captain."

Then he turned and went to find Morrow's office.
