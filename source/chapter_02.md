# Chapter 2: The Rings

The Vigilant fired on nineteen.

Jonah had the drive lit on twenty. He'd had his hand on it since he came through the hatch, and the countdown had gone on in the cabin speaker in that flat Blank voice, and somewhere around twenty-four he'd stopped hearing the numbers and started hearing the space between them. He knew what a cutter's gunner did in the last five seconds of a count. He'd talked to one once, in a yard bar, a Blank on leave who'd wanted to tell a Collar how it felt. You stop listening to the count, the gunner had said. You watch the target. If it so much as twitches before zero, you fire, and you're covered, because it twitched.

So Jonah didn't twitch. He lit the drive on twenty with the jets already turning the barge, and the Kestrel's two hundred metres swung on its nose like a door, and on nineteen the first round went through the space where the cabin had been.

He felt it go. Not heard. Felt. A pressure in the hull, a shiver in the deck under his boots, and a light on the panel that said the aft cargo ring had lost something it would rather have kept.

"Strap in," he said again, to the cabin, to whoever was in it. "Strap in, strap in, strap."

The second seat creaked. Marchand. Not Ochoa. It was going to be a while before that seat was anybody but Ochoa.

He put the Kestrel into the rings.

---

He had done it once already that day and it had been the best flying of his life, and this time it was worse, and he did it anyway.

The debris came at him the way it always did, slow and enormous and turning, but now there was a cutter behind him putting rounds into it, and every round that missed the barge hit something else, and everything it hit became more debris, smaller and faster and coming from directions the ring plane had never sent anything from before. He took the barge under a hull slab and something the size of a house came off the top of it and went past the cabin close enough to see rivets. He took the barge over a drive cowling and the drive cowling was gone, and where it had been there was a spray of glass and metal expanding in every direction, and he went through it because there was nowhere to go around, and heard it on the hull like rain on a roof.

And he was sick.

That was the part nobody would ever believe, later, when it was a story. He'd flown the run of his life with a fever. It had come on him on the ladder. By the time he'd got the drive lit his hands were shaking on the yoke, not with fear, he knew what fear did to his hands, but with something coming up from inside them, and by the time the second round went past, the shaking had turned into something else.

Plate was coming out of his hands.

Not all of it. Not the way it had in the hold. It came out in patches, on the backs of his fingers and the heels of his palms, dark glass forming and holding for a second and then sliding back in, over and over, like something trying to work out how a hand went. Where it formed on the yoke it stuck. He'd move his hand and it would come away with a sound, a small wet click, and there would be a smear of something on the grip that wasn't sweat.

His vision doubled. Not blurred. Doubled, cleanly, so that there were two of everything and both of them were sharp, and he had to pick one, and he picked the one that was where things actually were, and mostly he was right.

"Vale," Marchand said. His voice sounded far away. "Vale, your face."

"Busy."

"Your face is doing something."

"So's the cutter, Silas. Pick one to watch."

The legion transport was ahead. Half a warship, broken across the middle four hundred years ago and still the largest single thing in the rings, tumbling so slowly that from the Kestrel's cabin it looked still. Its open end faced them. Inside it was a cathedral of dead decks and drive housings and things nobody had a name for anymore, and it was dark, and it was the only place in the ring plane big enough to hide a barge.

He didn't think about it. His hands had already started the turn.

Behind his heart, the thing that had come into him on the hold deck sat up.

He felt it. It had been lying there since the fight, hungry and waiting, not doing anything, and now it moved. It wasn't afraid. He understood that suddenly and completely. It didn't know what the cutter was. It didn't know what a barge was or a ring plane or a round going through a hull. It had noticed that the body it was in was moving very fast and doing something difficult and doing it well, and it was interested, the way it had been interested in the old break in his wrist. It wanted to see.

So he let it see.

He couldn't have said what that meant, either. He didn't give it his hands. He didn't give it anything. He just stopped holding the door shut for a moment, and the thing behind his heart looked out through his eyes at the open end of the legion transport coming up at them, and the two images in his vision became one, and he saw the way through.

He didn't have words for how. Later, in the Chamber, an old man would give him words, and the words would be Sense and Edge and the number a Flamen writes next to them, and he would say, that's not what it was like. What it was like was that the transport's dead interior stopped being darkness with things in it and became a shape. A whole shape, all at once, every deck and housing and hanging cable and the width of every gap, as if he'd been born inside it and knew where the furniture was.

The Kestrel went in.

He took it through a gap between two drive housings that Bram would swear, for the rest of his life, had not been wide enough, and around a hanging mass of cable that would have taken the cabin off, and down, into the belly of the dead ship, and cut the drive, and cut the jets, and cut the lights, and the barge drifted on into the dark on what it had, and stopped against something with a bump that everybody in the cabin felt in their teeth.

And then it was quiet.

Outside, far off, through the hull, they heard the Vigilant put three more rounds into the ring plane at nothing, and then stop.

---

They waited nine hours.

The cutter went around the transport twice. They heard it. A patrol cutter has a drive you can hear through a hull at a kilometre, if the hull is quiet enough, and the Kestrel was as quiet as a barge gets, with the drive cold and the heaters off and four people in the cabin breathing as little as they could. The second time around it slowed. It sat off the open end of the transport for what Marchand's watch said was fifty minutes, and Jonah sat in the pilot's seat with his hands in his lap, not on the yoke, because he wasn't sure what his hands would do, and looked at the dark.

Then it went. They heard it go up out of the ring plane and away, and the sound went with it, and after that there was nothing.

"They'll be back," Marchand said.

"Yes."

"They'll sit at the edge and wait. Ochoa said it. They can sit for a month."

"Ochoa said they'd sit for a month if they knew we were coming out. They think we're dead, Silas. They put a round through the aft ring and watched us go into the biggest wreck in the plane with a cutter on our tail and no way out. Nobody comes out of that. That's what their gunner's writing down right now. Barge destroyed, presumed, all hands."

"Presumed."

"It's a lot of ring. They're not going to search it for a Collar barge. They'll write it up and they'll go home, and the report will say a decurion boarded a salvage barge and there was a hostile lorica aboard and the barge was destroyed with all hands. And that'll be that."

"A hostile lorica." Marchand was looking at him. He'd been looking at him for nine hours. "On a Collar barge."

"That's what the marines saw."

"That's what I saw."

Jonah didn't answer.

"I saw it, Vale. I was in the hatch. I saw you go down on your knee and I saw him say the word and I saw you stop, and then I saw." Marchand stopped. His hands were folded on the manifest, which he'd picked up off the hold deck at some point and had not let go of since, and they were very steady, which was how Jonah knew he was frightened. "What are you?"

"I don't know."

"Don't."

"I don't. I put my hand in the box because the captain told me not to let anybody touch it and the man who'd just killed him was about to touch it. That's all I decided. Then it decided the rest."

"It."

"Whatever was in the box."

"Where is it now?"

Jonah looked at his hands. The plate had stopped coming out of them around the third hour. The fever hadn't. He could feel it in his teeth.

"In me," he said.

Marchand didn't say anything for a while.

"He was a decurion," he said at last. "Equestrian order. Fourteen years' service, it'll be in his record. Do you know what they do to a peregrine who kills a Stripe?"

"I've an idea."

"You don't. You have an idea of what they do to a peregrine who hits a Stripe. There's no page for the other thing. There's no page because it doesn't happen. A collar doesn't let it happen. It's not a crime, Vale, it's not on the list, it's a thing that can't be, and when a thing that can't be happens they don't have a punishment for it, they have Flamens, and the Flamens have a knife." He unfolded his hands and looked at them and folded them again. "And it won't only be you. It'll be the crew. It'll be Morrow. It'll be the yard."

"I know."

"So what are we going to do?"

That was the question. Jonah had been sitting with it for nine hours and had stopped, at some point, thinking about the cutter, and started thinking about that.

"We're going to go home," he said. "We're going to go home with a dead captain and a hole in the aft ring, and the hole's going to look like a debris strike, because Bram's going to get out on the hull with a torch and make it look like one, and the captain's going to have died in the same strike, and nobody boarded us, because who boards a barge in the rings, and there was no decurion, and there was no box." He looked at Marchand. "And you're going to write that down, because it's your name on the report."

"They'll read us."

"They'll read a barge crew. Two Collar cutters, a Collar pilot, and a Blank purser. They read us every time we dock. They'll get exactly what they get every time."

"Not from you."

"Yes," Jonah said. "From me."

He didn't know if it was true. He was about to find out.

---

He went down into the hold alone.

Ochoa was where he'd left him. Bram had put a blanket over him, one of the grey ones from the crew locker, and had put his hands on his chest, which Jonah thought Ochoa would have found sentimental. The blood had gone dark. The hold smelled of it.

Varro was where he'd left him too. Nobody had put a blanket on Varro.

Jonah crouched beside the decurion and looked at him for a while. The face was still surprised. The fang had gone back into the arm as the plate came off him, the way it was supposed to, and the arm was just an arm, and on the inside of the forearm the narrow stripe sat on the skin like a thing painted on.

The thing behind Jonah's heart leaned toward it.

He'd known it would. He'd felt it looking at Varro nine hours ago, in the seconds after, and he'd said no, out loud, and it had stopped doing anything about it and not stopped wanting. For nine hours in the dark it had wanted. Not the way a man wants a meal. The way a fire wants what's next to it. There was nothing in it that was cruel. It was just what it was, and what it was was hungry, and there was a lorica on the deck in front of it that belonged to nobody now.

"He was going to kill me," Jonah said, to the hold. "He'd have taken the hand off and then he'd have decided I wasn't worth the paperwork."

The thing didn't care. He hadn't expected it to.

"And he's going out the airlock either way. Into the rings. Him and his stripe. Nobody's going to find him."

He put his hand on Varro's arm.

He hadn't meant to. That was the truth and he would tell it to the old man with the stick, years later, and the old man would nod as if it was the answer he'd expected. He'd meant to argue with himself a while longer. He'd meant to decide. But the hand went down on the stripe the way his hand had gone into the box, before the deciding was done, and the thing behind his heart went out through his palm and into the dead man's arm, and Jonah felt it begin to eat.

It was not fast. He had thought it would be fast. It went into the stripe and found the lorica there, the cutting, the dog-brained loyal thing that had lived in Varro for fourteen years and put a blade in his hand on command and had just, an hour ago by its own reckoning, watched its man die and had nothing to do about it. It found that, and it did not tear it. It took it apart. Jonah felt the pieces come up his arm one at a time, and each of them was a small dead want, a wish to stand, a wish to strike, a wish to be told what to do, and each of them came into him and was folded away somewhere, and the folding felt like being fed.

And something else came with it.

He felt it arrive and did not understand it, and then he did. He'd stood in Founding Square at fourteen and watched a Thinstripe from a visiting cutter sharpen against Corin Aldane, steel knives, unplated, for the crowd, and he'd watched the Stripe's feet. He'd never forgotten them. The way the man had not seemed to move his feet at all and had never once been where Corin's knife went. And now he had that. It was in his legs. It was in the small of his back. It sat there, patient and certain, a way of standing that he had never learned, and under it, faint, folded in with the rest, was a feeling he recognised from the wrong side. It was the feeling of looking at a Collar. It was contempt, easy and unexamined, the kind you don't know you're carrying because you've never had to put it down, and it was in him now, and it was looking at him.

He took his hand off the arm.

The stripe was gone. There was skin there, pale, with a shape on it like a stripe that had been rubbed off with a thumb.

Jonah sat back against the hold bulkhead and breathed for a while.

The fever had changed. It was still there, but it had gone from a thing in his teeth to a thing all through him, evenly, like heat from a stove, and under the heat something had settled. He could feel his hands. He knew, without looking, where the plate would come out if he asked it to, and that it would come out where he asked, mostly, and that the crooked fang was on the wrong side of his wrist and would stay there because that was where it had learned to be. He felt, for the first time since the hold deck, like the thing in him and the body it was in were arguing about the same house.

Above him, the cabin hatch was open. He hadn't heard it open. He looked up and it was open, a hand's width, and nobody was in it, and then he heard the second seat creak.

He didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that would make it unseen.

---

Bram went out on the hull with a torch and the fever went with Jonah into the pilot's seat, and between them they took the Kestrel out of the transport and out of the rings and across eleven hours of nothing toward a moon that looked, from the outside, like a rock somebody had spilled oil on.

Varro went into the rings before they left the transport. Jonah did it himself. He took the decurion to the airlock and put him in it and cycled it, and watched through the port as the body went out into the dark inside the dead ship, turning slowly, and became one more thing in a ring plane full of things. He didn't say anything over it. He thought he should and found he had nothing, and then found that he did, and that it was the same thing the crowd said when a Stripe won a bout in Founding Square, and he didn't say that either.

Ochoa stayed. Ochoa was going to the melt, because he'd been born a Collar and had died one, whatever the paper said, and the melt was where Collars went. Bram wrapped him properly. Hollis stopped saying "he just did it" around the fourth hour and started crying instead, which was better.

Nobody talked to Jonah. Marchand sat in the second seat with the manifest and wrote in it, for a long time, in his small clerk's hand, and when he'd finished he read it back to himself twice, silently, moving his lips, and then closed it and held it and looked out at the dark, and the three of them who had been in the hold when Jonah went down there did not look at Jonah at all.

He didn't blame them. He didn't much want to look at himself.

The collar was the last thing.

He'd forgotten it. It was a thing you forgot, the way you forgot your own neck. And then somewhere in the eighth hour he'd put his hand up to his throat without thinking and felt the ring there, cold, and remembered that it was nothing. That the thing in him had eaten it in the first second of the bond and left the shell. That every inspector on Anvil would look at it and see a collar, and any one of them could say the word, and nothing would happen.

That was going to be a problem. He could see it coming the way he could see debris. Not this docking. Maybe not the next. But one day somebody with a stripe was going to say the word to him, in a room with other people in it, and his knees were going to stay straight.

He put the thought away with the rest. There were a lot of things in there now.

---

Anvil came up on the screen. The yards first, on the sunward side, a hundred kilometres of broken ships laid out on grey rock like a fleet that had lost a war with the ground, and then the lights of the dock ring, and then the docking beacon, and then, on the open channel, the voice of a dock inspector asking for the Kestrel's manifest and the citizen of record.

Marchand gave it. His voice was steady and dull and exactly right. Debris strike in the outer rings. Captain lost. Aft cargo ring damaged, request a yard slot. No salvage. No boarding. Four aboard, three peregrine, one citizen, the citizen speaking.

"Reading on docking," the inspector said, and cut the channel.

Jonah brought the barge in.

The reading was a Blank with a grade you could count on one hand and a wand that he waved at each of them in turn on the dock, bored, the way they always did it, and Jonah stood in the line with Bram on one side and Hollis on the other and thought, very hard, about nothing.

He hadn't known that would work. He'd thought about it for eleven hours and what he'd decided was that the thing in him had gone still, in the hold, when Varro said the word to the collar, and that it had gone still because it was listening, and that maybe it could be asked to be still now. So he asked. He didn't do it with words. He just held it, the way you hold your breath, and it held, and it was the strangest feeling he'd ever had, like holding a second heart still inside the first.

The wand came past his throat and his chest and his hands and went on to Hollis.

"Peregrine, peregrine, peregrine," the Blank said, writing. "Citizen. Clear. Sorry about your captain." He didn't sound sorry. He didn't sound anything. He went away.

Jonah let go, and nearly sat down on the dock.

Morrow was there.

He'd been there the whole time, at the back, behind the inspector, the way a yard owner had to stand when the Empire was reading his crew, and now he came forward, big and grey and tired in a coat that had been good once, and looked at the wrapped shape that Bram and Hollis were carrying between them, and put his hand on it, and kept it there.

"Debris," he said.

"Debris," Marchand said.

Morrow looked at Jonah. Jonah looked back. Neither of them said anything, and Morrow's hand stayed on the blanket, and after a moment he nodded once, slowly, the way a man nods at a hull that's come apart faster than the numbers said it should.

"Take him to the yard house," Morrow said. "I'll speak to his people. Silas, the manifest, my office, now. Jonah."

"Sir."

"Go home. You look like death. You'll come to my office tomorrow and we'll talk about the petition, because I'm not stopping it for this, do you hear me, I'm not stopping it for this, the man would've cut my ears off." His voice caught, and he let it, and went on. "Tomorrow. Go home."

Jonah went.

Tom was at the dock gate. He'd have been there since the beacon; he was always there, in his good coat, with his hat in his hand for the inspector, and his face when he saw Jonah come through the gate on his own feet did something that Jonah could not, at that moment, afford to look at for long.

And Mara was beside him.

She came forward. She had her hair tied back for the yards and burn marks on both forearms from the torches and she was smiling with her whole face, and she came at him to put her arms around him the way she always did, and he stepped back.

He didn't mean to. His body did it. There was a thing in him that had come out of his hands on a yoke that morning with no warning at all, and it was hungry, and he did not know what it would do if she touched him, and his body put a step between them before he could think.

Mara stopped.

She looked at him. Her smile stayed where it was, but something behind it went careful.

"Jonah?"

"I'm all right." He heard his own voice and it sounded like a stranger doing an impression of him. "I'm all right. Ochoa's dead. I'll tell you. I'll tell you all of it, I just." He looked at his hands. "Not yet. Let me. Not yet."

She looked at his hands too. Then she looked back at his face, and after a moment she nodded, and did not come closer, and that was when he knew she was going to be the hardest one to lie to.

Tom put a hand on his shoulder. It was fine. Nothing came out.

"Come home," his father said. "Come home, son. Whatever it is, come home first."

They walked. Jonah had a stone box in his kit that was empty and a sealed packet inside his suit that was not, and a fever, and a dead man's feet, and something behind his heart that had eaten a Thinstripe and was already, faintly, hungry again.

At the top of the dock ramp he looked back once.

Marchand was standing where the inspector had left him, with the manifest under his arm, looking after the three of them. When he saw Jonah look, he didn't look away. He raised his voice just enough to carry.

"I didn't see anything," he said. "Captain."

And then he turned and went to find Morrow's office.
