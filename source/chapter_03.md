# Chapter 3: Sharpening

Founding Day on Anvil fell on the eighty-seventh anniversary of the day the Empire had taken Anvil, because somebody in the City had thought that was funny.

Jonah had thought it was funny too, once, when he was twelve and had worked out that the date on the banners was the date in the old yard ledgers. He had said so, loudly, in Founding Square. Tom had put a hand over his mouth so fast that Jonah bit his own tongue. Nobody had heard. Or somebody had, and had decided a twelve-year-old with a collar wasn't worth the walk. He'd learned two things that day. The Empire had a sense of humour. His father didn't, not about this.

The square was full by the second bell. It was the only day of the year the yards shut. Eighty thousand Collars, four thousand Blanks, and the two hundred Stripes who ran the moon, crammed under the rock dome with the sun-lamps turned up to festival. Purple and white banners hung from the gantries. In the middle of it all was the sharpening ring, a circle of sand a dozen paces across with a low rail around it.

Three weeks since the rings. Ochoa had gone to the melt on the fourth day. Morrow had spoken over him and had said his name right, once, clearly, and had not looked at Jonah while he did it. Then the furnace had taken the collar and the body both. Jonah had stood with Bram, Hollis, Mara and his father and had not been able to think of anything to say. That was a first.

Then he had gone home and learned what he was.

It had taken three weeks and it was not finished. He had done it at night, in the back room of his father's two rooms in the tenements, with the door wedged and a blanket over the vent. The plate made a small wet click when it came, and the walls in the tenements were thin. He had learned to bring it out where he wanted. Mostly. He had learned to bring the fang out of the back of his right wrist on purpose, crooked, and to put it away. He had learned to hold the whole thing still inside him as he had on the dock, for longer now, an hour, two, until his teeth ached. He had learned that it was hungry all the time, and that the hunger went up and down. When it was up he could feel it looking at the Blanks in the yard as it had looked at Varro.

He had not learned what it was. Lying on his back in the dark, he had asked it, out loud, in a whisper: what are you. It had listened with great attention and said nothing at all.

He hadn't told Mara. He had told her the boarding, Ochoa, Varro, the fake strike, Marchand. She had listened with her hands flat on the table and her face very still. Then she had said, and the rest. He had said, that's all of it. She had looked at him for a long moment and said, all right. That was worse than being called a liar. She'd let him have it, and they both knew he was carrying it.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what."

"Standing like you're waiting to be hit." She was beside him at the rail, in her festival clothes, which were her yard clothes with the burns turned inward. "Nobody's going to hit you. It's Founding Day. They only hit us on the other three hundred and sixty-four."

"That's a beautiful thought. Somebody should put it on a banner."

"Somebody should put you on a banner. Look at you. Three weeks and you're standing like a Stripe."

He made himself stand like something else.

She was right. He'd caught himself doing it in the yards. Weight where Varro would have put it, feet a little apart, one a little back, not knowing until a Blank foreman looked at him twice. The feet weren't his. They'd come up his arm out of a dead man in a hold. They had opinions about where he should stand, and the opinions were good, and that was the worst part.

"Corin's up," Mara said.

---

The Lesser Ludi were four bouts. Collar against Collar, steel knives, unplated, in the sand, with the crowd on its feet. Up on the dais under the purple banners the Stripes watched with the faces of people who had paid for something and were waiting to see if it was worth it. The winner of the four went on the auxiliary rolls. That was the prize. A Collar who won in the sand on Founding Day got a ship off Anvil, a uniform, a rifle. He got the chance, one day, if he lived and killed enough of the right people in the right wars, to be given a cutting and a thin purple stripe. He got to stand on a dais on some other moon and watch other Collars fight in the sand.

Most of them died in the wars before that. Everybody knew it. They went anyway. Jonah had never understood it, and had said so, once, to Corin. Corin had said, then you've never been afraid enough.

The Greater Ludi were not for Collars. Stripe against Stripe, plated, fangs out, to the death unless the loser gave up his stripe first. The winner ate the loser's lorica. That was the law, the Lex Spolia, and it was the only ladder in the Empire that went up. A genius that ate another took about a fifth of what it ate, so a Thinstripe of thirty who killed a Thinstripe of forty walked off the sand at thirty-eight. Every house in the City had climbed that way. Every Collar on Anvil knew the arithmetic and would never be let near it.

Corin came into the ring against a cutter from the north yards, a big man, older, with a torch grip on his knife.

Jonah watched Corin's feet.

He hadn't meant to. Corin set himself, and something in Jonah's legs had an opinion about the set. Too square. Weight too even. A man who stood like that could go either way and would go neither fast enough. The cutter's set was worse. The feet were bored already. Under the boredom was the other thing that had come folded in with them, the easy contempt, looking at two Collars in the sand and thinking, look at them.

Jonah bit the inside of his cheek hard enough to taste it.

Corin won. He won well, if you had your own eyes and not a dead man's. He took the cutter's first rush on his forearm and bled and didn't look at it. He went in low and fast, got the knife under the bigger man's chin, and held it there. The cutter dropped his own knife in the sand and the crowd made the sound a crowd makes. Corin didn't cut him. He didn't have to. The bout was won at the throat and everybody knew it.

Corin stood up in the sand with the cutter's blood on his forearm and his own on his shirt. He looked first, not at the dais, where his name was going onto a roll, but at the rail, where Mara was.

Mara was clapping. She had her whole face in it.

Jonah watched her clap and thought about nothing, hard. He could do that now. He'd learned it in the dark with a blanket over the vent, and it was useful for more than readings.

"He's good," he said.

"He's always been good," Mara said. "You never watched."

"I'm watching now."

"I know," she said, and didn't look at him. "That's what's strange."

---

The sharpening came after.

It always came after. The Lesser Ludi were for the Collars and the sharpening was for the Stripes. In the Ludi two Collars fought each other for a place on a roll. In the sharpening a Stripe called a Collar out of the crowd and fought him with steel, unplated, for practice, and the Collar could not say no.

That was the law. Jonah had looked it up once, in the yard office, in a book Morrow was not supposed to have. Four lines. He could still say them. A bearer of the equestrian or senatorial order may call any citizen or peregrine to sharpen, without plate, with steel, for the improvement of the art. The called may not decline. Death in the sharpening is no crime. And underneath, in a different hand, somebody's note: for the improvement of the art.

The cadets were in the ring. Six of them, from the garrison, young Thinstripes in the grey of the auxiliary school with their sleeves rolled and the narrow stripes fresh on their arms. They walked the sand in a loose circle and looked over the crowd. Their instructor stood by the rail, a Thinstripe in his forties with a shut face.

Up on the dais, the quaestor watched.

Everyone had seen him before. Marcus Vibius Priscus had come to Anvil a year ago to hold the office of quaestor for the province. The governor, whoever the governor was, never came, so Priscus was the Empire on this moon. He was thirty or so. He sat very still. His sleeve was rolled, and the stripe on his forearm was broad, a band of purple as wide as three fingers. The cadets' stripes were scratches beside it. He had the face of a man reading a document he already knew the end of.

Beside him sat a young woman with the same stripe.

Jonah hadn't seen her before. She had the quaestor's colouring and none of his stillness. She sat with one boot up on the rail of the dais, which nobody else on the dais was doing. She watched the cadets circle with the exact face Jonah made when Marchand talked about fuel.

"That's the sister," Mara said, low. "Livia. She came out with him. They say she's a better blade than half the garrison and won't fight any of them."

"Why not?"

"Because she's a Broadstripe and they're not. Or because she doesn't like it. Nobody knows. She goes to the Flamens' house and reads. Every day, all day. A Broadstripe." Mara shook her head. "You'd think she'd have something better to do."

"Like sit on a dais and watch children stab us."

"Jonah."

"I'm just saying it's a full day."

One of the cadets had stopped circling. A tall boy with a soft face and a stripe so new it still looked painted. He was looking straight at the rail where Jonah was. Jonah understood that he had said the last thing a little too loudly.

The cadet pointed.

"Him."

The crowd went quiet around Jonah. He felt Mara's hand close on his arm and let go, because holding him would only make it worse.

"The one with the mouth," the cadet said, and grinned. "Come on, then. For the improvement of the art."

---

There was a form to it. There was a form to everything the Empire did to you, and the form was the point.

He went over the rail. A Blank in garrison grey handed him a knife, hilt first. It was a good knife, better than anything in the yards. They wanted him armed properly, because that was what made it sharpening and not slaughter. The cadet was in the middle of the sand with his own knife held low, shifting his feet, smiling. The instructor had come to the rail with his arms folded. The other five cadets had gone to the edges.

And the crowd said the prayer.

Mars Pater, eighty thousand people said, which was all most of them knew. The Blanks went on with the rest of the old words, Mars Pater te precor. Up on the dais the quaestor's lips moved with it. The cadet in the sand said it with his eyes on Jonah. It was the prayer they said before the sand, and Collars were not permitted to say it.

Jonah said it.

He said all of it, in the old Latin, as it was written in the front of the manifests that came off Flamen cutters. He said it clearly, in a voice that carried, and he got the endings right.

The cadet's smile went. The instructor's arms came unfolded. Up on the dais the quaestor did not move at all. The woman beside him took her boot off the rail and leaned forward and stared. Eighty thousand people did not know whether to laugh. He could feel them not knowing.

Then the cadet came at him, and the feet took over.

He would tell the old man about it later, in the Chamber. The old man would ask how long it took, and he would say nine seconds. The old man would say, then it took no time at all, and you were not there for it. Which was true. The cadet came in with a fast low thrust meant to open his belly. A good thrust, well taught, drilled into a boy for a year. Jonah's feet had already left. They didn't go back. Back was where the thrust expected him. They went left, and a little forward. The cadet's knife went past his hip close enough to feel the wind of it. Jonah's left hand closed on the cadet's wrist. His right hand put the good knife at the cadet's throat.

He stopped it there.

So close the boy would have a red line under his jaw for a week. He had not decided to stop. The feet had put him there and the hand had put the knife there. Something else had stopped it. The part of him that had grown up in this square, that had watched Corin stop his knife at a cutter's chin an hour ago and thought, that's how you win without becoming them.

The cadet stood very still with a knife at his throat and a Collar's hand on his wrist. His face was white. He had the eyes of a boy who has just been told something about the world that he will spend the rest of his life trying to unlearn.

The crowd did not make the sound.

Eighty thousand people and nobody moved. Jonah stood in the sand with the cadet's pulse going under his thumb, and thought, clearly, oh, and let him go.

He stepped back. He turned the knife around and held it out, hilt first, to the Blank at the rail. The Blank did not take it. Jonah put it down in the sand.

"Hold him."

It was the instructor. He had come over the rail. Two of the cadets were already moving. Jonah felt their hands on his arms and let them. He didn't know what would come out of him if he didn't.

"Fifty," the instructor said. "For insolence in the sand. Post."

"He won," somebody said.

Not loud. A woman's voice, from the dais, in the tone of a person correcting a sum. It stopped the two cadets holding Jonah as surely as a word to a collar. The quaestor's sister was on her feet with her hands flat on the rail of the dais, looking at the instructor, not at Jonah. She said it again, slightly louder.

"He won. He was called, he took the knife, he said the prayer, he won. What's insolent?"

The instructor opened his mouth.

"The boy won."

The quaestor had not stood up. He had not, as far as Jonah could see, moved. But his voice came down off the dais level and dry and carrying without effort, and the square went a different kind of quiet.

"He was called under the law. He fought under the law. He stopped, which is more than the law requires, and which I note." Priscus turned his head, finally, and looked at the instructor. "Your cadet was put on his back by a peregrine with a kitchen knife in front of the province. That is not insolence. That is instruction. Whose?"

Nobody answered.

"Fifty," Priscus said. "The instructor. Post."

He looked away, back at the ring. The two cadets let go of Jonah's arms fast. The instructor stood in the sand with a face Jonah would remember for a long time. The quaestor's sister sat back down, put her boot back up on the rail, and did not look at Jonah at all.

Jonah went back over the rail.

The crowd made the sound, then. It came late and strange and not quite for him, but it came. He walked through it to Mara. She looked at him, and did not touch him, and said, "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody."

"Jonah."

"Nobody taught me. I've got quick feet. Everybody's always said."

"Nobody's ever said that. Not once. I've known you since you were six."

He didn't have anything for that. He stood beside her with his heart going and the thing behind it going too. Not hunger. A warm satisfaction. The feet were pleased. A dead man's way of standing had just been used on a Stripe, in the sand, and it had liked that very much.

Something clicked. Small, inside. He would learn later what it was. Right then he only knew that his hands were steadier than they'd been in three weeks.

---

He found the edge of the square after.

On the east side the rock dome met the yard road and the gantries opened out. You could see down through the pressure glass to the surface, the hundred kilometres of broken ships under the hard little sun. He had gone there since he was a boy when the square was too much. He stood with his hands on the rail and breathed.

"Where does a peregrine learn a Fulvian step?"

He didn't turn around at once. He knew the voice. He'd heard it correct a sum in front of eighty thousand people an hour ago.

When he did turn, she was three paces away, alone, with her hands in the pockets of a coat that had cost more than the Kestrel. The broad stripe was on her forearm where the sleeve was pushed back. Her head was on one side.

"I don't know what that is," he said.

"Yes, you do. You did one. Left and forward, off the line, on a low thrust. It's the first step they teach in House Fulvia and it's the only step that boy's instructor knows. You did it better than the instructor, and I want to know where a Collar barge pilot learned it."

"Watched it once. I'm quick."

"Watched it where?"

"Here. In the sand. I was fourteen."

She gave him a considering look. The look of somebody who fought people for a living and was trying to work out where his weight was.

"That's a lie," she said, "but it's a good one, and you told it well, so I'll let you keep it." She took a hand out of a pocket and pushed her hair back. "You should have cut him."

"Why?"

"Because now he'll remember you. If you'd cut him he'd remember the cut. Now he'll remember your face. That's worse. For you, I mean. Not for him."

"I'll try to be uglier next time."

That got something. Not a laugh. The corner of her mouth, which she seemed to notice and put back.

"You said the prayer," she said.

"Everyone says the prayer."

"Not everyone. You know that. You knew it when you said it. You said it in better Latin than my brother's clerk, with the endings right, in front of the whole province, on purpose. You'd been called and you wanted them to know that you knew what they were doing." She tilted her head the other way. "Do you know what would have happened if my brother hadn't been on the dais?"

"Fifty. Post."

"Two hundred. And the boy would have cut your throat in the sand afterward. The instructor would have written it up as the improvement of the art, and it would have been true." She said it flatly. "I'm not telling you that to frighten you. I'm telling you because you seem to be somebody who'd rather know."

"I'd rather know."

"Then know it." She put her hand back in her pocket. "And stop standing like that. You stand like a Stripe. Somebody's going to notice."

"Somebody did."

"Somebody who reads a great deal and is very bored," she said, "and who is going to go and read some more, because that's what she does, and forget about barge pilots. Good day."

She turned to go.

"Why did you say it?" Jonah said.

She stopped.

"On the dais. He won. You said it before he did."

She looked at him over her shoulder. For a moment the boredom was gone from her face and there was something else there, something he couldn't name and would think about for a long time in a dark room under a planetoid. Then it was back.

"Because it was true," she said, "and nobody else was going to. That's usually why."

She walked away up the yard road toward the Flamens' house and did not look back. He realised he hadn't told her his name, and that she hadn't asked. He didn't know whether that was good.

---

Mara found him there ten minutes later. She came up the road with something in her fist and the whole-face look on, the one he hadn't seen since the dock. She stopped in front of him and opened her hand.

A ring. Hull ring, cut from plate, a band of dull grey metal that had been part of something that flew, once. It had a bevel where the torch had gone through.

"The Halcyon," she said. "First ship you ever broke. I asked Morrow which and he found it in the book. You were fifteen. You cut the plate for the aft seam and Bram said you cried afterward because it was a beautiful ship."

"Bram's a liar."

"Bram's a liar." She was still holding it out. "Well?"

He looked at it. He looked at her. Behind his heart was something that had eaten a Thinstripe and was hungry again, and an hour ago had been pleased about putting a knife to a boy's throat. He had not told her, and she knew he had not told her. She was standing in front of him with a piece of the first ship he'd ever broken in her hand.

"I'm not what I was three weeks ago," he said.

"I know."

"I don't know what I am."

"I know that too." She didn't move. "I'm not asking what you are. I'm asking if you'll wear the ring."

He took it. He put it on. It fit, because she'd measured his hand in his sleep, probably, years ago, because that was what she was like. He looked at it on his finger and then up at her. She was smiling. He put his arms around her, and nothing came out of him at all, and he held on.

Over her shoulder, on the yard road, the quaestor's sister had stopped.

She was fifty paces off. She'd turned, for whatever reason people turn, and was standing very still with her hands in her pockets and her head a little on one side, watching. When she saw that Jonah had seen her she did not look away and did not look embarrassed. She looked exactly as bored as she had on the dais.

Then she went on up the road.

---

Morrow made the announcement at the fourth bell, from the yard office steps, to a crowd of Collars who had come because Morrow was announcing something and that was usually worth hearing.

The petition had been granted. Jonah Vale, peregrine, of Anvil, had served six years as pilot of the barge Kestrel out of the Morrow yard, and had brought that barge home with its captain lost and its crew alive. He was to be manumitted by order of the quaestor of the province and enrolled as a citizen of the Republic, with investiture to follow on the last day of Founding Week, and to hold the captaincy of the Kestrel thereafter.

The crowd made the sound. It was a good sound, this time, and it was for him. Jonah stood on the steps beside Morrow with his father's hand crushing his shoulder. For a moment he felt exactly what he was supposed to feel.

Then he saw Marchand.

The purser was at the edge of the crowd with his manifest under his arm. He was not clapping. His face was doing nothing at all. Marchand had been the citizen of record on the Kestrel for six years, the Blank whose name went on the report. He had been passed over for the captaincy when Ochoa took it under Morrow's dispensation. He had been told, because Ochoa had told him, that it would come to him when Ochoa was done.

Ochoa was done. The captaincy was going to a Collar. The Collar was going to be a citizen in a week, with a mark on his arm, and Marchand was going to be his purser.

Jonah looked at him across the crowd and Marchand looked back. Neither said anything. Then Marchand turned and went into the crowd. Beside him, going the same way, with the cutter's blood still on his shirt and his name on a roll, was Corin.

Corin didn't look at Jonah. He was looking at the rail, where Mara stood with her hands over her mouth and the whole of her face in her eyes.

Up on the dais, the quaestor rose. The reading had been taken, the signatures made, the day's law done. The dais stood with him. He came down the steps with his household behind him and crossed the square toward the Flamens' house. As he passed the yard office he turned his head, once, and looked at Jonah on the steps.

A short look. The look a man gives a line in a ledger that has been added correctly and that he does not expect to see again.

Jonah looked back. In his hand the ring was warm. In his legs a dead man's feet were pleased. And behind his heart, quietly, the thing that had come out of a box looked at the broad purple stripe on the quaestor's arm as he passed. For the first time since Varro, it was not hungry at all.

It was paying attention.
