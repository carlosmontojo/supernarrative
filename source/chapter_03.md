# Chapter 3: Sharpening

Founding Day on Anvil fell on the eighty-seventh anniversary of the day the Empire had taken Anvil, because somebody in the City had thought that was funny.

Jonah had thought it was funny too, once, when he was twelve and had first worked out that the date on the banners was the date in the old yard ledgers, and had said so, loudly, in Founding Square, and Tom had put a hand over his mouth so fast that Jonah bit his own tongue. Nobody had heard. Or somebody had, and had decided a twelve-year-old with a collar wasn't worth the walk. Either way he'd learned two things that day, which were that the Empire had a sense of humour and that his father didn't, not about this, and he'd carried both of them since.

The square was full by the second bell. It was the only day of the year the yards shut. Eighty thousand Collars and four thousand Blanks and the two hundred Stripes who ran the moon, all of them crammed under the rock dome of the square with the sun-lamps turned up to festival and the banners hanging from the gantries, purple and white, and in the middle of it all the sharpening ring, which was a circle of sand a dozen paces across with a low rail around it, and which was the whole point.

Three weeks since the rings. Ochoa had gone to the melt on the fourth day. Morrow had spoken over him and had said his name right, once, clearly, and had not looked at Jonah while he did it, and then the furnace had taken the collar and the body both and that was Ochoa, twice over, done. Jonah had stood with Bram and Hollis and Mara and his father and had not been able to think of anything to say, which was the first time in his life that had happened to him.

Then he had gone home and learned what he was.

It had taken three weeks and it was not finished. He had done it at night, in the back room of his father's two rooms in the tenements, with the door wedged and a blanket over the vent, because the plate made a sound when it came, a small wet click like the one it had made on the yoke, and the walls in the tenements were thin. He had learned to bring it out where he wanted. Mostly. He had learned to bring the fang out of the back of his right wrist on purpose, crooked, and to put it away, and to hold the whole thing still inside him the way he had on the dock, for longer now, an hour, two, until his teeth ached with it. He had learned that it was hungry all the time, that the hunger went up and down like a tide, and that when it was up he could feel it looking at the Blanks in the yard the way it had looked at Varro.

He had not learned what it was. He had asked it. Lying on his back in the dark with the blanket over the vent, he had asked it, out loud, in a whisper, what are you, and it had done what it always did, which was listen with great attention and say nothing at all.

He hadn't told Mara. That was the other thing he had learned. He had told her the boarding, and Ochoa, and Varro, and the fake strike, and Marchand, and she had listened to all of it with her hands flat on the table and her face very still, and then she had said, and the rest, and he had said, that's all of it, and she had looked at him for a long moment and said, all right. And that was worse than if she'd called him a liar, because she'd let him have it, and he had to carry it now, and she knew he was carrying it.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what."

"Standing like you're waiting to be hit." She was beside him at the rail, in her festival clothes, which were her yard clothes with the burns turned inward. "Nobody's going to hit you. It's Founding Day. They only hit us on the other three hundred and sixty-four."

"That's a beautiful thought. Somebody should put it on a banner."

"Somebody should put you on a banner. Look at you. Three weeks and you're standing like a Stripe."

He made himself stand like something else.

She was right, was the problem. He'd caught himself doing it in the yards. Standing with his weight where Varro would have put it, feet a little apart, one a little back, and not knowing he was doing it until he saw a Blank foreman look at him twice. The feet weren't his. They'd come up his arm out of a dead man in a hold and they had opinions about where he should stand, and the opinions were good, and that was the worst part.

"Corin's up," Mara said.

---

The Lesser Ludi were four bouts. Collar against Collar, steel knives, unplated, in the sand, and the crowd on its feet, and up on the dais under the purple banners the Stripes watching with the faces of people who had paid for a thing and were waiting to see if it was worth it. The winner of the four went on the auxiliary rolls. That was the prize. A Collar who won in the sand on Founding Day got a ship off Anvil, a uniform, a rifle, and the chance, one day, if he lived and if he killed enough of the right people in the right wars, to be given a cutting and a thin purple stripe and to stand on a dais on some other moon and watch other Collars fight in the sand.

Most of them died in the wars before that. Everybody knew it. They went anyway. Jonah had never understood it, and had said so, once, to Corin, and Corin had looked at him with an expression Jonah had never seen on him before or since and had said, then you've never been afraid enough.

Corin came into the ring against a cutter from the north yards, a big man, older, with a knife he held like a torch.

Jonah watched Corin's feet.

He hadn't meant to. It happened the way the standing happened. Corin came in and set himself, and something in Jonah's legs looked at the set and had an opinion about it, and the opinion was that it was wrong. Too square. Weight too even. A man who stood like that could go either way and would go neither fast enough. And the cutter's set was worse, and the thing in Jonah's legs was bored already, and under the boredom was the other thing, the easy, unexamined thing that had come folded in with the footwork, and it was looking at two Collars in the sand and it was thinking, look at them.

Jonah bit the inside of his cheek hard enough to taste it.

Corin won. He won well, was the truth of it, if you had eyes and not a dead man's. He took the cutter's first rush on his forearm and there was blood, and he didn't look at it, and he went in low and fast and got the knife under the bigger man's chin and stopped it there, and held it, and the cutter dropped his own knife in the sand and the crowd made the sound a crowd makes. Corin didn't cut him. He didn't have to. The bout was won at the throat and everybody knew it and the cutter would go home with his blood in his eyes and tell it for years.

Corin stood up in the sand with the cutter's blood on his forearm and his own on his shirt and looked, first, not at the dais, where the reading was being taken and his name was going onto a roll, but at the rail, where Mara was.

Mara was looking at him too. She was clapping. She had her whole face in it, the way she had everything.

Jonah watched her clap and thought about nothing, hard, and found that he could do that now, and that it was a thing he'd learned in the dark with a blanket over the vent, and that it was useful for more than readings.

"He's good," he said.

"He's always been good," Mara said. "You never watched."

"I'm watching now."

"I know," she said, and didn't look at him. "That's what's strange."

---

The sharpening came after.

It always came after. The Lesser Ludi were for the Collars and the sharpening was for the Stripes, and the difference was that in the Ludi two Collars fought each other for a place on a roll, and in the sharpening a Stripe called a Collar out of the crowd and fought him with steel, unplated, for practice, and the Collar could not say no.

That was the law. Jonah had looked it up once, in the yard office, in a book that Morrow was not supposed to have, and had found it, four lines, and had read them until he could say them, and could still. A bearer of the equestrian or senatorial order may call any citizen or peregrine to sharpen, without plate, with steel, for the improvement of the art. The called may not decline. Death in the sharpening is no crime. And then, underneath, in a different hand, somebody's note: for the improvement of the art.

The cadets were in the ring. Six of them, from the garrison, young Thinstripes in the grey of the auxiliary school with their sleeves rolled and the narrow stripes fresh on their arms, walking the sand in a loose circle and looking at the crowd the way you'd look at a rack of tools. Their instructor stood by the rail, a Thinstripe in his forties with a face like a shut door.

Up on the dais, the quaestor watched.

Jonah had seen him before. Everyone had. Marcus Vibius Priscus had come to Anvil a year ago to hold the office of quaestor for the province, which meant that the governor, whoever the governor was, never came, and Priscus was the Empire on this moon. He was thirty or so. He sat very still. His sleeve was rolled like the cadets', and the stripe on his forearm was broad, a band of purple as wide as three fingers, and the cadets' stripes looked like scratches beside it. He had the face of a man reading a document that he already knew the end of.

Beside him sat a young woman with the same stripe.

Jonah hadn't seen her before. She had the quaestor's colouring and none of his stillness. She sat with one boot up on the rail of the dais, which nobody else on the dais was doing, and she was watching the cadets circle with an expression that Jonah, who had spent three weeks learning to read his own face in a piece of hull glass, recognised as the exact face he made when Marchand talked about fuel.

"That's the sister," Mara said, low. "Livia. She came out with him. They say she's a better blade than half the garrison and won't fight any of them."

"Why not?"

"Because she's a Broadstripe and they're not. Or because she doesn't like it. Nobody knows. She goes to the Flamens' house and reads. Every day, all day. A Broadstripe." Mara shook her head. "You'd think she'd have something better to do."

"Like sit on a dais and watch children stab us."

"Jonah."

"I'm just saying it's a full day."

One of the cadets had stopped circling. He was a tall boy with a soft face and a stripe so new it still looked painted, and he was looking straight at the rail where Jonah was, and Jonah understood, with the part of him that had grown up in this square, that he had said the last thing a little too loudly.

The cadet pointed.

"Him."

The crowd went quiet around Jonah the way water goes quiet around a dropped stone. He felt Mara's hand close on his arm and let go, because she knew, and because holding him would only make it worse.

"The one with the mouth," the cadet said, and grinned. "Come on, then. For the improvement of the art."

---

There was a form to it. There was a form to everything the Empire did to you, and the form was the point, because a thing with a form was a thing that had been thought about, and a thing that had been thought about was law.

He went over the rail. A Blank in the garrison grey handed him a knife, hilt first, and it was a good knife, better than anything in the yards, and he understood that they wanted him armed properly because that was what made it sharpening and not slaughter. The cadet was in the middle of the sand with his own knife held low, shifting his feet, smiling. The instructor had come to the rail and was watching with his arms folded. The other five cadets had gone to the edges.

And the crowd said the prayer.

It came up out of eighty thousand people like weather. Mars Pater, they said, which was all most of them knew, and the Blanks went on with it, the rest of the old words, Mars Pater te precor, and up on the dais the quaestor's lips moved with it and the cadets' lips moved with it and the cadet in the sand said it with his eyes on Jonah, and it was the prayer, the one they said before the sand, and Collars were not permitted to say it.

Jonah said it.

He said all of it. He said it in the old Latin the way it was written in the front of the manifests that came off Flamen cutters, because he had wanted to know what he was carrying and had learned it, and he said it clearly, in a voice that carried, and he got the endings right.

The cadet's smile went. The instructor's arms came unfolded. Up on the dais the quaestor did not move at all, and the woman beside him took her boot off the rail and leaned forward and looked at Jonah as if he had grown a second head, and the crowd, eighty thousand of them, did not know whether to laugh. He could feel them not knowing. It went through the square like a held breath.

Then the cadet came at him, and the feet took over.

He would tell the old man about it, later, in the Chamber, and the old man would ask him how long it had taken, and he would say nine seconds, and the old man would say, then it took no time at all, and you were not there for it. Which was true. He was not there for it. The cadet came in with a fast low thrust that was meant to open his belly, and it was a good thrust, well taught, the kind of thing a garrison school drills into a boy for a year, and Jonah's feet had already left. They didn't go back. Back was where the thrust expected him. They went left, and a little forward, and the cadet's knife went past his hip so close he felt the wind of it, and Jonah's left hand was on the cadet's wrist and his right hand, with the good knife in it, was at the cadet's throat.

He stopped it there.

He stopped it so close that the boy would have a red line under his jaw for a week. He had not decided to stop. He'd decided nothing. The feet had put him there and the hand had put the knife there and something else, something that had grown up in this square and had watched Corin stop his knife at a cutter's chin an hour ago and had thought, that's how it's done, that's how you win without becoming them, had stopped it.

The cadet stood very still with a knife at his throat and a Collar's hand on his wrist, and his face was white, and his eyes were on Jonah's, and they were the eyes of a boy who has just been told a thing about the world that he will spend the rest of his life trying to unlearn.

The crowd did not make the sound.

It was silent. Eighty thousand people and nobody moved, and Jonah stood in the sand with the cadet's pulse going under his thumb, fast as a bird's, and thought, clearly, oh, and let him go.

He stepped back. He turned the knife around and held it out, hilt first, to the Blank at the rail. The Blank did not take it. Jonah put it down in the sand.

"Hold him."

It was the instructor. He had come over the rail and his face had gone from a shut door to a door that somebody was kicking, and two of the cadets were already moving. Jonah felt their hands on his arms. He let them. He didn't know what would come out of him if he didn't.

"Fifty," the instructor said. "For insolence in the sand. Post."

"He won," somebody said.

It was not loud. It was a woman's voice and it came from the dais and it was said in the tone of a person correcting a sum, and it stopped the two cadets holding Jonah as surely as a word to a collar. He looked up. The quaestor's sister was on her feet. She had her hands flat on the rail of the dais and she was looking at the instructor, not at Jonah, and her face was that face, the one he made at Marchand, and she said it again, slightly louder, as if the man might not have heard.

"He won. He was called, he took the knife, he said the prayer, he won. What's insolent?"

The instructor opened his mouth.

"The boy won."

The quaestor had not stood up. He had not, as far as Jonah could see, moved. But his voice came down off the dais like a verdict, level and dry and carrying without effort, and the square went a different kind of quiet.

"He was called under the law. He fought under the law. He stopped, which is more than the law requires, and which I note." Priscus turned his head, finally, and looked at the instructor. "Your cadet was put on his back by a peregrine with a kitchen knife in front of the province. That is not insolence. That is instruction. Whose?"

Nobody answered.

"Fifty," Priscus said. "The instructor. Post."

He looked away. That was all. He looked away, back at the ring, and the two cadets let go of Jonah's arms like his skin had gone hot, and the instructor stood in the sand with a face that Jonah would remember for a long time, and up on the dais the quaestor's sister sat back down and put her boot back up on the rail, and did not look at Jonah at all.

Jonah went back over the rail.

The crowd made the sound, then. It came late and it came strange and it was not quite for him, but it came, and he walked through it to where Mara was, and she looked at him, and did not touch him, and said, "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody."

"Jonah."

"Nobody taught me. I've got quick feet. Everybody's always said."

"Nobody's ever said that. Not once. I've known you since you were six."

He didn't have anything for that. He stood beside her with his heart going and the thing behind it going too, in a way he hadn't felt before, not hunger, something else, a kind of warm satisfaction, like a dog that has been told it is good, and he understood that it was the feet. The feet were pleased. Somewhere in him, a dead man's way of standing had just been used the way it liked to be used, on a Stripe, in the sand, and it had liked it very much.

Something clicked. Small, inside, like a lock going over. He would learn later what it was. Right then he only knew that his hands were steadier than they'd been in three weeks.

---

He found the edge of the square after.

There was a place, on the east side, where the rock dome met the yard road and the gantries opened out and you could see down through the pressure glass to the surface, the hundred kilometres of broken ships laid out under the hard little sun, and it was where he had gone since he was a boy when the square was too much. He went there now. He stood with his hands on the rail and looked at the ships and breathed.

"Where does a peregrine learn a Fulvian step?"

He didn't turn around at once. He knew the voice. He'd heard it correct a sum in front of eighty thousand people an hour ago.

When he did turn, she was three paces away, alone, with her hands in the pockets of a coat that had cost more than the Kestrel, and the broad stripe on her forearm where the sleeve was pushed back, and her head on one side.

"I don't know what that is," he said.

"Yes, you do. You did one. Left and forward, off the line, on a low thrust. It's the first step they teach in House Fulvia and it's the only step that boy's instructor knows, and you did it better than the instructor, and I want to know where a Collar barge pilot learned it."

"Watched it once. I'm quick."

"Watched it where?"

"Here. In the sand. I was fourteen."

She looked at him for a moment. It was a considering look. It was, he thought, the look of somebody who fought people for a living and was trying to work out where his weight was.

"That's a lie," she said, "but it's a good one, and you told it well, so I'll let you keep it." She took a hand out of a pocket and pushed her hair back. "You should have cut him."

"Why?"

"Because now he'll remember you. If you'd cut him he'd remember the cut. Now he'll remember your face. That's worse. For you, I mean. Not for him."

"I'll try to be uglier next time."

That got something. Not a laugh. The corner of her mouth, which she seemed to notice and put back.

"You said the prayer," she said.

"Everyone says the prayer."

"Not everyone. You know that. You knew it when you said it. You said it in better Latin than my brother's clerk, with the endings right, in front of the whole province, on purpose, because you'd been called and you wanted them to know that you knew what they were doing." She tilted her head the other way. "Do you know what would have happened if my brother hadn't been on the dais?"

"Fifty. Post."

"Two hundred. And the boy would have cut your throat in the sand afterward, and the instructor would have written it up as the improvement of the art, and it would have been true." She said it flatly, the way you'd say the fuel was at thirty-one. "I'm not telling you that to frighten you. I'm telling you because you seem to be somebody who'd rather know."

"I'd rather know."

"Then know it." She put her hand back in her pocket. "And stop standing like that. You stand like a Stripe. Somebody's going to notice."

"Somebody did."

"Somebody who reads a great deal and is very bored," she said, "and who is going to go and read some more, because that's what she does, and forget about barge pilots. Good day."

She turned to go.

"Why did you say it?" Jonah said.

She stopped.

"On the dais. He won. You said it before he did."

She looked at him over her shoulder, and for a moment the boredom was gone from her face entirely and there was something else there instead, something he couldn't name and would think about for a long time in a dark room under a planetoid, and then it was back.

"Because it was true," she said, "and nobody else was going to. That's usually why."

And she walked away up the yard road toward the Flamens' house, and did not look back, and Jonah stood at the rail and watched her go and realised he hadn't told her his name, and that she hadn't asked, and that he didn't know, for the first time in his life, whether that was a good thing.

---

Mara found him there ten minutes later. She came up the road from the square with something in her fist and a look on her face that he hadn't seen since the dock, the whole-face one, and she stopped in front of him and held out her hand and opened it.

It was a ring. Hull ring. Cut from plate, the way they were, a band of dull grey metal that had been part of something that flew, once, and had a bevel on it where the torch had gone through.

"The Halcyon," she said. "First ship you ever broke. I asked Morrow which and he found it in the book. You were fifteen. You cut the plate for the aft seam and Bram said you cried afterward because it was a beautiful ship."

"Bram's a liar."

"Bram's a liar." She was still holding it out. "Well?"

He looked at it. He looked at her. There was a thing behind his heart that had eaten a Thinstripe and was hungry again and had, an hour ago, been pleased about putting a knife to a boy's throat, and he had not told her about it, and she knew he had not told her, and she was standing in front of him with a piece of the first ship he'd ever broken in her hand.

"I'm not what I was three weeks ago," he said.

"I know."

"I don't know what I am."

"I know that too." She didn't move. "I'm not asking what you are. I'm asking if you'll wear the ring."

He took it. He put it on. It fit, because she'd measured his hand in his sleep, probably, years ago, because that was what she was like. He looked at it on his finger and then he looked up, and she was smiling, and he put his arms around her, and nothing came out of him at all, and he held on.

Over her shoulder, on the yard road, the quaestor's sister had stopped.

She was fifty paces off. She'd turned, for whatever reason people turn, and she was standing very still with her hands in her pockets and her head a little on one side, watching, and when she saw that Jonah had seen her she did not look away and did not look embarrassed. She looked, for one moment, exactly as bored as she had on the dais.

Then she went on up the road.

---

Morrow made the announcement at the fourth bell, from the yard office steps, to a crowd of Collars who had come because Morrow was announcing something and that was usually worth hearing.

The petition had been granted. Jonah Vale, peregrine, of Anvil, having served six years as pilot of the barge Kestrel out of the Morrow yard, and having brought that barge home with its captain lost and its crew alive, was to be manumitted by order of the quaestor of the province and enrolled as a citizen of the Republic, with investiture to follow on the last day of Founding Week, and to hold the captaincy of the Kestrel thereafter.

The crowd made the sound. It was a good sound, this time, and it was for him, and Jonah stood on the steps beside Morrow with his father's hand crushing his shoulder and his father's face doing the thing it did, and heard it, and felt, for a moment, exactly the way he was supposed to feel.

Then he saw Marchand.

The purser was at the edge of the crowd with his manifest under his arm, as he always was, and he was not clapping, and his face was doing nothing at all, and Jonah thought, the captaincy. Marchand had been the citizen of record on the Kestrel for six years, the Blank whose name went on the report, the one who had been passed over for the captaincy when Ochoa took it under Morrow's dispensation, and had been told, Jonah knew, because Ochoa had told him, that it would come to him when Ochoa was done.

Ochoa was done. And the captaincy was going to a Collar. And the Collar was going to be a citizen in a week, with a mark on his arm, and Marchand was going to be his purser.

Jonah looked at him across the crowd and Marchand looked back, and neither of them said anything, and then Marchand turned and went into the crowd, and beside him, going the same way, with the cutter's blood still on his shirt and his name on a roll, was Corin.

Corin didn't look at Jonah. He was looking at the rail, where Mara stood with her hands over her mouth and the whole of her face in her eyes.

Up on the dais, the quaestor rose. The reading had been taken, the signatures made, the day's law done. He stood, and the dais stood with him, and he came down the steps with his household behind him and crossed the square toward the Flamens' house, and as he passed the yard office he turned his head, once, and looked at Jonah on the steps.

It was a short look. It was the look a man gives a line in a ledger that has been added correctly and that he does not expect to see again.

Jonah looked back. In his hand the ring was warm. In his legs a dead man's feet were pleased. And behind his heart, quietly, the thing that had come out of a box looked at the broad purple stripe on the quaestor's arm as he passed, and was, for the first time since Varro, not hungry at all.

It was paying attention.
