# Chapter 6: Imperium

The cell was better than his father's rooms. Jonah noticed that in the first minute and disliked himself for it.

Cut rock, square. A drain, a bench, a steel door with a slot. Dry and warm. They had unbound his hands and left him a jug of water. Through the rock above him the quaestor's house went about its night: boots, a door, a voice he couldn't make out.

He sat with his back to the wall and waited.

The thing under his ribs was awake. It had come back up as they walked him down the stairs, slowly, and stopped under his heart. It lay there pressed against the seam.

He had not looked at the seam. He didn't know how. He could feel it, though. Something that had been whole and wasn't. He had put it on its knees by force in his father's room, and it had gone, and had not understood why. Something between them had torn in the going. It wasn't hurt. It was confused, and it kept turning the confusion over, and every time it did he felt it.

"I know," he said to the cell. "I'll explain it. I don't know how yet."

It listened. It always listened.

---

They came for him at the third hour. Two vigiles, not the sergeant.

Up one flight, along a corridor with real carpet, into a room with a brazier in the corner and a table in the middle. On the far side of the table, sleeve rolled, hands folded, a lamp at his elbow, the quaestor of the province sat reading.

He did not look up. He finished the page, turned it, and finished the next. Jonah stood between the vigiles and let him. The reading was the first question.

On the table between them: the stone box, empty, lid open. And the seal.

A packet the size of a hand, wrapped in oilcloth and tied, with a disc of dark red wax on the tie and a mark pressed into the wax. Six days under a floorboard and Jonah had never once looked at the mark. Ochoa had said, the seal, Vibius, promise, and died. The promise was Jonah's business. The rest was Ochoa's.

He looked at it now and could not make the mark out. He thought, Vibius. Marcus Vibius Priscus, who had put a hand on his shoulder that morning and told him to be precise. He'd had six days to work out which door on Anvil had that name on it. He'd been busy holding still.

The quaestor closed the book.

"Sit down."

Jonah sat. The vigiles went to the wall.

Priscus looked at him. The look went through his face, over his hands, and rested for a moment under his ribs before moving on. Jonah's stomach went cold. He was being read. Not by a glass. By the man.

"You'll have heard of Pulse," Priscus said.

"No, sir. Collars don't hear about a lot of things. That's the design."

Priscus's eyes came up off his folded hands. It was the first time all evening he had looked at Jonah rather than through him.

"That's true. Good. Things go faster when the other man knows the rules." He laid his hands flat. "My house's lorica has a gift. I can hear your heart from where I sit, as well as you can feel it. When a man lies, his heart skips. Most men never learn to stop it. I hear the skip. So don't. You'll be tempted. Everyone is. It wastes both our evenings, and I'm told you dislike waste."

"Who told you that?" Jonah heard it come out of his mouth and could do nothing about it. "Sorry, sir. It's been an hour since they took me out of my own engagement dinner, and you're the first person who's spoken to me since, and you already know what I dislike. I'm grateful, honestly. I'm nineteen. I didn't know I was a man who dislikes waste until an hour ago."

The vigiles at the wall moved. Priscus did not.

"Was that a joke?"

"It was an attempt, sir. I'm nervous. I get talkative. It's the worst thing I do, and it's the only thing I brought."

Priscus watched him for a moment that went on longer than the question needed.

"That's permitted too," he said. "So is silence, when you'd rather have some. Silence isn't a lie. It's not an answer either, and I'll draw my own conclusions from it, but it won't cost you anything you haven't already spent. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

"True. Then we'll begin."

---

He began with the box.

"Where did it come from?"

"A founding-era hull. Inner rings." True, and the Vigilant's report would say the same.

"What was in it?"

"Cloth. Something under the cloth."

Priscus waited.

"I don't know what it was," Jonah said. Also true. He had it, and it had him, and he did not know what it was.

"Is it in your possession?"

"No."

The word was out before he'd decided on it. His heart did nothing at all. Priscus watched him for a long moment.

"That's true," the quaestor said. "And I think it's the first answer you've given that surprised you. We'll come back to it. The purser says you took something from the hull. Is the purser lying?"

"The captain took it. I carried the box."

"Then where is it now?"

"I don't know where the captain put it." Ochoa had put it in Jonah's hand, through a torn glove, and had not lived to say where it went after.

Priscus tilted his head, listening for something in another room. Then he straightened.

"You're good at this. I want you to know I've noticed. Four true things, and I know less than when you sat down. My father's clerks have that skill. It took them twenty years." He looked down at the box. "Your barge was boarded in the rings by the cutter Vigilant on the fourteenth of last month. The Vigilant's report says its boarding officer was lost, its marines came back talking about a hostile lorica on a peregrine barge, and the barge was destroyed with all hands. Your barge docked at Anvil eleven hours later with a debris strike, a dead captain, and no boarding. Which is true?"

Jonah said nothing.

"Silence. Fine." Priscus didn't seem troubled. "The purser doesn't mention a boarding either. He mentions a great deal, he's a thorough man, but not that. So he was aboard for it, and he'd rather I didn't know what he saw. Which means, Vale, that whatever is or isn't in your possession put a decurion of the equestrian order into the rings of Quirinus."

He said it without weight.

"I'm not going to ask you about that. Not because I don't want to know. Because I already do, and there's no page for it. I've had a Flamen read you twice. He'll read you a third time in a moment. If it says what it said before, then the purser is a jealous man with a good imagination, and the marines saw a Collar with quick feet in bad light. That's what the page says. I read the page."

He looked up.

"Collars see monsters in every barge. So do marines. Bring the Flamen."

---

Same old man. They'd got him out of bed. Grey robe over whatever he slept in, red eyes, a glass the size of a book. He set it on the table and laid Jonah's arm across it without a word.

Jonah held still.

It was harder this time. In the Flamens' house that morning it had gone down on its own, fast, flat, because it knew what a glass was. Now it went down slowly, a piece at a time. He felt it reach the seam and catch there, and hang. Sweat started on his back. He thought, go, go. Then it was down.

The Flamen looked into the glass for a long time.

"One," he said. "Standard. Fused." He frowned. "There's a wobble."

"A what?"

"The lines don't hold still, quaestor. They should. A fused cutting sits. This one doesn't." He tilted the glass. "It's like reading a man with a fever. It's still one. It's just not sitting."

"Is that a lorica of the equestrian order?"

"No, quaestor. Nothing like it. It's a Blank's cutting, one, fused at nine, this morning, by me. It hasn't settled, is all. They do that. Deep blood." He straightened. "If you want my mark on it, it's clean."

"Put your mark on it."

The Flamen took the glass and went. The quaestor sat back.

"Three times. One, one, one. I'd have liked it otherwise. A hostile lorica is Flamens' business. They'd have taken you, the purser, the marines and the whole matter up to their house on the rock, and I'd never have read another page of it." He looked at Jonah. "But it's one. So it's mine."

He put out his hand and picked up the seal.

---

He turned it over, slowly. Jonah watched his face, because there was nothing else to watch, and because under his ribs it had come back up, unasked, and was watching too.

Priscus looked at the wax.

His face had done nothing all evening. It had read a page, listened to a heart, ordered a Flamen out of bed, all with the same expression. It did nothing now either, for about a breath. Then something behind it went still.

Jonah knew that from the inside. Three weeks learning his own face in hull glass. The moment when whatever runs the face stops running it and goes elsewhere to think, and the face carries on by itself. For one breath, behind the quaestor's face, nobody was home.

Then he was back. He set the seal down and put his hand flat on it.

"Who else has seen this mark?"

"The sergeant. Tonight."

"The sergeant sees my house's mark on every paper he carries. Who else?"

"No one. It was under a floorboard."

"Your captain?"

"He's dead."

"Before that."

"He gave it to me to deliver. He said a name. I never opened it."

"What name?"

Jonah looked at him. Nothing looked back. Priscus already knew the answer. He needed to know whether Jonah did.

"Vibius."

The hand on the seal did not move.

"The purser heard it too," Jonah said. "In the hold. He wrote it down. I saw him read it back."

"The purser heard a dying man say a word across a hold and wrote down what he thought he heard. A name is not a mark. There are eleven houses in the Senate with Vibius in them and four hundred men on the Limes called it for luck." His voice hadn't changed. "The purser will be seen to. He's a citizen who did his duty. He'll find that duty is noticed."

He let that sit.

"You. You held a sealed packet addressed to a senatorial house under a floorboard for six days and didn't open it. Why?"

"I said I'd deliver it."

"To whom?"

"To Vibius. Whoever that was. I hadn't worked it out yet."

Priscus listened to his heart say that. Then he laughed. One breath, through the nose. Not kind, not unkind. A man reading a sum that has come out to a number he has never seen before.

"True. God help you, that's true. You had my father's seal under your floor for six days and you were going to walk it up the road and knock." He took his hand off the packet. "Do you know what's in it?"

"No, sir."

"Neither do I. And I'm not going to."

He stood, took the packet to the brazier, and put it on the coals. Oilcloth, tie, wax and all. He stood over it with his hands behind his back.

The oilcloth went first, curling, with a smell. The wax ran, and the mark ran with it. Then the paper caught, thick pages of it. He watched every page go black and lift and fall. He did not turn round until there was nothing on the coals that had ever been anything.

Then he sat down again and folded his hands. The ledger face. A line added correctly.

"There was no packet. There was a box from a hull, which is Flamens' business and will go to them. There was a purser's letter, mistaken on every point. There was a citizen, read three times and found clean, arrested on my order because the letter alleged sedition and I was bound to look. I have looked. There is nothing."

"Sir."

"You'll be released in the morning, when the sergeant's back on shift. I'll not have it said I let a man out of this house at night. Your father will have you home by the fourth bell. You'll go to your ship. You'll be precise with your purser and careful with your feet. You'll never speak of the rings to anyone, and if you do, Vale, I'll hear it. I hear a great many things. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

Priscus listened to that, and nodded.

"Take him down."

---

Back on the bench. Back to the wall. The jug beside him.

He had watched the quaestor burn his own father's name, and he understood it. Priscus had a father. The father had a seal. The seal had been in the hold of a Collar barge under a Gracchan captain, and a purser had written it down. A quaestor could not become a praetor with that on a page. So there was no page. He had made it not exist with a brazier and a sentence, without raising his voice.

And the strange part was that Jonah believed him.

He'd been read three times tonight and had listened back. Nothing in the quaestor had sounded untrue. He had said there was no packet because there wasn't, now. He had said Jonah would be released, and his heart had done nothing. He'd said it in front of two vigiles. He had to mean it.

Jonah held onto that. It was what he had.

The door slot opened.

Not the sergeant. A hand, with a cup in it. Above the hand, where the sleeve was pushed back, a broad purple stripe.

"I'm told you had a jug," she said through the slot. "I'm told it's yard cistern water, which I've had, and I wouldn't give it to a dog. This is from the house. Take it before somebody comes."

He got up and took it. Her fingers were cold. She didn't let go at once.

Jonah said, "You're the quaestor's sister."

"I'm the quaestor's sister." The slot was narrow. One eye, her mouth, the stripe. "And you're the man with the Fulvian step who said the prayer. I watched you get a line on your arm this morning. I watched five vigiles take you out of a dinner tonight for a matter concerning the Flamens, which has never happened on this moon. So I asked what the matter was."

"And?"

"And I was told it was nothing. A purser's letter and a mistake, and you'd be out by the fourth bell." She let go of the cup. "Which is what I came down to tell you. My brother said it. To me, in the hall, on his way up. He doesn't say things he doesn't mean. It's the one thing about him everyone agrees on."

"So."

"So drink that and stop waiting to be hit. It's a habit. I noticed it in the square. It'll get you hit."

Jonah drank. Best water he'd ever had. He was ashamed of noticing.

Jonah said, "Why did you come down, if he already told you it was nothing?"

The eye in the slot considered him.

"Because I saw something on the glass this morning and told my brother, and he said it was nothing. Then five vigiles went down the yard road. I've read for three years in a house that teaches that what's on the glass is always something." A pause. "And because it was true, that you'd be out by the fourth bell, and nobody had come down to say so. That's usually why."

Jonah said, "You said that in the square, too. Word for word."

"I say it a great deal. It's the only argument I have that works on him." The corner of a mouth, maybe. "What's your name?"

He'd stood at a rail with her for the length of a conversation. She hadn't asked and he hadn't said.

"Jonah. Vale. Like the goodbye."

"Not like the valley."

"You heard that."

"Everyone heard that. The decurion's face was worth the whole festival." The eye moved; she'd checked the corridor. "Jonah Vale. All right. I'll remember it. Drink your water."

"Livia."

She stopped.

"Mara told me. At the rail, before you came over. She said you read all day, and I said you'd think you'd have something better to do."

"I heard that too. It's why I came over."

The slot closed. Her boots went up the corridor, quick and light. He stood in the middle of the cell with a cup of the best water on Anvil and did not sit down for a long time.

---

They came at the fourth hour.

He was on his feet before the key turned. Four bells. The sergeant's shift. He'd put the cup down and straightened his coat. He was going to walk up the yard road in it and his father was going to see him.

It was not the sergeant.

Four Blanks he didn't know, in a grey that was not the vigiles' grey, with a paper. The one with the paper didn't look at it, or at Jonah. He looked at the wall over Jonah's shoulder and said a number. The number was not a name. Then, in the voice of a man reading a manifest: "Transport. Ad metalla. By order."

He did not say whose.

Ad metalla. To the mines. Everybody on Anvil knew the words. The Empire had one mine that mattered, on a rock called Tessera at the far end of the Belt, and the Collars who went down it did not come back up. Metalla VII, the papers called it. The yards called it the Pit.

Jonah said, "There's a mistake. The quaestor said the fourth bell. He said it in front of his vigiles. Go up and ask him."

Nobody went up.

"Ask his sister."

The nearest Blank stepped in with the strap. Jonah's feet moved before he did. Half a step back, off the line, and the Blank's hand closed on air.

Under his ribs it came up all at once, into his arms, all the way. The seam pulled. Past it, waiting, was the rest: the plate, the crooked fang, Varro's feet. Four Blanks in a corridor. It could be done. It wanted to be done.

The second Blank put a rifle stock against his chest and pushed him into the wall, not hard. Nobody said anything. They were waiting to see what he was.

He thought about the house above him, and what was in it, and who.

He held out his hands.

They strapped them. The strap had been on a great many hands before his. One of them picked the cup off the bench, looked at the quality of it, and set it back down, carefully, square to the edge.

Other stairs, not the ones he'd come down. A door, a yard at the back of the house, in the rock. A ground car with its engine running and two more grey Blanks beside it. Sun-lamps at their night setting. Nobody on the yard road. Nobody at the gate. Nobody on Anvil awake to see a citizen, read three times and found clean, put in a car by men with no names.

He thought about the fourth bell.

Priscus had said it to his sister in a hall. She had come down a flight of stairs with a cup because it was true and nobody had said so. He lied to her. I heard him and I believed it. She's asleep up there believing it now.

A man who could hear a heart had said it with his own heart doing nothing, because it was true when he said it. Then he had gone up the stairs and changed his mind, or had never meant it past the door. Jonah would never know which. It didn't matter which.

There was no page for what Jonah was. No page for a Collar who could kill a Stripe. No page for a burned packet. No page for a citizen promised the fourth bell by a man who could not lie. So there was no trial. You did not try what was not on a page. You put it in a car.

The dock was empty. A grey hull with no name, ramp down, hold smelling of men. They walked him up and the ramp came up behind him. He stood in the dark with his hands strapped, a drawing on his arm, and something under his ribs that was, for the first time since the Kestrel, hungry again.

He said her name once, quietly, to the dark. It was the last true thing anyone had said to him.

Then he said his father's.

Then he sat down on the deck to wait. He was going to get very good at it.
