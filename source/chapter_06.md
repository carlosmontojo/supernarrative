# Chapter 6: Imperium

The cell was better than his father's rooms. Jonah noticed that in the first minute and disliked himself for noticing.

It was cut rock like everything else on Anvil, but it had been cut well, square, with a drain in the floor and a bench along one wall and a door of real steel with a slot in it, and it was dry, and it was warm. They had unbound his hands. They had left him a jug of water. Somewhere above him, through the rock, he could hear the quaestor's house going about its night, boots on a floor, a door, a voice he couldn't make out, and he sat on the bench with his back to the wall and the line on his arm and the ring on his finger and waited, because waiting was what there was.

The thing under his ribs was awake.

He had felt it come back up as they walked him down the stairs. Not far. Not the way it had come up in his father's room, into his arms and back, ready. It came up the way a man comes up out of deep water when he has run out of breath, slowly, and stopped somewhere under his heart, and stayed there, and he could feel it pressed against the place where the seam had gone.

He had not looked at the seam. He didn't know how. But he could feel it the way you feel a cut in your mouth with your tongue, something that had been whole and wasn't, a place where he had put the thing on its knees by force and it had gone, and had not understood, and something between them had torn a little in the going. It wasn't hurt. He didn't think it could be hurt. It was something worse than hurt. It was confused, and it was lying against the confused place, turning it over, and every time it turned it over he felt it.

"I know," he said, very quietly, to the cell. "I know. I'll explain it. I don't know how yet."

It listened. It always listened.

---

They came for him at the third hour.

Two vigiles, not the sergeant, and they walked him up one flight and along a corridor with a real carpet on it and into a room that had a brazier in the corner and a table in the middle and a chair on either side of the table, and on the far side of the table, in the far chair, with his sleeve rolled and his hands folded and a lamp at his elbow, sat the quaestor of the province, reading.

He did not look up. He read to the end of the page he was on and turned it and read to the end of the next, and Jonah stood between the two vigiles and watched him do it and understood that he was meant to, and that the reading was the first question.

On the table, between them, were two things. The stone box, empty, with its lid open. And the seal.

It was a packet the size of a hand, wrapped in oilcloth and tied, and on the tie, holding it closed, a disc of dark red wax with a mark pressed into it. Jonah had never looked at the mark. He'd had six days with it under a floorboard and had not once taken it out and looked, because Ochoa had said, the seal, Vibius, promise, and had died, and Jonah had decided that the promise was the whole of his business with it and the rest was Ochoa's.

He looked at it now, across the table, and could not make the mark out, and thought, Vibius.

He had thought it in the cell, too. He'd had two hours in the cell and had thought it for most of them. Vibius. Marcus Vibius Priscus, quaestor of the province, who had put his hand on Jonah's shoulder that morning and told him to be precise. The captain had said, the seal, Vibius, and the captain had said it as a place to take the thing to, a name, a door, and Jonah had had six days to think about what door on Anvil had that name on it and had not, because he had been busy holding still.

The quaestor closed the book.

"Sit down," Priscus said.

Jonah sat. The vigiles went to the wall.

Priscus looked at him. It was not a long look and it was not a short one. It was the look, Jonah thought, that the Flamen had given the glass, and it went through his face and his hands on the table and came to rest, briefly, on the place under his ribs, and stayed there a moment, and moved on. And Jonah understood, with a cold drop in his stomach, that he was being read.

Not by a glass. By the man.

"You'll have heard of Pulse," Priscus said.

"No, sir."

"That's true." Priscus said it the way you'd note the time. "Good. Then I'll tell you, because I find that things go faster when the other man knows the rules. My house's lorica has a gift. Most houses' do. Mine is this: I can hear your heart from where I sit. I can hear it as well as you can feel it. When a man lies, his heart does a thing, a small thing, most men never learn to stop it, and I hear the thing." He unfolded his hands and laid them flat. "So don't. You'll be tempted. Everyone is. It wastes both our evenings and I'm told you're a man who dislikes waste."

Jonah said nothing.

"That's also permitted," Priscus said. "Silence isn't a lie. It's not an answer, either, and I'll draw what conclusions I draw from it, but it won't cost you anything you haven't already spent. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

"True. Then we'll begin."

---

He began with the box.

He asked where it had come from, and Jonah told him, because it was true and because the Vigilant's report would say the same. A founding-era hull in the inner rings. He asked what had been in it, and Jonah said, cloth, and something under the cloth, and Priscus waited, and Jonah said, I don't know what it was, and Priscus listened to his heart say that and nodded slowly, because it was true. He did not know what it was. He had it, and it had him, and he did not know what it was.

"Is it in your possession?"

"No."

The word came out before he'd decided to say it, and he felt his heart do nothing at all, and Priscus's eyes stayed on him for a long moment.

"That's true," the quaestor said, "and I think it's the first thing you've said that you were surprised by. We'll come back to it. The purser says you took something from the hull. Is the purser lying?"

"The captain took it. I carried the box."

"Then where is it now?"

"I don't know where the captain put it." Which was true. Ochoa had put it in Jonah's hand, through a torn glove, and had not lived to say where it went after.

Priscus turned his head slightly, the way a man does listening for a sound in another room, and then turned it back.

"You're good at this," he said. "I want you to know I've noticed. You've told me four true things and I know less than when you sat down. It's a skill. My father's clerks have it and it took them twenty years." He looked down at the box. "Your barge was boarded in the rings by the cutter Vigilant on the fourteenth day of last month. The Vigilant's report says its boarding officer was lost, its marines came back speaking of a hostile lorica aboard a peregrine barge, and the barge was destroyed with all hands. Your barge docked at Anvil eleven hours later with a debris strike and a dead captain and no boarding. Which is true?"

Jonah said nothing.

"Silence. Fine." Priscus did not seem troubled. "The purser doesn't mention a boarding either. He mentions a great deal, he's a thorough man, but not that. Which means he was aboard for it and he'd rather I didn't know, which means he saw something he'd rather I didn't know he saw, and which means, Vale, that whatever is or isn't in your possession put a decurion of the equestrian order into the rings of Quirinus." He said it without any weight at all. "I'm not going to ask you about that. Not because I don't want to know. Because I already do, and because there's no page for it, and because I've had a Flamen read you twice and I'm going to have him read you a third time in a moment, and if it says what it said the last two times then the purser is a jealous man with a good imagination and the marines saw a Collar with quick feet in bad light. That's what the page says. I read the page."

He looked up.

"Collars see monsters in every barge," he said. "So do marines. Bring the Flamen."

---

It was the same old man. They'd got him out of bed; he had the grey robe over what was under it and his eyes were red and he had the glass with him, a small one, a plate the size of a book that he set on the table and laid Jonah's arm across without a word.

Jonah held still.

It was harder. He'd known it would be. In the Flamens' house that morning the thing had gone down on its own, fast, flat in the ditch, because it had felt a glass and known what a glass was. Now it went down slowly. It went down the way a man goes into cold water, a piece at a time, and he felt it go past the seam, and catch there, the way a sleeve catches on a nail, and hang for a moment, and he felt a sweat start on his back that he could not stop, and he thought, go, go, and did not know if it heard, and then it was down.

The Flamen looked into the glass for a long time.

"One," he said, finally. "Standard. Fused." He frowned. "There's a wobble."

"A what?"

"The lines don't hold still, quaestor. They should. A fused cutting sits like a stone. This one's." He tilted the glass. "It's like reading a man with a fever. It's still one. It's just not sitting."

"Is that a lorica of the equestrian order?"

"No, quaestor. Nothing like. It's a Blank's cutting, one, fused at nine, this morning, by me. It's a cutting that hasn't settled, is what it is. They do. Deep blood." He straightened. "If you want my mark on it, it's clean."

"Put your mark on it," Priscus said, and the Flamen took the glass and went.

The quaestor sat back.

"Three times," he said, to the table. "One, one, one. I'd have liked it to be otherwise. It would have been simpler. A hostile lorica is a thing for the Flamens, and they'd have taken you and the purser and the marines and the whole matter up to their house on the rock and I'd never have had to read another page of it." He looked at Jonah. "But it's one. So it's mine."

He put out his hand and picked up the seal.

---

He turned it over. He did it slowly, the way he'd turned the pages, and Jonah watched his face because there was nothing else to watch, and because the thing under his ribs had come up again, unasked, from wherever the glass had sent it, and was looking too.

Priscus looked at the wax.

It was a small thing. It would have been nothing on another man. The quaestor's face had done nothing all evening, had read a page and listened to a heart and ordered a Flamen out of bed with the same expression, the expression of a man watching a scale settle, and it did nothing now, either, for about the length of a breath, and then something behind it went still.

Not his face. Behind it. Jonah had spent three weeks learning his own face in a piece of hull glass and he knew what that looked like from the inside, the moment when the thing that runs the face stops running it and goes somewhere else to think, and the face keeps doing what it was doing on its own. Priscus's face kept doing what it was doing. And behind it, for a breath, nobody was home.

Then he was back, and he set the seal down on the table, and put his hand flat on top of it.

"Who else has seen this mark?"

"The sergeant. Tonight."

"The sergeant sees my house's mark on every paper he carries. Who else?"

"No one. It was under a floorboard."

"Your captain?"

"He's dead."

"Before that."

"He gave it to me to deliver. He said a name. I never opened it."

"What name?"

Jonah looked at him. Priscus looked back, and there was nothing behind the look at all, it had gone somewhere and was thinking, and Jonah understood that this was the question, the only one, and that the quaestor already knew the answer and needed to hear whether Jonah did.

"Vibius," he said.

Priscus's hand did not move on the seal.

"The purser heard it too," Jonah said. "In the hold. He wrote it down. I saw him read it back."

"The purser," Priscus said, "heard a dying man say a word across a hold, and wrote down what he thought he heard, and a name is not a mark. There are eleven houses in the Senate with Vibius in them and four hundred men on the Limes called it for luck. The purser will be seen to. He's a citizen who did his duty and reported a thing that concerned him and he'll find that duty is noticed." His voice hadn't changed. "You. You held a sealed packet addressed to a senatorial house for six days under a floorboard and didn't open it. Why?"

"I said I'd deliver it."

"To whom?"

"To Vibius. Whoever that was. I hadn't worked it out yet."

Priscus listened to his heart say that. Then he did a thing that Jonah did not expect. He laughed. It was one breath, through the nose, and it was not a kind laugh and it was not an unkind one. It was the laugh of a man reading a sum that has come out to a number he has never seen before.

"True," he said. "God help you, that's true. You had my father's seal under your floor for six days and you were going to walk it up the road and knock." He took his hand off the packet. "Do you know what's in it?"

"No, sir."

"Neither do I," Priscus said, "and I'm not going to."

He stood up.

He took the packet in one hand, and he crossed to the brazier in the corner of the room, which had been lit for the cold and had burned down to a red bed, and he put the packet on the coals, oilcloth and tie and wax and all, and stood over it with his hands behind his back and watched.

It took a while. The oilcloth went first, curling, with a smell. Then the wax ran, and the mark in it ran, and then the thing inside caught, and it was paper, and it was thick, pages of it, and the quaestor stood and watched every page go black and lift and fall into the bed, and did not turn around until there was nothing on the coals that had ever been anything.

Then he came back to the table and sat down, and folded his hands, and looked at Jonah, and his face was the face from the square. The ledger face. The line that had been added correctly.

"There was no packet," he said. "There was a box, from a hull, which is Flamens' business and will go to them, and a purser's letter, which was mistaken on every point, and a citizen who was read three times and found clean, and who was arrested on my order because the letter alleged sedition and I was bound to look. I have looked. There is nothing. You'll be released in the morning, when the sergeant's back on shift, because I'll not have it said that I let a man out of this house at night." He paused. "Your father will have you home by the fourth bell. You'll go to your ship. You'll be precise with your purser, and you'll be careful with your feet, and you'll never speak of the rings to anyone, and if you do, Vale, I'll hear it. I hear a great many things. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

Priscus listened to that, and nodded.

"Take him down," he said.

---

He was back on the bench with his back to the wall and the jug beside him and he did not know what to do with what he was feeling, so he sat with it.

He had watched the quaestor burn his own father's name. He had watched it and understood it, all of it, the way he'd understood Ochoa's hands on the chart, and it sat in him now beside the other things, heavy and clear. Priscus had a father, and the father had a seal, and the seal had been on a packet in the hold of a Collar barge with a Gracchan captain, and a purser had written it down, and a quaestor could not be a praetor with that on a page. So there was no page. The man had made the page not exist with a brazier and a sentence, and had done it without raising his voice, and then had told Jonah he'd be home by the fourth bell.

And the strange thing, the thing Jonah sat with, was that he believed him.

He turned it over. He'd been read three times tonight, once by a glass and twice by a man, and he knew what it felt like now, to be listened to, and he had listened back. He'd heard nothing in the quaestor that wasn't true. Priscus had burned the packet because it was his father's, and he had said there was no packet because there wasn't, now, and he had said Jonah would be released, and Jonah, who had grown up on a moon where a Stripe's word was weather, had heard it and had thought, he means it. He's cold and he's quick and he's just killed a thing that a dying man asked me to carry, and he means it. He has to. He said it in front of two vigiles.

He held onto that. He had a father in a wrecked room and a ring on his finger and a thing under his ribs pressed against a seam, and he held onto the quaestor's word the way he'd held onto the yoke in the rings.

The door slot opened.

He looked up. It was not the sergeant. It was a hand, and the hand had a cup in it, and on the forearm above the hand, where the sleeve had been pushed back, there was a broad purple stripe.

"I'm told you had a jug," she said, through the slot. "I'm told it's the water from the yard cistern, which I've had, and I wouldn't give it to a dog. This is from the house. Take it before somebody comes."

He got up and took it. Her fingers were cold. She didn't let go of the cup at once.

"You're the quaestor's sister."

"I'm the quaestor's sister." The slot was narrow; he could see her mouth and one eye and the stripe. "And you're the man with the Fulvian step who said the prayer, and I watched you get a line on your arm this morning, and I watched you get taken out of a dinner tonight by five vigiles for a matter concerning the Flamens, which is a thing that has never happened on this moon, so I asked what the matter was."

"And?"

"And I was told it was nothing. That it was a purser's letter, and a mistake, and that you'd be out by the fourth bell." She let go of the cup. "Which is what I've come to tell you. My brother said it. To me. In the hall, on his way up. He doesn't say things he doesn't mean; it's the one thing about him that everyone agrees on. So." She stopped. "So you can drink that and stop looking like a man waiting to be hit. It's a habit. I noticed it in the square. It'll get you hit."

Jonah drank. It was good water. It was the best water he'd ever had and he was ashamed of noticing it.

"Why did you come down?" he said.

The eye in the slot looked at him.

"Because I saw something on the glass this morning," she said, "and I told my brother, and he said it was nothing, and then five vigiles went down the yard road. And I've been reading for three years in a house that teaches that the things on the glass are always something." She was quiet a moment. "And because it was true, that you'd be out by the fourth bell, and nobody had come down here to say so. That's usually why."

"You said that in the square."

"I say it a great deal. It's the only argument I have that works on him." Something in the one eye that might have been the corner of a mouth. "What's your name?"

He looked at her. He'd stood at a rail with her for the length of a conversation and she had not asked, and he had not said, and he'd walked away not knowing if that was a good thing.

"Jonah," he said. "Vale. Like the goodbye."

"Not like the valley."

"You heard that."

"Everyone heard that. The decurion's face was worth the whole festival." The eye moved; she'd looked over her shoulder, down the corridor. "Jonah Vale. All right. I'll remember it. Drink your water."

"Livia."

She stopped.

"Mara told me," he said. "At the rail. Before you came over. She said you read all day, and I said you'd think you'd have something better to do."

"I heard that too," she said. "It's why I came over." And the slot closed, and her boots went away up the corridor, quick and light, and he stood in the middle of the cell with a cup of the best water on Anvil in his hand and did not sit down for a long time.

---

They came at the fourth hour.

He was on his feet before the key turned. He'd been listening for it. He'd been counting bells and there had been four and he had thought, the sergeant's back on shift, and had put the cup down and stood up and straightened his coat, because he was going to walk up the yard road in it and his father was going to see him.

It was not the sergeant.

It was four Blanks he didn't know, in a grey that was not the vigiles' grey, with a paper, and the one with the paper did not look at it and did not look at Jonah either. He looked at the wall over Jonah's shoulder and said a number, and the number was not a name, and then he said, in the voice of a man reading a manifest, "Transport. Ad metalla. By order," and did not say whose.

"There's a mistake," Jonah said. "The quaestor said. The fourth bell. He said it in front of his vigiles. Ask him. Go up and ask him."

Nobody went up.

"Ask his sister."

They bound his hands. They did it the way the vigiles had, without being told. They did it with a strap that had been used on a great many hands before his, and one of them took the cup off the bench, and looked at it, at the quality of it, and set it back down carefully, as if it mattered where it went.

He did not fight them. He thought about it. The thing under his ribs came up, all the way, the way it had in his father's room, and was in his arms, and he could feel the seam pull as it came and could feel, past the seam, all of it, the plate and the crooked fang and Varro's feet, waiting to be asked, and he thought about four Blanks in a corridor under the quaestor's house, and the quaestor's house above them, and what was in it, and who.

He didn't ask.

He let them take him up. Not the stairs he'd come down. Other stairs, that went to a door that went to a yard he'd never seen, at the back of the house, in the rock, where a ground car was waiting with its engine running and its rear door open and two more grey Blanks beside it, and the sun-lamps were still at their night setting, and there was nobody on the yard road, and nobody at the gate, and nobody, anywhere on Anvil, awake to see a citizen of the Republic who had been read three times and found clean put into the back of a car by men with no names and driven to the dock.

He thought, on the way, about the fourth bell.

He thought about the quaestor saying it. He thought about the quaestor saying it to his sister in a hall, and the sister coming down a flight of stairs with a cup because it was true and nobody had said so, and he thought, he lied to her. He lied to her, and I heard him, and I believed it, and she believed it, and she's asleep up there believing it now.

He had been read by a man who could hear a heart. And the man had said a thing with his own heart doing nothing at all, because it was true when he said it, because he'd meant it, in that room, with two vigiles at the wall, and then he'd gone up the stairs and changed his mind, or had never meant it past the door, and Jonah would never know which, and it did not matter which.

There was no page for what Jonah was. There was no page for a Collar who could kill a Stripe, and no page for a packet that had been burned, and no page for a citizen who had been told the fourth bell by a man who could not lie. There were only pages for things that could be, and he was not, anymore, a thing that could be.

So there was no trial. Of course there was no trial. You did not try a thing that was not on a page. You put it in a car.

The dock was empty. The transport was a grey hull with no name on it, and its ramp was down, and its hold smelled of men, and the Blanks walked him up the ramp and into it and the ramp came up behind him and he stood in the dark with his hands bound and a line on his arm that was a drawing and a thing under his ribs that was, for the first time since the hold of the Kestrel, hungry again.

He said her name, once, quietly, to the dark, because it was the last true thing anyone had said to him.

Then he said his father's.

Then he sat down on the deck to wait, because waiting was what there was, and he was going to have to get very good at it.
