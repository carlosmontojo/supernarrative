# Chapter 38: What a Stripe Is Worth

The Flamen who sold readings kept his glass in a cutting shed on the north side, behind the melt-house, where the smoke went, and Jonah stood outside it on the third morning with the hood up and felt, through the rock, what was inside.

Three hearts. One fast and thin, an old man's, going the way Gaspar's went, and two slow and plated, Tiro, one on either side of the door inside, the way the boys had stood at the road's end. And a fourth thing that was not a heart. It was in the middle of the shed on a stand, and it was the Vein, and it was cracked; he felt the crack in it the way he had felt the knot in the face of the Deep, a place where the dark glass had been whole and was not, and the thing in the doorway of him looked at it and was interested, because it had never felt a broken piece of its mother before.

"Not that," he said, under his breath, in the old tongue. "We're here to be read. Let it."

He knocked.

---

Nasso had been a Flamen of the College for thirty years and had been defrocked for eleven, and the eleven showed more.

He was sixty, thin, in a robe that had been white and was the colour of the shed, and he had a Flamen's hands, long, clean, with the cutting in the right forearm showing grey at the wrist where the robe was pushed back, and a Flamen's face, which was a face that had looked at four thousand arms on a glass and had stopped, at some point, seeing men on the other end of them. He sat behind the cracked slab on a stool. The two Tiro stood where Jonah had felt them stand. They were Blanks with hired cuttings and short fangs and the bored look a man wears who is paid to stand by a door, and they looked at a Halyard's hood and did not straighten.

"Morrow's man," Nasso said.

"No."

"Morrow's yard sent a slab across this glass at the second bell. A harvest slab, whole, with a College groove on the edge of it, off a Halyard skimmer that's on the lower docks lying about its casks. I paid coin for it and I'll sell it up the ring a shard at a time and nobody'll ask." The defrocked Flamen's voice was dry and quick. "And now there's a Halyard at my door. You'll forgive me for adding."

"Add what you like. I've come to be read."

Nasso looked at him.

"Halyards don't get read. Halyards run dark. It's the one thing they're for."

"Then I'm a poor Halyard." Jonah put a shard on the slab. Not on the crack; beside it. "That's the price of a reading on Anvil. I asked. It's what a Blank pays for a cutting's grade so he can tell his wife. I've paid it. Read me."

The old man looked at the shard. He looked at it for longer than a Flamen looks at matter, because a Flamen who sells readings from a cracked glass in a smoke-shed has looked at very little matter in eleven years, and then he looked at the hood.

"Arm," he said.

Jonah gave him the right one.

He pushed the sleeve back himself, to the elbow, and laid the forearm on the cracked dark glass of the Vein with the white line up, and the two Tiro by the door saw the line and straightened, and Nasso saw it and did not.

"Struck," the old Flamen said.

"Yes."

"A citizen's line, cut. Seven years, by the scar. And the plate's dark under it; I can see that from here, I don't need the glass for that." He put his long clean hands on the slab, either side of the arm, and did not touch it yet. "You know what a struck man is, on a glass? He's a void. That's the word. The College cuts the line and reads void and writes it, and that's a man's grade for the rest of his life, and a void doesn't come to a shed and pay to be read, because there's nothing to read. There's nothing there."

"Read it anyway."

"I'm telling you what a reading costs." Nasso did not move his hands. "Not the shard. The shard's the price of a Blank's grade. A struck man on my glass is a thing I'm supposed to send to the garrison, and if I read him and don't send him, that's my neck, and a neck's worth more than a shard. So the price is the shard and the thing I'm going to ask you after, and you'll answer it, because I'll have read you and I'll know whether you're lying."

"You'll not know that."

"I've read four thousand arms."

"Then read this one," Jonah said, "and tell me what it's worth. That's what you sell. What a stripe's worth. What a cutting's worth. What a man's worth on a glass. I've come to find out what I'm worth. I'll pay what it costs."

Nasso put his hands on the arm.

---

It was not like Livia's glass.

He had thought it would be. He had stood in a line six times in seven years with his arm on the Vein and a Flamen's hands on it and a thing behind his heart held flat, or in the cellar, or being nothing, and he had learned what a reading felt like from the inside, which was a hand going through a house looking for a room, and he had learned to have no room. He had a room now. He had come to the shed to have it found. He stood with his arm on a cracked slab in a smoke-shed on Anvil and said, to the thing in the doorway of him, in the old tongue: let him. Don't hide. Don't be nothing. Be what you are and let the glass have it, because I want to know, and this is the only glass on this rock that'll not write it down.

And Tacitus let the glass have it.

He felt it happen. He felt the old Flamen's hands go into the arm through the plate, the reading, the hand going through the house, and he felt the thing with a name stand in the doorway with its face to the hand and not step back, not go into the cellar, not be still; stand, and be looked at, the way it had stood on the Deep road and let a fang come, and the hand found it.

Nasso's hands stopped.

He would tell that, later, to the one person, and she would say, what did it feel like, and he would say: it was a man walking into a room he had walked into four thousand times and finding somebody sitting in it. Not hiding. Sitting. Looking back at him. And the man stopping in the door.

"That's not a cutting," Nasso said.

His voice had changed. The dry quick thing had gone out of it, and what was under it was very old and very careful, a Flamen's voice, the one they used for the slab, and he did not take his hands off the arm.

"No," Jonah said.

"That's not a fuse. There's no fuse. There's no mark on it; a cutting's got the College's mark on it the way a coin's got a face, and I've never once in four thousand arms put my hands on a lorica with no face on it." The hands did not move. "It's whole. Gods. It's whole and it's old and it's looking at me."

"Yes."

"Grade." Nasso said it to the glass, the way they did. "Grade." He stopped. "It won't hold still."

Jonah waited.

"The lines. On the glass. A grade's a line on the glass and it holds and I read it, and these don't. They're moving. They go to forty and they go past it and they come back, and they go to something I've no line for and come back, and they'll not stop." The old man's hands were shaking, and it was not the shard. "Force nine. Speed ten. Hold seven; that's the plate, it's wrong-shaped, I can feel it, it's shut where it should be open. Sense nine. Wit." He stopped again. "Wit's not there. There's no value. It's not nothing; nothing reads as a line at zero. It's not reading at all. The glass is asking and it's not answering."

"It doesn't answer," Jonah said. "It's got one word."

Nasso looked up at the hood.

"Gifts," he said, and looked down again, because a Flamen finishes a reading. "Plate. Blade. Hush; that's turned wrong too, it's turned in, I've seen that once in eleven years on a Halyard captain. Seal. Malformed. Reach. Malformed. Both of them taken, not grown, and taken wrong." He breathed. "And four I don't know."

"Four."

"Four things on this glass I've never seen. I've read the tree. I've read every gift a lorica's held in four hundred years, they're a hundred and twelve and I know all of them by the line, and there's four here that aren't in it. One of them's eating. I can feel that. It's the biggest thing on the glass and it's an eating thing and it's looking at the crack in my slab the way a man looks at a plate of fish." He took his hands off the arm.

He took them off the way a man takes his hands off a thing that has just spoken to him, and sat back on the stool, and looked at the hood, and his heart, which had been fast and thin, was faster.

"Unregistered," he said.

Jonah did not say anything.

"That's the word. The College's word. I've never once had to use it. Nobody has; there've been three in a hundred years and the grain-men cut all three, and they teach it to novices as a thing that doesn't happen, the way they teach the Sertorian Form." Nasso's voice was flat. "Genius: unregistered. Lineage: none. Grade: indeterminate. That's the reading. That's what's on my glass, and I'll tell you what it's worth, since you asked, since you paid for that too."

"Tell me."

"A senator's seat." The old man said it without any weight. "To the man who brings it in. The grain-men'd give a Blank a broad stripe for it; they'd give a defrocked Flamen his robe back and a house in the City and his name off the list. That's what an unregistered's worth. It's worth more than the slab I bought this morning and more than every shard I'll sell up the ring for the rest of my life. It's the most valuable thing that's ever been in this shed and it's sitting in a Halyard's arm looking at me." He was quiet a moment. "And you knew that. Before you put the arm on the glass. You knew what I'd find and you knew what it's worth and you came anyway, to a man who sells readings, and paid him a shard to see it."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because I wanted to know," Jonah said, "and you're the only glass on this rock that doesn't write to the College. And because I wanted to see what you'd do."

The two Tiro by the door had their fangs out.

---

He had counted them before he knocked.

Two Blanks with hired cuttings, six and seven, Plate and Blade and nothing else, paid to stand by a door and put a fang in a Collar who would not pay, and they had listened to a Flamen read a thing on a glass that was worth a senator's seat, and they had done the sum, and it had come out the way sums came out on Anvil. They came off the door together. Not high and low, the garrison way; they had not been to a garrison. One for each side, the way two men come at a drunk.

He did not put the plate up.

That was the thing he would keep. He had put it up at the road's end in the light with a Flamen watching and he had put it up on the dock road with a Thinstripe forty paces off, and he stood in a smoke-shed with his arm still on a cracked slab and did not put it up, because a Flamen who sold readings did not need to see plate to know what he had read, and because two Blanks with hired cuttings who had never been to a garrison were not a thing that needed plate. They were a thing that needed Varro.

He took the first one's wrist.

Both hands. The way Varro had known and Livia had named on a rail and a garrison school taught in its first month: a wrist is a hinge and a hinge goes one way. The fang went out of the fingers onto the shed floor and was plate again, and the arm went the way arms do not go, and the man sat down against the slab's stand and looked at his arm and did not get up.

The second one had the fang at his throat.

He let it come. He stood with his arm on the glass and let a hired Tiro's fang come at the throat seam of a man with no plate up, and the plate came up under it, a hand's width, at the seam only, the Wall, the old man's Wall, the plate going where the fear was because he did not lie about where the fear was, and the seam was shut, wrong, backward, in a lump, and the fang skidded on a thing that had not been there and went past his ear into the shed wall.

Jonah took the arm that had the fang in it and turned it, and the man went over his hip onto the floor, and Jonah put a boot on the wrist and leaned, and the fang went back into the cutting because a man's cutting will not hold a fang out when the man is screaming, and the man screamed.

"Stop," Jonah said. In the road's tongue. "You're not dead. Neither of you. That's a thing I decided before I knocked. Sit there."

They sat there.

He took his boot off the wrist and turned round, and Nasso had not moved from his stool, and Nasso's hand was under the slab.

"There's a bell under there," Jonah said. "For the garrison. You've had your hand on it since I said yes. You've not rung it." He came round the slab. "Tell me why."

The old Flamen looked at him.

"Because I've read four thousand arms," Nasso said, "and I know what a man's going to do before he does it, most days, from the arm, and I don't know what you're going to do, and a man who doesn't know what a thing's going to do doesn't ring a bell at it." He took his hand out from under the slab. Slowly. "And because of the other thing."

"Say it."

"You didn't kill them."

Jonah stood over him.

He thought about the old man on the rock of the Cradle with the stick across his knees, saying: there will be a man who has seen you, and he will be small and afraid and greedy, and you will have to decide what a man like that is worth, and I will tell you now what the answer is, so that you will have heard it once: he is worth nothing, and you kill him, and you say his name, and you go on, because a small frightened greedy man who has seen you is the thing that ends you, not the Vibian, not the Trimmer, him. And Jonah had said, on the rock, yes. And had meant it.

He thought about a man on a bucket with his eyes shut saying, once, to take one. He thought about a guard coming down a road slow with his rifle down. He thought about a boy's cutting saying please and a thing with a name that had heard the word once and knew what it was for.

He thought about what a stripe was worth.

"Two shards," he said.

Nasso did not say anything.

"Two. On the slab. Beside the first. That's three, and three's more than you'd get from the ring for Morrow's slab in a year, and it's yours, and it's for the reading, which I've had, and for the thing you've not done, which is ring the bell." He put them down. "And for the thing you'll not do. Which is write it."

"A Flamen finishes a reading."

"Finish it in your head. Say the remarks. I know how they go; I've had three." Jonah stood back from the slab. "Say them to me and then never say them again."

Nasso looked at the three shards on the cracked glass.

Then he looked at the arm, which was still there, on the slab, with the white line up, because Jonah had not taken it away, and he said it, in the voice for the slab, the old careful one, the way a Flamen finishes:

    READING. Unlicensed. Anvil, lower docks.
    Bearer: unknown.
    Lorica: present. Genius: UNREGISTERED. Lineage: none.
    Grade: indeterminate. Lines will not hold still.
    Force 9. Speed 10. Hold 7. Sense 9. Wit: no value returned.
    Gifts: Plate. Blade. Hush. Seal (malformed). Reach (malformed). Four unrecognized.
    Remarks: do not repeat this reading.

"Do not repeat this reading," Jonah said. "That's the remark. You've said it. It's yours."

He took his arm off the glass.

---

He would tell the one person, years later, that this was the mistake, and she would say, which, and he would say: the shed. The Flamen. I'd read the four thousand arms in his hands and the bell under his slab and the shake in his voice when he said whole, and I'd counted it, and it came out to a small frightened greedy man with a thing in his head worth a senator's seat, and I stood over him with my boot off a Blank's wrist and I did the sum the old man told me the answer to on a rock, and I got it wrong on purpose.

And she would say, why.

And he would say: because he hadn't rung the bell. Because a boy's cutting had said please and I'd stopped, and a guard had come down a road slow, and I'd started to think that a man who has the bell under his hand and doesn't ring it is a man who's decided something, and I wanted that to be worth a stripe. I wanted it to be worth more than a senator's seat. I wanted there to be a line on the slate for it.

And she would say, was there.

And he would say, no. There was a line for what he did after. That's the only kind there is.

---

He went out of the shed into the smoke with the reading in him and the hood up and the two Tiro sitting against the wall with their arms in their laps, and Nasso on his stool with three shards on a cracked slab and his long clean hands flat beside them and his heart going fast and thin, and he did not look back, because he had decided, and a decided thing is a thing you do not look back at, the old man had said, or you will do it twice.

He stood in the north yards with the melt-house chimney going over him.

"Indeterminate," he said, to the thing in the doorway of him, in the old tongue. "Lines that won't hold still. Wit, no value returned. Four unrecognized." He breathed the smoke. "That's what I am on a glass. Not forty-two. Not anything. The College's word for a thing that doesn't happen. That's the answer I told Yara I'd have in a month, and I've got it, and it's not an answer, it's a reading that says the glass doesn't know."

The thing in the doorway did not say its word.

It did the other thing. It turned, in the doorway, the way it had turned in the Chamber when it heard its name, and it looked, not at him, but at the thing the Flamen had read: at itself. At the eating thing and the shifting thing and the hearing thing and the thing that felt boots through rock, four things a man with four thousand arms behind him had no line for, and it looked at them the way a man looks at his own hands the first time somebody tells him they are his, and Jonah felt it look, and felt what it felt, which was not pride and not hunger and not interest.

It was the thing a man feels who has been told, by somebody with the right to say it, that he does not have a name in the book, and has decided, standing in the smoke, that he will have one.

"Yes," Jonah said. "All right. Yes. Tonight."

Behind him, in the shed, an old man who had finished a reading sat for a long time with his hands beside three shards, and then took a pen out of his robe, and did not use it, and put it back, and took it out again.
