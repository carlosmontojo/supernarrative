# Chapter 37: Morrow

The yard office had a new lock. A Flamen's lock, with a want in it.

Jonah put his hand flat on it. It opened.

The room had not changed. A desk, a lamp, the manifest shelf, the book Morrow was not supposed to own on the third shelf. Under the lamp, a man of fifty-seven sat with his head in his hands and a paper in front of him.

Morrow did not look up.

"We're shut. The bank's got the keys on the seventh day. If you've come for a hull there's none. If you've come for wages there's none of those either. If you've come from Yara, tell her I can't take her casks. I've nowhere to put them the bank won't count."

"I've not come from Yara."

Morrow looked up.

A grey coat. A hood. A man standing in a doorway that had been locked, and no sound of it opening. Morrow's hand went to the cutter's bar that leaned against the desk.

"You'd not reach it," he said.

"No." Morrow took his hand back. "I'd not." He put the paper down. "Then who've you come from."

"Nobody." Jonah shut the door behind him. "I've come about the seventh day."

---

He sat where the clients sat. Morrow watched him sit.

The yard owner had got old from the outside in. Big, grey, tired, in the same coat he had worn on a dock seven years ago. Bigger in it now. A man got bigger on yard-bread when he paid his cutters first.

"You're the Halyard from the dock road," Morrow said.

"Yes."

"Two auxiliaries. Corin's. Curfew." He said it without weight. "Bram's my cutter. Was. He's at his sister's with his face open, and he says a Halyard got him up the road. He says it loud. He's not once said what the Halyard did first. Bram tells everybody everything. So I know what you are. You're a man Bram won't talk about."

Jonah said nothing.

"And you've come through my lock." Morrow put his hand flat on the paper. "I had it changed in the spring because the bank asked. They'd not lend on a yard with a lock a Collar could open with a pin. It's a Flamen's lock. It's got a want in it. And it opened for you."

"It did."

"I don't want to know." Morrow looked at the hood. "I said that to a boy once, in this room. I don't want to know, I said. I want to have known nothing, ever. He tried, and it didn't hold, and he went down a hole. So I'll not say it to you. I'll ask instead. What do you want with the seventh day?"

"Tell me about the paper."

Morrow looked at it.

"It's a note. Marchand's bank. I signed it in the winter of three eighty-nine for a sum that'd keep the yard open through the spring. I'd two hulls in and no matter to cut them with and forty Collars on wages. Nobody else on Anvil would lend to a yard that paid Collars. He would. He'd lend to anybody. That's a bank."

"And the rate."

"The rate had a war in it. Every spring since, I've paid the rate and not the sum. This spring the legate took my two hulls for the fleet at the fleet's price, which is nothing, and I couldn't pay the rate." He turned the paper round so the hood could see it. "So the bank takes the yard. Seventh day. Lawful. He's never once done anything that wasn't. He's got a house on the upper ring now. Publicans get one. He collects the province's tax and owns the province's bank, and he's never raised his voice."

"And the melt."

Morrow's hand stopped on the paper.

"Which melt."

"Winter of three eighty-nine. South yards. Adult male, one. Paid by the yard." Jonah kept the Belt in his voice. "A man in the Halyard told me the yard paid and the money wasn't the yard's. He said it was Marchand's. Out of his own purse. Through you. So the ledger'd say the yard."

Morrow was quiet for a long moment.

"Gaspar," he said.

"Yes."

"Gaspar's a drunk who's told that story a hundred ways. He's told it that Marchand paid so he could watch. He's told it that I begged. He's told it that Mara begged. None of it's what happened." Morrow put both hands flat. "I'll tell you what happened. You've come through my lock and Bram won't talk about you. I've a seventh day coming and there's nothing left in this yard to be careful about."

"Go on."

"Tom Vale died in a chair. I found him."

"Why were you there."

"He'd stopped going to the quaestor's door. I thought, that's worse. It was." Morrow's face did nothing. "A Collar's melt costs what a Blank's month costs. I'd not got a Blank's week. Forty cutters, two hulls and a note. So I went to the bank."

"To him."

"To him. I sat in his office on the upper ring, which is a very clean office. I said, Silas, Tom Vale's dead in a chair and I've not got the melt. I want it from you. I want it from you because you wrote the letter."

"You knew."

"Everybody knew. Anvil's a small rock." Morrow did not raise his voice. "I said, you wrote the boy down and the boy went down a hole and his father sat in a chair till he died. You'll pay for the fire. Out of your own purse, not the bank's. It'll go through my yard so the ledger says the yard. Tom Vale's not having his melt paid by the man who did it. Not on a paper anybody can read."

"And he paid."

"He didn't argue. He took out a purse and counted it, twice. He said, let's be precise, Ezra, this is a gift to the yard and not to the family. I said, yes. I took it. I burned Tom Vale with it." Morrow breathed. "I stood at the front with Mara and let her say the name. Marchand stood at the back and watched his money go up. It's on my ledger as a gift to the yard. It's the only line on my ledger I'm not ashamed of."

Jonah sat very still.

Five years on a rock he had counted Morrow's yard paying the melt. It had never once come out to a big tired man in a clean office saying, you'll pay for the fire because you wrote the letter. It weighed differently.

"Why," he said.

"Why what."

"Why him. Knowing. Why not the quaestor. The Flamens. Anybody."

"I went to all of them." Morrow looked at the lamp. "The quaestor, the week after they took him. Told nothing at a door. The legate. He's a Thinstripe with a staff who doesn't see Blanks. I wrote to a senator in the City, a Vibius, whose name I'd got off a manifest of Ochoa's. Ochoa had carried for him. I thought a man Ochoa carried for might want to know Ochoa's pilot was in a hole. Nothing."

"And then."

"Then I wrote to the other lot. The Gracchans." Morrow said it flat. "There's a man on Marl who's one, or was. He'd bought a hull off me once and paid in coin nobody'd seen on Anvil. I wrote and said, a boy off a barge with a dead captain's been put down a hole on Tessera without a trial. I'd like to know why, and I'd like him out."

"And."

"That one wrote back. Two years on. A hand I didn't know, no name on it." He was quiet a moment. "It said: the convict you name is on the Tessera rolls, alive, under a number. It said: what was he carrying."

Jonah did not move.

"That's all it said. Not, we'll help. Not, here's the price. What was he carrying." Morrow looked at the hood. "I thought, there's people who'll pull a boy out of a hole to get it, and people who'll put him in one to keep it, and neither of them's asking about the boy. So I burned the letter. And I told Mara."

"You told Mara."

"That he was alive. Two years on. She'd stopped eating." Morrow's voice did not change. "I told her he was on the rolls under a number and nobody was going to get him out. She said, thank you, Ezra. She went on. The year after, she married Corin, because her family was under and Corin had a band and a ration."

"Did she ever say you should have kept it to yourself."

"Never once." He took his hands off the desk. "I've never once been sure I shouldn't have. That's the whole of it. That's what's in this yard the bank can't count. I've told a Halyard in a hood because Bram won't talk about him and my lock opened. Now I'd like to know why you came. It wasn't for a story. Gaspar sells those."

Jonah put the slab on the desk.

---

Not a shard. The slab. He had cut three shards off the end of it in the Heron's hold with the crooked fang, one for Gaspar and two for what came after. What was left was a hand deep and most of an arm long, the Vein's dark glass. It lay on Ezra Morrow's desk under the lamp beside a bank's paper.

Morrow did not touch it.

He had one grade of Plate in his arm, the cutting a yard owner was invested with at forty so he could stand behind an inspector. One grade was enough to feel a harvest slab across a desk. His hand went to his own forearm without his knowing, and stayed there.

"That's the Vein," he said.

"Yes."

"That's a College slab. A harvest slab. That's more matter than this yard's cut in a year." He did not look away from it. "Where did a Halyard get a harvest slab."

"Off a Halyard ship. Crew gets a share." Jonah kept his hands in his lap. "It clears the paper. Not at the bank. The bank'll not take matter off a Halyard without a manifest, and there isn't one. There's a Flamen in the north yards who reads for coin and sells what he reads. He'll buy a slab. He'll not ask where, because he can't. A man who sells readings doesn't ask where things come from."

"And then."

"He'll give you coin. Less than it's worth. Enough for the note. You take it to the upper ring on the sixth day and you put it on the clean desk. You say, let's be precise, Silas, and you count it twice."

Morrow said nothing.

"And then you pay your cutters," he said. "That's the whole of the price. It's not a gift to the family. It's a gift to the yard," Jonah said.

The yard owner sat with his hand on his forearm and looked at the slab for a long time. Jonah felt his heart through the floor, big and slow and tired. Thirty years of paying Collars like citizens and never once being paid back.

"Who are you," Morrow said.

"Hal's cousin. Off the Heron."

"Halyards don't give a yard a slab."

"This one does."

"Why."

Jonah thought about a man on a dock with his hand on a blanket, saying debris. A man on the office steps saying a name right, once, and not looking at him while he did it. A hand on the back of a boy's neck. A man in a clean office saying, you'll pay for the fire because you wrote the letter.

"Because you paid for the fire," he said. "And you made him pay for it. You told nobody. It's the only line on your ledger you're not ashamed of. I'd have paid you for that alone, without the note," Jonah said.

"That's not a reason a Halyard has."

"And because the Empire doesn't want a yard that pays Collars like citizens to exist. I'd like it to, a while longer. Thank you for the lock not mattering more than it did." He stood up. "You'll not see me again. Don't look. Bram won't talk about me and you'll not either. You've a seventh day and a slab and a Flamen to see. A man in a hood is something a yard owner never saw."

He went to the door.

"Halyard," Morrow said.

He stopped.

"The lock." The yard owner had not moved. "It's a Flamen's. It's got a want in it. I asked the man who fitted it what it wanted. He said, to stay shut, that's all they want. I said, what opens it. He said, the key, or a Flamen, or nothing." He looked at the hood. "Which are you."

"Nothing," he said. "It opened for nothing. Tell the bank," Jonah said.

He opened the door.

---

Julia Morrow was on the other side of it.

Sixteen. Her father's size and none of his tiredness. She had stood in the dark outer room with an unlit lamp in her hand for as long as it took a man to tell a story. She looked at a hooded Halyard coming out of her father's office and did not step back.

She looked at the hood. She looked at how he stood. She looked, longest, at his hands. A Collar's hands with a Halyard's grey on the sleeves. The right sleeve had ridden up on the door. The white line was showing.

"Father," she said, not loud, past him. "There's a man come out through the locked door."

"I know, Julia. Go to bed."

"He's got a struck line."

"Go to bed."

She did not go to bed. She stood in the dark with the unlit lamp and looked at Jonah, and Jonah looked back. That was a face that would say what it saw out loud, in a room, for the rest of its life. There was a woman in the City with that face. He had said her name every night for seven years.

"Good night," he said, in the Belt turn.

"You're not a Halyard," Julia Morrow said.

"No."

"Then what are you."

"Ask me in a month," he said, and went past her into the yard.

He did not look back. Behind him the office door closed. Morrow's voice, low, saying his daughter's name. Hers, through the wood: "But Father, he's got Tom Vale's." Then the door shut on the rest.

He stood in the yard.

He had cut his first plate here at fifteen, the aft seam of the Halcyon, and cried. The sun-lamps were down and the cradles were empty. Nobody in it. He stood in the middle of it with the hood up and spoke to Tacitus, in the old tongue, because it was listening.

"That's two. Gaspar's had his shard and Morrow's had his slab. One's a drunk who did nothing, and I've paid him for a story. One's a good man who did everything, and I've paid him for a yard. Neither of them knows who paid."

He looked at the office window, where the lamp still burned.

"He made Marchand pay for the fire. He sat in the clean office and made him count it twice. Nobody told me that. It wasn't on any slate."

Then the small touch. The hand with no hand.

"I know," he said. "There's room. There's always room. It's the only kind part of a count," Jonah said. He put his hands in the coat. "Tomorrow the Flamen. Then I'll have the reading, and I'll know what I am. I'll have a name for it, and I'll go."

He went out by the cutters' gate. It had no lock. Morrow had never once thought a cutter would steal from him, and he had been right.
