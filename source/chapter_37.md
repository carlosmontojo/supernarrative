# Chapter 37: Morrow

The yard office had a new lock, and it opened for his hand the way the old one would have opened for his key.

He stood in the doorway a moment with the hood up and let the room come through him. It had not changed. A desk, a lamp, the manifest shelf, the book that Morrow was not supposed to have on the third shelf where it had always been, and the smell of a yard office, which was torch-smoke and cold steel and the yard-bread they grew on scrap in the back room, and on the desk, under the lamp, a man of fifty-seven with his head in his hands and a paper in front of him that he had read, Jonah thought, the way Marchand read a manifest, item by item, until it came out the same every time.

Morrow did not look up.

"We're shut," he said. "The bank's got the keys on the seventh day. If you've come for a hull there's none, and if you've come for wages there's none of those either, and if you've come from Yara tell her I can't take her casks, I've nowhere to put them the bank won't count."

"I've not come from Yara."

Morrow looked up.

He looked at a Halyard's grey coat and a hood and a man standing in his doorway who had come through a locked door without a sound, and his face did the thing it had done on a dock seven years ago over a blanket, a big grey tired man looking at a hull that had come apart faster than the numbers said, and he put the paper down.

"Then who've you come from."

"Nobody." Jonah shut the door behind him. "I've come about the seventh day."

---

He sat where the clients sat, and Morrow watched him sit, and Jonah watched Morrow watch, and neither of them said anything for a while.

The yard owner had got old. Not the way Gaspar had, from the inside out; from the outside in, the way a hull does that has been left in the rings, the paint going and then the plate and the shape of it still there under the wear. He had been big and grey and tired seven years ago in a coat that had been good once, and the coat was the same coat, and he was bigger in it the way a man gets bigger who eats yard-bread because he has paid his cutters first, and his hands on the desk were a Blank's hands that had never held a torch and had held every other thing a yard needed holding.

"You're the Halyard from the dock road," Morrow said.

"Yes."

"Two auxiliaries. Corin's. Curfew." He said it without weight. "Bram's my cutter. Was. He's at his sister's with his face open and he says a Halyard got him up the road, and he says it loud, the way Bram says things, and he's not once said what the Halyard did first, and Bram tells everybody everything. So I know what you are. You're a man Bram won't talk about." Morrow put his hand flat on the paper. "And you've come through my lock. I had it changed in the spring because the bank asked; they'd not lend on a yard with a lock a Collar could open with a pin. It's a Flamen's lock. It's got a want in it. And it opened for you."

Jonah did not say anything.

"I don't want to know," Morrow said. "I said that to a boy once, in this room. I don't want to know what you're asking me, I said, I want to have known nothing, ever. And he tried, and it didn't hold, and he went down a hole." He looked at the hood. "So I'll not say it to you. I'll ask instead. What do you want with the seventh day?"

"Tell me about the paper."

Morrow looked at it.

"It's a note. Marchand's bank. I signed it in the winter of three eighty-nine for a sum that'd keep the yard open through the spring, because I'd two hulls in and no matter to cut them with and forty Collars on wages, and nobody else on Anvil would lend to a yard that paid Collars. He would. He'd lend to anybody; that's a bank. And the sum had a rate, and the rate had a war in it, and every spring since the war I've paid the rate and not the sum, and this spring the legate took my two hulls for the fleet at the fleet's price, which is nothing, and I couldn't pay the rate." He turned the paper round so the hood could see it. "So the bank takes the yard. On the seventh day. It's lawful. He's not once done a thing that wasn't. He's the most lawful man on Anvil. He's got a house on the upper ring now, did you know that? Publicans get one. He collects the province's tax and he owns the province's bank, and he's never raised his voice."

"And the melt."

Morrow's hand stopped on the paper.

"Which melt."

"Winter of three eighty-nine. South yards. Adult male, one. Paid by the yard." Jonah kept the Belt in his voice. "A man in the Halyard told me the yard paid and the money wasn't the yard's. He said it was Marchand's. Out of his own purse. Through you. So the ledger'd say the yard."

Morrow did not say anything for a long time.

"Gaspar," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Gaspar's a drunk who's told that story a hundred ways." The yard owner's voice did not rise. "He's told it that Marchand paid so he could watch. He's told it that I begged. He's told it that Mara begged. None of it's what happened and I'll tell you what happened, because you've come through my lock and Bram won't talk about you and I've a seventh day coming and there's nothing left in this yard to be careful about." He put both hands flat. "Tom Vale died in a chair. I found him. I'd gone to see him because he'd stopped going to the quaestor's door and I thought, that's worse, and it was. And a Collar's melt costs what a Blank's month costs and I'd not got it. I'd not got a Blank's week. I'd forty cutters and two hulls and a note. So I went to the bank."

"To him."

"To him. I sat in his office on the upper ring, which is a very clean office, and I said, Silas, Tom Vale's dead in a chair and I've not got the melt, and I want it from you, and I want it from you because you wrote the letter." Morrow's face did nothing. "I knew. Everybody knew. Anvil's a small rock. I said, you wrote the boy down and the boy went down a hole and his father sat in a chair till he died, and you'll pay for the fire, and you'll pay for it out of your own purse and not the bank's, and it'll go through my yard so the ledger says the yard, because Tom Vale's not having his melt paid by the man who did it, not on a paper anybody can read." He breathed. "And he paid. He didn't argue. He took out a purse and counted it, twice, the way he counts, and he said, let's be precise, Ezra, this is a gift to the yard and not to the family, and I said, yes, and I took it, and I burned Tom Vale with it, and I stood at the front with Mara and let her say the name, and Marchand stood at the back and watched his money go up. That's the melt. It's on my ledger as a gift to the yard. It's the only thing on my ledger I'm not ashamed of."

Jonah sat very still.

He had counted this, on a rock, for five years. He had counted Morrow's yard paying the melt and had put it on the slate with the rest, a good man who paid and a bad man who paid through him, and it had not once come out to a big grey tired man sitting in a clean office saying, you'll pay for the fire because you wrote the letter. He put it with the rest. It weighed differently.

"Why," he said.

"Why what."

"Why did you go to him. Knowing. Why not the quaestor. The Flamens. Anybody."

"I went to all of them." Morrow looked at the lamp. "That's the other thing I'll tell you, because there's a seventh day. I went to the quaestor about the boy, the week after they took him, and I was told nothing at a door. I went to the legate; the legate's a Thinstripe with a staff who doesn't see Blanks. I wrote to a senator in the City whose name I'd got off a manifest of Ochoa's, a Vibius, because Ochoa had carried for him and I thought a man Ochoa carried for might want to know Ochoa's pilot was in a hole. I got nothing. And then I wrote to the other lot."

"The other lot."

"The Gracchans." Morrow said it flat. "There's a man on Marl who's one, or was; he'd bought a hull off me once and paid in coin nobody'd seen on Anvil. I wrote to him. I said, a boy off a barge that came in from the rings with a dead captain's been put down a hole on Tessera without a trial, and I'd like to know why, and I'd like him out. And that one wrote back." He was quiet a moment. "Two years on. A letter in a hand I didn't know, no name on it. It said: the convict you name is on the Tessera rolls, alive, under a number. And it said: what was he carrying."

Jonah did not move.

"That's all it said. What was he carrying. Not, we'll help. Not, here's the price. What was he carrying." Morrow looked at the hood. "And I sat in this room with it and I thought, I don't know what he was carrying and I don't want to, I said so to his face, and whatever it was there's people who'll pull a boy out of a hole to get it and people who'll put him in one to keep it, and neither of them's asking about the boy. So I burned the letter. And I told Mara."

"You told Mara."

"That he was alive. Two years on. She'd stopped eating." Morrow's voice did not change. "I told her he was on the rolls under a number and that nobody was going to get him out, and she looked at me the way she looks at a seam that's not going to hold, and she said, thank you, Ezra, and she went on. And the year after that she married Corin, because her family was under and Corin had a band and a ration, and she's never once said to me that I should have kept it to myself, and I've never once been sure I shouldn't have." He took his hands off the desk. "That's the whole of it. That's what's in this yard that the bank can't count. I've told a Halyard in a hood because Bram won't talk about him and my lock opened, and I'd like now to know why you came, because it wasn't for a story. Gaspar sells those."

Jonah put the slab on the desk.

---

Not a shard. The slab. He had cut three shards from the end of it in the Heron's hold with the crooked fang, one for Gaspar and two for what came after, and the rest of it was a hand deep and most of an arm long and it was the Vein's dark glass and it lay on Ezra Morrow's desk under the lamp beside a bank's paper, and the room went quiet the way the Chamber had gone quiet when a slab was set down in it.

Morrow looked at it.

He did not touch it. He was a Blank and he had a cutting in his arm, one grade, the Plate a yard owner was invested with at forty so he could stand behind an inspector, and the cutting felt the slab from across the desk the way Yara's had felt the mark on her hatch, and Morrow's hand went to his own forearm without his knowing it had, and stayed there.

"That's the Vein," he said.

"Yes."

"That's a College slab. That's a harvest slab. That's more matter than this yard's cut in a year." He did not look away from it. "Where did a Halyard get a harvest slab."

"Off a Halyard ship. Crew gets a share." Jonah kept his hands in his lap. "It clears the paper. Not at the bank; the bank'll not take matter off a Halyard without a manifest and there isn't one. There's a Flamen in the north yards who reads for coin and sells what he reads. He'll buy a slab. He'll not ask where, because he can't; a man who sells readings isn't a man who asks where things come from. He'll give you coin. Less than it's worth. Enough for the note." He looked at the paper. "You take the coin to the upper ring on the sixth day and you put it on the clean desk and you say, let's be precise, Silas, and you count it twice."

Morrow did not say anything.

"And then you pay your cutters," Jonah said. "That's the whole of the price. It's not a gift to the family. It's a gift to the yard."

The yard owner sat with his hand on his forearm and looked at a slab of the Vein on his desk for a long time, and Jonah felt his heart through the floor, a big slow tired heart that had paid Collars like citizens for thirty years and had never once been paid back, and it was doing a thing, and Jonah let it.

"Who are you," Morrow said.

"Hal's cousin. Off the Heron."

"Halyards don't give a yard a slab."

"This one does."

"Why."

Jonah thought about it.

He thought about a man on a dock with his hand on a blanket, saying debris, and nodding once, slowly. He thought about a man on the yard office steps saying a name right, once, clearly, and not looking at him while he did it. He thought about a man in this room saying, I want the boy who flew my barge home to walk out of that house with a line on his arm and a ship, and I want to have known nothing, and putting his hand on the back of a boy's neck the way he had put it on a dead captain's blanket. He thought about a man sitting in a clean office saying, you'll pay for the fire because you wrote the letter.

"Because you paid for the fire," he said, "and you made him pay for it, and you didn't tell anybody, and it's the only thing on your ledger you're not ashamed of. And because a yard that pays Collars like citizens is a thing the Empire doesn't want to exist, and I'd like it to, for a while longer. That's why." He stood up. "You'll not see me again. Don't look. Bram won't talk about me and you'll not either, because you've a seventh day and a slab and a Flamen to see, and a man in a hood is a thing a yard owner never saw."

He went to the door.

"Halyard," Morrow said.

He stopped.

"The lock." The yard owner had not moved. "It's a Flamen's. It's got a want in it. I asked the man who fitted it what it wanted and he said, to stay shut, that's all they want, and I said, what opens it, and he said, the key, or a Flamen, or nothing." He looked at the hood. "Which are you."

"Nothing," Jonah said. "It opened for nothing. Tell the bank."

He opened the door.

---

Julia Morrow was on the other side of it.

She was sixteen. She had her father's size and none of his tiredness and she had been standing in the yard office's outer room in the dark for as long as it took a man to tell a story, with a lamp she had not lit in her hand and a face that had the Morrow habit, which was noticing, and she looked at a hooded Halyard coming out of her father's office and did not step back, and did not say anything, and noticed.

Jonah felt her notice.

He felt it the way he had felt Mara notice on a stair, and it was not the same; it was a girl who had never seen him and had nothing to know him by and was looking at him anyway, at the whole of him, the way a cutter looks at a hull before the torch goes in, to see where the seams are. She looked at the hood. She looked at the way he stood. She looked, longest, at his hands, which were a Collar's hands with a Halyard's grey on the sleeves, and at the right one, where the sleeve had ridden up on the door, and at the white line.

"Father," she said, not loud, past him, into the office. "There's a man come out through the locked door."

"I know, Julia. Go to bed."

"He's got a struck line."

"Go to bed."

She did not go to bed. She stood in the dark with the unlit lamp and looked at Jonah, and Jonah looked back, because there was nothing else to do with a girl of sixteen who had seen a struck line and said so, and he thought, that's a face that'll be trouble for somebody. That's a face that's going to look at a thing and say what it is, out loud, in a room, for the rest of its life, and there's a woman in the City with that face, and I've said her name every night for seven years.

"Good night," he said, in the Belt turn.

"You're not a Halyard," Julia Morrow said.

"No."

"Then what are you."

"Ask me in a month," Jonah said, and went past her into the dark of the yard, and did not look back, and heard, behind him, the office door close, and Morrow's voice, low, saying his daughter's name, and hers saying, "But Father, he's got Tom Vale's," and the door shutting on the rest.

He stood in the yard.

It was the yard. He had cut his first plate in it at fifteen, the aft seam of the Halcyon, and cried, and it was dark now with the sun-lamps down and the two hulls gone to the fleet and the cradles empty, and it smelled of torch-smoke and cold steel, and there was nobody in it, and he stood in the middle of it with the hood up and said, to the thing in the doorway of him, in the old tongue, because it was listening:

"That's two. Gaspar's had his shard and Morrow's had his slab. One's a drunk who did nothing and I've paid him for a story, and one's a good man who did everything and I've paid him for a yard, and neither of them knows who paid." He looked at the office window, where the lamp was. "He made Marchand pay for the fire. He sat in the clean office and made him count it twice. Nobody told me that. It wasn't on any slate."

The thing in the doorway did the small thing.

"I know," Jonah said. "There's room. There's always room. It's the only thing about a count that's kind." He put his hands in the coat. "Tomorrow the Flamen. Then I'll have the reading, and I'll know what I am, and I'll have a name for it, and I'll go."

He went out of the yard by the cutters' gate, which had no lock, because Morrow had never once thought a cutter would steal from him, and had been right.
