# Chapter 25: Inspection

He saw her from the line before she saw him, and he had three hundred breaths to decide what to do with his face, and he used all of them.

The Throat was as it had been on the first day. The dock cut into grey rock, the lamps too far apart, the cage hanging in its mouth with the wind coming up. The table. The slab on its stand, the big one, dark, with a depth in it. Except that on the first day there had been one Flamen behind it and a Blank with a list, and today there were five robes and a prefect and a Centurion, and behind them, against the rock, sixteen slabs of the Vein leaning in a row where Tullus's men had carried them up, and a woman with a book on her knee sitting on a stool at the end of the row, reading.

She had not looked up. She was reading the slabs. She had been reading them, Rennick said down the line, since the College came down the cage the night before; she and the old one had sat in the garrison hall with the slabs and a lamp until the fourth bell and had not stopped, and when they'd come down to the Throat at dawn she'd had the slabs brought with her, and had gone on.

She had the same colouring. That was the first thing. He'd kept her face for six years in the place where he kept things, beside a cup of the best water on Anvil, and he'd thought, some nights, that he'd kept it wrong, that a face kept six years in the dark became a thing you'd drawn and not a thing you'd seen. He had not kept it wrong. She was twenty-five and she sat on a stool at the bottom of the Throat with the grey scholar's robe over what was under it and the sleeve pushed back, and on the forearm where the sleeve was pushed back was the broad stripe, and her head was on one side, considering, the way it had been at the yard rail, and she was reading a slab of the Vein in the old tongue with her lips moving very slightly, and she had a book on her knee that was not the College's, and a stylus, and she wrote in it, once, and went on reading.

He stood in the line with decury six and looked at her, and Tacitus, in the doorway of him, felt his heart go, and did not understand it, and noticed.

That was new. It had noticed his heart on the derelict and stopped there, interested. It had felt his heart six years, fast and slow, and had never once, that he knew, thought anything about it. It thought something now. He felt it turn, in the doorway, and look at him the way it had on the road when he said sorry, and he could not tell it, because a Flamen with a wand was walking the line, and he thought instead, in the old tongue, as flat as he could make it: not now. Be nothing. I asked you. Now.

It went down.

Not the way it had gone down on Anvil, flat in a ditch. It went down the way a man goes down into a cellar he has been asked to wait in, on purpose, because it had a name and had been asked, and Jonah stood in the line at the bottom of the Throat with his heart going and nothing in him at all, and thought about his face.

He had been shaved at the Throat six years ago and had shaved since with a chisel edge, the way the road did; his skull was bare and had a scar across it from the riot. He had a white line on his forearm where a Blank's line had been for a week. He had lost a stone twice and put it back once. He was thirty and had been nineteen, and he had stood on a rock for five years being beaten by a stick and sitting at a dinner that was not there, and the old man had said, on the last night of Bearing, that he no longer stood like a man who had been beaten a great deal; he stood like a man who had never once been interested in anything.

So he stood like that.

---

They read the slabs first. Pell had been right.

The old Flamen did it. He was the Master of Readings, Rennick said, a man of sixty with a face like the Flamen who had cut Jonah's collar and eyes that had read a great many arms, and he stood in front of the sixteen slabs with the prefect beside him and read them the way a man reads a ledger he has been handed by a dead clerk, without any weight, aloud, in the old tongue, so that the line could hear and understand none of it.

Jonah understood all of it.

"A count," the Master of Readings said. "Twenty-three years. Every intake. Numbers, and names where the man gave one, and a father or a field where he gave that. In the College's hand; a Master's hand, prefect, this is a man who wrote the rolls before he wrote this." He touched the first slab. "Three sixty-six. The first line is his own name. Aulus Terentius Pharus, Latium, the College, Senator."

Tullus's grey face did nothing.

"That name is struck," the Master of Readings said. "It was struck in three sixty-six by decree and it is not to be written or spoken and it is on this slab in the hand of the man who bore it, and I will have to write to the College about that, prefect, because a struck name on a slab of the Matter in a room at the bottom of your Throat is a thing the College does not know about, and has kept a man alive to write for twenty-three years, and I will need to know who authorised the lamp."

"The galleries," Tullus said. His voice was flat. "He did the galleries."

"He designed the galleries. I know who he was. Everyone my age knows who he was; we were novices when he stood up in the Senate." The old Flamen looked at the slab. "He was the best Master the College has had since the Finding and he went mad about the wall and they cut him in the Forum and I watched, and I did not know he had lived, and I did not know he was here, and I would like very much to know why I did not know." He moved down the row. "Sixteen. The last." He bent. He read, and stopped.

Jonah watched him stop.

"A seal," the Master of Readings said, quietly. "In the margin. A Master's seal, drawn from memory, on a slab, with a chisel point." He straightened. "Nothing. He was Master; it was his to draw. It opens nothing on a slab. And below it." He bent again. "His own name. Again. Given to the wall he drew, in the twenty-third year. Counted." He was silent a moment. "That is not his hand."

The line did not breathe.

"It is the same tongue and the same form and it is not his hand. It is larger. It is a hand that learned to write by cutting, and has not cut long." The old Flamen turned and looked at Tullus. "He had a servant. A convict, who came to the room, and wrote him in when he died, and put him against the wall, because that is what the line says and the room was empty. Your locked room, prefect, with one key, that a man was carried out of. I would like to know how, and I would like to know who, and I am going to read every man on this road until I do."

"That is what you are here for," Tullus said.

"Yes." The Master of Readings looked at the slab a moment longer. Then he looked down the row, at the woman on the stool at the end of it, who had not looked up. "Vibia. You have read these all night. Anything."

She looked up.

"The hand," she said. "The second one." Her voice was the voice from the slot; it had the endings right and a corner in it that her brother's had not had. "It's not a convict's. A convict who learned his letters from a Master would write like a Master, badly. This is a man who learned the tongue first and the letters after, from the same Master, and has his rhythm. Look at the line breaks. He breaks where Pharus broke." She turned a page in her own book. "And the seal is not drawn from memory. It's drawn from drill. The lines don't hesitate. He'd made it a hundred times before he cut it there. Whoever wrote the second hand had been taught to make that seal."

The Master of Readings looked at her for a long moment.

"Taught," he said. "Why."

"I don't know. It opens one door in the Empire and it's on Latium." She closed the book. "I'm saying what's on the slab. That's what I was sent for."

"Read the line," the old Flamen said. "With Pollio. Every man. Start at the cage end."

---

Pollio read him first.

He was not young anymore. He'd been young in the fourth year with a clean robe and a frown and had been young in the sixth with the same, and he was a Flamen now, not a novice, with a robe that had seen the Throat three times and a face that had decided, somewhere in the last two years, what it was for, and when the line moved and decury six came to the slab and Jonah put his arm on it, Pollio did not look at the glass. He looked at Jonah's face first.

"This one," he said.

"Read him," said the Master of Readings, from the row of slabs.

Pollio laid his hands on the glass and read.

Jonah held. He did not hold the way he had held six years ago, a second heart pressed flat inside the first with his teeth aching. He'd asked. Tacitus was in the cellar of him, waiting, on purpose, and the glass looked into the cellar and found nothing, because there was nothing there that a glass had a line for, and Jonah stood with his arm on the dark and his face doing what a Broadstripe's did at a fish course, and felt, through the plate under his skin and the stand of the slab and the rock of the dock, five hearts. The Master's, slow. Tullus's, wrong. Sabinus's, at the wall, bored. Pollio's, fast.

And one more, at the end of the row, on a stool, that had been slow all morning and was not now.

"Void," Pollio said. "Clean. And." He stopped. He tilted the glass. "There. The wobble. Master, this is the one I wrote. Fourth year and sixth. Four four seven one. Cut at intake, reads void, reads void with a wobble every time, and he's been on this road six years, and the wall takes cut ones in a year."

"So?"

"So he should be in the wall. And he isn't. And the room at the end of the Deep road is empty and the man who wrote the second hand learned his letters from a Master, and this one." Pollio looked at Jonah's face again. "This one was a citizen for a week. It's on the intake. He'd have had letters."

The Master of Readings came down the row.

He was a man of sixty who had read a great many arms and he laid his hands on the slab beside Pollio's and looked down into it for a long time, and Jonah stood with his arm on the glass and his heart going and nothing in him, and thought about a fish.

"Void," the old Flamen said at last. "There is a wobble. Pollio is right; there is a thing in the lines that does not sit. It is what a fever looks like, and what a man looks like whom the wall has had its hand on for six years and not closed it, and I have seen that twice in forty years and both of those men were dead within the month." He looked at Jonah's face. "You are dying, four four seven one."

"Yes, Master."

"You have been dying six years."

"Yes, Master. Corr said a month."

"Who is Corr?"

"He's in the wall, Master. Slab sixteen. North yards. He said a month."

The old Flamen looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked at the slab, and back, and something in his face shifted, and it was not suspicion. It was a man who had watched a Master cut in the Forum forty years ago and had just heard a convict say slab sixteen the way the Master would have said it.

"Vibia," he said, without turning. "Read him."

---

She came down the row with the book under her arm.

She did not hurry. She walked the way she'd walked away up the yard road on Founding Day with her hands in her pockets, and she stopped at the slab and set the book on the stand beside it and looked at the glass and not at Jonah, and put her hands flat on either side of his arm, and read.

She read for a long time.

He felt her heart through the stand. It had been slow all morning on the stool, reading a dead man's count, and it had been fast since Pollio said this one, and it was fast now, and steady, the way a duellist's heart is fast and steady when the other man has stepped, and she was looking at the glass, and then she was looking at his arm.

At the white line on it. Where a Blank's line had been for a week, drawn by a thing in a Flamens' house on Anvil the way a child draws a house, and had been cut out on this slab six years ago, and had been drawn again, white, by the same thing, so that it looked like what a cut line looked like.

She looked at it for the length of five of her heartbeats. He counted them.

Then she looked at his face.

He'd had three hundred breaths in the line and had used them. He'd decided on the face. It was the face from the rock, the one the old man had made him sit in for three hours with a fish that was not there, and he wore it now, and it was bored, and under it his heart was going the way it had gone in a hold with a Stripe's fang at his captain's throat, and Tacitus, in the cellar, waiting on purpose, felt it, and noticed, and did the thing it had done for six years, which was nothing, because it had been asked.

She looked at him the way she'd looked at him at the yard rail. Considering. Finding out where the weight was. And her face did not do the thing faces did. It did what her brother's had done over a seal, behind a table, for the length of a breath: it kept doing what it was doing, on its own, and behind it, for that breath, nobody was home.

Then she was back.

"Number," she said. In the old tongue. Quietly.

"Four four seven one."

He said it in the road's tongue, because that was what a convict spoke, and because if he had said it in hers his voice would have had the endings right, and she had heard him say a prayer once with the endings right in front of a province, and had said, you did it on purpose.

She wrote it in her book. Not the College's. Hers. She wrote for longer than a number takes.

"Void," she said, to the Master, in the old tongue. "There's the wobble. It's a fever; look at him, he's sweating through his shirt at the bottom of a Throat in winter. Pollio's right that he should be in the wall and Pollio's wrong that it means anything but that the wall is slow with this one. It's slow with some. Slab nine has a man who took four years." She took her hands off the glass. "He's a cut one who's dying slowly and a purser's pilot who had letters for a week. If a Master wanted a servant who could write, he'd have picked a man who could read. Mark him void."

"And the hand?"

"I'll look at the hand again when I've read the rest of the road," Livia Vibia said. "There are sixty men on it. I'd like to see all of them before I say a convict wrote a Master's seal from drill, because I don't believe it, and I'd like to be wrong about it in front of the whole line and not the half."

The Master of Readings looked at her, and at Jonah, and at the glass.

"Void," he said. "Mark. Next."

Jonah took his arm off the slab.

He did not look at her. He turned, the way a man turns who has been told next, and walked back down the dock toward the cage with decury six, and behind him her heart was fast and steady through the rock, and his was the other thing, and Tacitus in the cellar of him felt both, and noticed, and for the first time in six years wanted to know what a thing was, and had no way to ask.

---

They read the line for two days. He did not sleep for either of them.

Pell said nothing about it. Pell had stopped asking things in the fourth year and had stopped saying them in the sixth, mostly, except to the wall, and he lay on the shelf above Jonah's and sang some nights and did not others, and on the second night, in the dark, with the lamp at the end that never went out, he said, to the roof of the gallery:

"The Broadstripe. The woman. She looked at you longer."

"She read me twice."

"She read the whole road once each. You, twice, and longer the second, and then she said void and told the old one he was wrong to his face in front of the prefect." The old man from Marl was quiet a moment. "I've been on the Limes sixty-six years. I've never seen a Stripe tell a Stripe he was wrong for a Collar. I saw it on Anvil, they said, at the sand, years back, some quaestor's sister said it about a cut one who'd won a bout. That's what the north yards said, when the north yards came down. A quaestor's sister said he won." He turned over. "I'm not asking. I'm saying I heard it, and I've a memory, and it's hers or it's another one, and there's not two."

Jonah lay with his hands on his chest.

"There's not two," he said.

"No." Pell breathed. "Then she knows you."

"I don't know."

"She knows you. A man's face doesn't change; a man's number does. She looked at your arm for a long time and then she looked at your face for a long time and then she lied to a Master for you in the old tongue, and I don't speak it, cut one, but I know what a lie sounds like in any tongue, it's the one thing they've all got the same." The old man was quiet. "She knows you. And she wrote something in her own book. And she's going up the Throat in a day with sixteen slabs of the rock's count and a Master's seal in the margin that she says a convict couldn't have drawn, and she's going to sit on a transport for a month with all that in her lap and think."

"Yes."

"That's how it goes."

"Yes."

Pell did not say anything else. After a while he began to sing, and it was the song from the north forty, the one with the drainage in it, and Jonah lay under it and thought about a woman on a stool with a book on her knee that was not the College's, reading a slab of the Vein with her lips moving, and about what was on the slab, which was six hundred and forty-two names and a Master's and one at the top that he had not been able to say for six weeks, and about the fact that she had read all of them, in the old tongue, all night, with a lamp, and had looked up from the last line at the man who had cut it and had said, mark him void.

She had the book of names in her lap. Not a copy. The slabs. The old man had said the slabs were a copy and Jonah was the book, and that was true, and it was also true that sixteen slabs of the Vein with six hundred and forty-two names on them were going up the Throat tomorrow in the arms of the only Stripe who had ever asked his.

He did not know what she would do with it. He did not know what she had seen on the glass or in his face or in the white line on his arm that a thing had drawn to look cut. He knew that her heart had been fast and steady, the way a duellist's is when the other man has stepped, and that she had written for longer than a number takes, and that she had told a Master he was wrong before the whole line and not the half.

"Because it was true," he said, aloud, in the dark, in the old tongue, to nobody. "And nobody else was going to."

Above him, Pell stopped singing.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something somebody said once. Go to sleep."

"You go to sleep," the old man said. "You're the one dying," and turned to the wall, and sang.

---

The College went up the Throat on the third day with the slabs, and Jonah was at the wall of the seventh chamber and did not see them go.

He felt them. He felt the cage come down and go up, four times, with the weight of the Vein in it, and he felt the fifth time, which was robes and a prefect and a stool, and he felt, in the last of the five, through the rock and the plate and six years, one heart go up the Throat that was slow again, the way it had been on the stool, reading, and he stood with the chisel on the wall and the Vein opening for him like a door and thought, there it goes. The count. Up, into the cold, in her lap.

Decimus came down the road at the dusk count.

He said the numbers. He came to decury six and said four four seven one and Jonah said it back, and the old guard made his mark and did not go on. He stood with the list in his hand and looked at Jonah in the lamp with the face that had waited in the tunnel, and then he looked down the road, at the cage end, where the stool had been, and said, in the voice for things that were not personal:

"She asked me your name."

Jonah did not move.

"On the dock. Before the cage. She stood with the book under her arm and the old one shouting for the slabs and she said, guard, the cut one, four four seven one, what was he called on the intake. And I said, Vale, my lady. Citizen, for a week. Off Anvil." Decimus looked at the list. "And she said, thank you. In the old tongue, and then in mine, so I'd have it in both. And she went up the cage."

"What did she write?"

"I don't read the old tongue, four four seven one. I read numbers." The old guard made another mark on his list that Jonah could not see. "She wrote a long time. That's all I've got. It's not much. It's what a guard has." He went on down the road.

Jonah stood at the wall.

Tacitus, in the doorway of him, up out of the cellar now, had heard a guard say Vale, my lady, and had felt his heart, and had noticed, and had wanted, for two days, to know what a thing was, and had no way to ask, and it did the only thing it had.

It waited to be told.

"Later," Jonah said, in the old tongue, very quietly, to the wall. "I'll tell you later. I don't know what it is either."
