# Chapter 25: Inspection

He saw her from the line before she saw him, and he had three hundred breaths to decide what to do with his face, and he used all of them.

The Throat was as it had been on the first day: grey rock, lamps too far apart, the cage hanging in its mouth with the wind coming up. The table. The slab on its stand, dark, with a depth in it. That first day there had been one Flamen behind it and a Blank with a list. Today there were five robes, a prefect, a Centurion. Against the rock, sixteen slabs of the Vein leaned in a row where Tullus's men had carried them up. A woman with a book on her knee sat on a stool at the end of the row, reading.

She had not looked up. She had been reading since the College came down the cage the night before, Rennick said down the line. She and the old one had sat in the garrison hall with a lamp until the fourth bell, and had not stopped since.

She had the same colouring. He had kept her face six years, beside a cup of the best water on Anvil, and thought sometimes he had kept it wrong. He had not. She was twenty-five. Grey scholar's robe, sleeve pushed back, the broad stripe on her forearm. Her head was tilted, considering. She read a slab of the Vein in the old tongue with her lips moving very slightly, a book on her knee that was not the College's, and a stylus. She wrote in it once, and went on reading.

He stood in the line with decury six and looked at her. Tacitus felt his heart go, and did not understand it, and noticed.

That was new. It had noticed his heart on the derelict and stopped there, interested, and in six years had never once thought anything about it. It thought something now. He felt it turn and look at him, the way it had on the road when he said sorry. He could not tell it; a Flamen with a wand was walking the line. He thought instead, in the old tongue, as flat as he could make it: not now. Be nothing. I asked you. Now.

It went down. Not flat in a ditch, as it had on Anvil. It went down like a man going into a cellar he has been asked to wait in, on purpose, because it had a name and had been asked. Jonah stood in the line with his heart going and nothing in him at all, and thought about his face.

His skull was bare, scarred from the riot. A white line ran on his forearm where a Blank's line had been for a week. The old man had told him, on the last night of Bearing, that he no longer stood like a man who had been beaten a great deal. He stood like a man who had never once been interested in anything.

So he stood like that.

---

They read the slabs first. Pell had been right.

The old Flamen did it. Master of Readings, Rennick said, sixty years old, with the eyes of a man who had read a great many arms. He stood in front of the sixteen slabs with the prefect beside him and read them without weight, aloud, in the old tongue, so the line could hear it and understand none of it.

Jonah understood all of it.

"A count," the Master of Readings said. "Twenty-three years. Every intake. Numbers, and names where the man gave one, and a father or a field where he gave that. In the College's hand. A Master's hand, prefect; this is a man who wrote the rolls before he wrote this." He touched the first slab. "Three sixty-six. The first line is his own name. Aulus Terentius Pharus, Latium, the College, Senator."

Tullus's grey face did nothing.

"That name is struck," the Master of Readings said. "Struck in three sixty-six by decree. Not to be written or spoken, and it's on this slab in the hand of the man who bore it. I'll have to write to the College about that, prefect. A struck name on a slab of the Matter in a room at the bottom of your Throat is something the College does not know about, and has kept a man alive to write for twenty-three years. I'll need to know who authorised the lamp."

"The galleries," Tullus said, flat. "He did the galleries."

"He designed the galleries. I know who he was. Everyone my age does; we were novices when he stood up in the Senate." The old Flamen looked at the slab. "Best Master the College has had since the Finding. He went mad about the wall, and they cut him in the Forum, and I watched. I didn't know he'd lived. I didn't know he was here. I'd like very much to know why I didn't." He moved down the row. "Sixteen. The last." He bent. He read, and stopped.

Jonah watched him stop.

"A seal," the Master of Readings said, quietly. "In the margin. A Master's seal, drawn from memory, with a chisel point." He straightened. "Nothing much. He was Master; it was his to draw. It opens nothing on a slab. And below it." He bent again. "His own name. Again. Given to the wall he drew, in the twenty-third year. Counted." He was silent a moment. "That's not his hand."

The line did not breathe.

"Same tongue, same form, and not his hand. Larger. A hand that learned to write by cutting, and hasn't cut long." The old Flamen looked up at Tullus. "He had a servant. A convict, who came to the room, wrote him in when he died, put him against the wall, because that's what the line says and the room was empty. Your locked room, prefect, one key, and a man was carried out of it. I'd like to know how. I'd like to know who. And I'm going to read every man on this road until I do."

"That's what you're here for," Tullus said.

"Yes." The old Flamen looked at the slab a moment longer. Then down the row, at the woman on the stool, who had not looked up. "Vibia. You've read these all night. Anything."

She looked up.

"The hand," she said. "The second one." Her voice had the endings right, and a corner in it her brother's had not had. "It's not a convict's. A convict who learned letters from a Master would write like a Master, badly. This is a man who learned the tongue first and the letters after, from the same Master, and has his rhythm. Look at the line breaks. He breaks where Pharus broke." She turned a page in her own book. "And the seal's not drawn from memory. It's drawn from drill. The lines don't hesitate. He'd made it a hundred times before he cut it there. Whoever wrote the second hand was taught to make that seal."

The Master of Readings looked at her a long moment.

"Taught," he said. "Why."

"I don't know. It opens one door in the Empire and it's on Latium." She closed the book. "I'm saying what's on the slab. That's what I was sent for."

"Read the line," the old Flamen said. "With Pollio. Every man. Start at the cage end."

---

Pollio read him first.

He was not young anymore. A Flamen now, a robe that had seen the Throat three times, a face that had decided what it was for. When decury six came to the slab and Jonah put his arm on it, Pollio did not look at the glass first. He looked at Jonah's face.

"This one," he said.

"Read him," said the Master of Readings, from the row of slabs.

Pollio laid his hands on the glass and read.

Jonah held. This was not six years ago, a second heart pressed flat inside the first, his teeth aching. He had asked. Tacitus was in the cellar of him, waiting, on purpose. The glass looked in and found nothing, because there was nothing there a glass had a line for. He stood with his face doing what a Broadstripe's did at a fish course. Through the plate and the stand and the rock he felt five hearts. The Master's, slow. Tullus's, wrong. Sabinus's, at the wall, bored. Pollio's, fast.

And one more, at the end of the row, that had been slow all morning and was not now.

"Void," Pollio said. "Clean. And." He stopped. He tilted the glass. "There. The wobble. Master, this is the one I wrote. Fourth year and sixth. Four four seven one. Cut at intake, reads void with a wobble every time, and he's been on this road six years, and the wall takes cut ones in a year."

"So?"

"So he should be in the wall. He isn't. The room at the end of the Deep road is empty. Whoever wrote the second hand learned his letters from a Master." Pollio looked at Jonah's face again. "This one was a citizen for a week. It's on the intake. He'd have had letters."

The Master of Readings came down the row.

He laid his hands on the slab beside Pollio's and looked into it a long time. Jonah stood with his arm on the glass, his heart going, nothing in him, and thought about a fish.

"Void," the old Flamen said at last. "There's a wobble. Pollio's right; there's something in the lines that doesn't sit. It's what a fever looks like, and what a man looks like whom the wall's had its hand on for six years and not closed. I've seen that twice in forty years, and both men were dead within the month." He looked at Jonah's face. "You're dying, four four seven one."

"Yes, Master."

"You've been dying six years."

"Yes, Master. Corr said a month."

"Who is Corr?"

"He's in the wall, Master. Slab sixteen. North yards. He said a month."

The old Flamen looked at him a moment longer, then at the slab, then back. Something shifted in his face, and it was not suspicion. It was a man who had watched a Master cut in the Forum forty years ago, hearing a convict say slab sixteen in a Master's cadence.

"Vibia," he said, without turning. "Read him."

---

She came down the row with the book under her arm.

She did not hurry. She walked the same unhurried way she had walked up the yard road on Founding Day, hands in her pockets. She stopped at the slab and set the book on the stand. She looked at the glass, not at Jonah. She put her hands flat on either side of his arm, and read.

She read a long time.

He felt her heart through the stand. Slow all morning, on the stool, reading a dead man's count. Fast since Pollio said this one. Fast now, and steady, the way a duellist's heart goes fast and steady once the other man has stepped. She looked at the glass, then at his arm.

At the white line, drawn by something in a Flamens' house on Anvil, a child's version of a house. Cut off this slab six years ago. Drawn again, white, to look like a cut line.

She looked at it five heartbeats. He counted them.

Then she looked at his face.

He had used all three hundred breaths in the line. He had the face from the rock, the one the old man had made him sit in for three hours with a fish that was not there. He wore it now, bored. Under it his heart pounded the same as it had in a hold, with a Stripe's fang at his captain's throat. Tacitus, in the cellar, waiting on purpose, felt it, and noticed. It did what it had done for six years, which was nothing, because it had been asked.

She looked at him the way she had looked at him at the yard rail. Considering. Finding the weight. Her face did not do what faces did. It did what her brother's had done over a seal, behind a table, for the length of a breath: kept moving on its own, and behind it, for that breath, nobody was home.

Then she was back.

"Number," she said, in the old tongue. Quietly.

"Four four seven one."

He said it in the road's tongue, because that was what a convict spoke, and because if he had said it in hers his voice would have had the endings right. She had heard him say a prayer once with the endings right in front of a province, and had said, you did it on purpose.

She wrote it in her book. Not the College's. Hers. She wrote longer than a number takes.

"Void," she said, to the Master, in the old tongue. "There's the wobble. It's a fever; look at him, sweating through his shirt at the bottom of a Throat in winter. Pollio's right that he should be in the wall, and wrong that it means anything but the wall being slow with this one. It's slow with some. Slab nine has a man who took four years." She took her hands off the glass. "He's a cut one dying slowly, and a purser's pilot who had letters for a week. If a Master wanted a servant who could write, he'd have picked a man who could read. Mark him void."

"And the hand?"

"I'll look at the hand again when I've read the rest of the road," Livia Vibia said. "There are sixty men on it. I'd like to see all of them before I say a convict wrote a Master's seal from drill, because I don't believe it. I'd like to be wrong in front of the whole line, not the half."

The Master of Readings looked at her, at Jonah, at the glass.

"Void," he said. "Mark. Next."

Jonah took his arm off the slab and walked back down the dock toward the cage with decury six. He did not look at her. Behind him her heart was fast and steady through the rock, and his was the other thing. Tacitus felt both, and noticed. For the first time in six years it wanted to know what something was, and had no way to ask.

---

They read the line for two days. He did not sleep for either of them.

Pell said nothing about it. He had stopped asking things in the fourth year and stopped saying them in the sixth, mostly, except to the wall. He sang some nights and not others. On the second night, in the dark, he said, to the roof of the gallery:

"The Broadstripe. The woman. She looked at you longer."

"She read me twice," Jonah said. "Everybody else on this road got read once. I should feel honoured. Mostly I feel weighed."

"That a joke?"

"An attempt."

"Didn't land."

Jonah said, "No. They rarely do, before a Flamen's had his hands on my arm."

"She read the whole road once each. You, twice, longer the second time. Then she told the old one he was wrong to his face, in front of the prefect." Pell was quiet a moment. "I've been on the Limes sixty-six years. Never seen a Stripe tell a Stripe he was wrong for a Collar. Once, on Anvil, at the sand, years back. A quaestor's sister said a cut one had won a bout. That's what the north yards said, when they came down." He turned over. "I'm not asking. I'm saying I heard it, and I've a memory. It's hers or it's another one, and there's not two."

Jonah lay with his hands on his chest.

Jonah said, "There's not two. I'd know. I've been counting things on this road for six years. It's the one habit they never got out of me."

"No." Pell breathed. "Then she knows you."

"She knows a face from a yard rail six years gone, and a convict at the bottom of a Throat who isn't supposed to have letters," Jonah said. "That's not the same as knowing me. I'm hoping."

"She knows you. A man's face doesn't change; a man's number does. She looked at your arm a long time and then your face a long time. Then she lied to a Master for you in the old tongue. I don't speak it, cut one, but I know what a lie sounds like. It's the one thing every tongue's got the same." He was quiet. "She's going up the Throat with sixteen slabs and a seal she says a convict couldn't have drawn. A month on a transport with all that in her lap, to think."

"Then I hope she thinks kindly," Jonah said.

"That's how it goes."

"It's how it always goes," Jonah said. "Somebody with more stripe than me decides what I am, and I find out after, from a guard who happened to be standing there."

Pell said nothing more. After a while he sang the song from the north forty, the one with the drainage in it. Jonah lay under it and thought about a woman with a book that was not the College's. She had read six hundred and forty-two names all night, and looked up from the last line to say, mark him void.

She had the old man's slabs in her lap now. Not a copy. The sixteen of them, going up the Throat tomorrow in the arms of the only Stripe who had ever asked his name.

He did not know what she would do with it. He knew her heart had been fast and steady. He knew she had told a Master he was wrong, before the whole line.

"Because it was true," he said aloud, in the dark, in the old tongue, to nobody. "And nobody else was going to."

Above him, Pell stopped singing.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something somebody said once. Go to sleep."

"You go to sleep," the old man said. "You're the one dying," and turned to the wall, and sang.

---

The College went up the Throat on the third day with the slabs, and Jonah was at the wall of the seventh chamber and did not see them go.

He felt them. The cage came down and went up, four times, with the weight of the Vein in it. The fifth time was robes and a prefect and a stool. In the last of the five, one heart went up slow again, the same slow it had been on the stool, reading. He stood with the chisel on the wall and the Vein opening for him, and thought: there it goes. The count. Up, into the cold, in her lap.

Decimus came down the road at the dusk count.

He said the numbers. He came to decury six, said four four seven one, and Jonah said it back. The old guard made his mark and did not go on. He looked at Jonah in the lamp, the face from the tunnel. Then down the road, at the cage end, where the stool had been, and said, in the voice for things that were not personal:

"She asked me your name."

Jonah did not move.

"On the dock. Before the cage. She stood with the book under her arm and the old one shouting for the slabs, and she said, guard, the cut one, four four seven one, what was he called on the intake. And I said, Vale, my lady. Citizen, for a week. Off Anvil." Decimus looked at the list. "She said, thank you. In the old tongue, and then in mine, so I'd have it in both. She went up the cage."

"What did she write?"

"I don't read the old tongue, four four seven one. I read numbers." He made another mark Jonah could not see. "She wrote a long time. That's all I've got. It's not much. It's what a guard has."

Jonah said, "It's plenty. Thank you."

"For what."

"For telling a dying man what a woman with a book thought of him, instead of telling him to shut up and cut," Jonah said. "You didn't have to give me that."

Decimus looked at him a moment in the lamp.

"No," he said. "I didn't." He went on down the road.

Jonah stood at the wall.

Tacitus, out of the cellar now, had heard a guard say Vale, my lady. It had felt his heart, and noticed. It had wanted, two days now, to know what something was, and had no way to ask. It did the only thing it had.

It waited to be told.

Jonah said, in the old tongue, very quietly, to the wall, "Later. I'll tell you later. I don't know what it is either."
