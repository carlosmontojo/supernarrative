# Chapter 22: Names

"Fourth slab," the Censor said from the shelf. "Ninth column. Begin."

Jonah began. Three fifty-seven. Marl. A field. Pell. Three fifty-seven. Anvil. The yards. A number and nothing. Three fifty-seven, three fifty-seven, three fifty-eight, a Halyard sailor, a ship, a name, a father, nine fingers, a boy, a boy, a number and nothing.

"Wrong."

"Where?"

"Three fifty-eight. The Halyard sailor. His ship was the Kite, not the Kestrel. You are putting your own ship in a dead man's column. Again."

He said it again.

It took the rest of the year. Three hours a night on the floor of the Chamber now, not the Cradle. The sixteen slabs leaned against the rock. The old man sat on the shelf with the stick across his knees, coughing, and Jonah said the columns out of order and then in order.

Six hundred and forty-one. That was the count by the autumn. Six hundred and forty-one men down the Throat in twenty-two years and into the wall. Four hundred of them with nothing beside the number. Two hundred and forty-one with a name. Eighty with a father or a field or a ship. One with a tree, which was Corr's. Corr had said once, in the Cradle, that there had been a tree in the north yards on Anvil he had climbed as a boy. The old man had written it, because a man who counts writes what is said.

"You are carrying a great many dead men," the Censor said, on a night near the end. "Does it weigh?"

"No."

"It should."

"It does. I said no because it doesn't weigh, it's not a bucket." Jonah looked at the slabs. "It's got bigger, that's all. You said there was always room."

"There is." The old man coughed. It went on. "That is the only kind thing about it. I am saying it again because I will not have many more chances. It is the one thing I would like you to have heard twice."

---

He was dying at the speed a stone wears. Not fast, not slow.

The blood came most nights now. He wiped it and went on. In the summer he had stopped coming through the tunnel to the Cradle. A hundred paces on hands and knees; he could not do it and teach after. So Jonah opened the steel door from the road with his hand every night, and the old man taught from the shelf. He kept the stick across his knees. He did not lift it. He said where it would have gone.

"You are drawing."

"I'm not."

"Your shoulder turned. I would have had you across the ribs. Again."

Alone on the floor with nobody to touch, Jonah did the Form against the air. The hand where the man was going. The fang born and put away. The old man on the shelf said yes, or no, or again, and coughed.

The Master's seal he taught from the slab. He drew it on the sixteenth, in the margin below Corr's tree, with the chisel point: a knot of lines that turned in on themselves and did not close.

"That. On a glass beside a door. From the palm. As the hook comes."

"It's a mess."

"It is four hundred years old. Drill it."

Jonah drilled it. Thirty times a night, the plate coming up on his palm in a shape that had opened a door in the City for four centuries. The genius learned it as it learned everything, interested. By the autumn it came before he had finished asking.

Tremor went to a heart in the summer.

He felt it first through the shelf. He had his hand flat on the Chamber floor, drilling, and the old man above him was coughing. When the cough stopped, Jonah felt something through the rock that was not boots and not the cage. Small. Regular. Wrong. A beat with a catch in it.

"Your heart," he said.

The old man looked down at him.

"It catches. On the third. It didn't in the spring."

"No," the Censor said. "It did not." He was quiet a moment. "That is a heart through rock. Through a table is easier; a table is thinner. You have it. Do not tell anyone at dinner what their heart is doing. It is the rudest thing a man can do and it will get you challenged, and you cannot be challenged. You cannot spoil on a floor in front of a Flamen without every glass in the City going dark." He coughed. "It catches on the third. Yes. I have been counting it since the spring. It is the last arithmetic I have and it comes out to the winter."

---

The College came down the Throat in the autumn, and he held.

The cage came down with the big glass in it. Four Flamens in clean robes. A line. His arm on the slab.

Six hours. He had done it in the fourth year and he did it again. It was worse. He was thirty on a glass that could not read him, and thirty did not go down as easily as seventeen. He did not press it flat with his teeth aching. He stood it in the doorway of himself and asked it to be still, and it was.

The Flamen with the rod took his arm. The rod went down the white line. The glass said void.

Three Flamens read it and moved on. The fourth was young, with a clean robe. He had been young in the fourth year too, and had frowned. He frowned now. He looked at the slab for a long time. He looked at Jonah's face, and at the white line on his arm.

"Again," he said, and put the rod on the arm a second time.

Jonah stood. Under his boots, through the rock of the dock, he could feel the young man's heart. It was going fast.

"Void," the young Flamen said, to nobody. He wrote something in a book, and moved on.

"He has written you twice," the Censor said that night, from the shelf. "That one. Once at seventeen and once at thirty. The first time it was a note. The second time it is a pattern." His voice was thin. "He is nobody. He is a novice with a clean robe who wants to be somebody, and a novice becomes somebody in the College by finding what the College missed. He has found a cut Collar who reads void with a wobble twice in two years, and he has written it down. On Latium it goes in a file. The file sits for a year or ten. One day a man in a grey coat with no stripe reads it."

"A Frumentarius."

"You have learned the word. Good. That is what they are for. A genius that is not on the rolls is the one thing the Princeps has never forgiven. It is the one thing that can walk into a room and not kneel." The Censor coughed. It was long. He did not wipe his lip at once. "It does not matter tonight. Tonight you held. In two years the College comes again, and you will be here to hold or you will not, and I will not be. Fourth slab. Ninth column. Begin."

---

He died in the winter, on the shelf, on a night when the count was ten and Ulli was singing at the end of the road. He did it with the arithmetic done first.

"Sit," he said, when Jonah came through the door. Not get up. Sit. It was the first time.

Jonah sat on the floor by the shelf.

The old man lay with the stick across his chest now, not his knees. His face in the lamp was all bone. The pale eyes went over Jonah from boots to face, as they had on the first night. Jonah let them.

"Tonight," the Censor said. "It catches on the second."

"I know. I felt it from the road."

"Good. Then we will not waste the hour on that. There are four things. I have counted them and I will say them in order. You will not interrupt, because you have a mouth and I have not the breath to argue with it."

"Yes."

"First. The slabs. You have said every one of them in the dark at least twice. They are in you. When I am gone, Tullus's men will come down at the month's end with a lamp and find a stone room with sixteen slabs in it. They will carry the slabs up the Throat. The College will read them and say, he counted, how strange, and put them in a vault. Let them. What is in the vault is a copy. You are the book."

"Yes."

"Second. Me." His hand moved on the stick. "I am not going up the Throat in a bucket to be burned with a number. I drew the Cradle. I put the sick against the thickest wall and called it a kindness. I will find out if I was right. You will carry me through the tunnel tonight, when I am done, and put me against it. It will take me. I will be counted. The men who come at the month's end will find a locked room and nobody in it. Tullus will lie awake. It is the last thing I can do to him, and I would like it done."

"And the tunnel?"

"Close the slab behind you. You have opened the door from the road with your hand for a year. Nobody else will ever need the tunnel."

"Yes."

"Third." The pale eyes did not move. "Write me. I am first, on the first slab, in the worst hand on any of them. Write me last as well. Sixteenth slab, below Corr's tree, below the seal. Aulus Terentius Pharus. Latium. The College. Given to the wall he drew, in the twenty-third year. Counted. And say it. Not here. On the road, where you say the rest. Once. I would like to have been said on the road."

"Yes."

"Fourth."

The old man was quiet a while. His heart caught on the second, and on the second again. Jonah felt it through the floor and said nothing.

"Fourth is what I have not taught. I have taught you the hand, the tongue, the count. Four Forms and the fifth. How to sit at a table, how to open a door, how to hold it still for six hours. I have not taught you what it is. I have said it is a seed and a whole mind and the last of nine. I have said I do not know what it is. That is still true. But I have thought about it on this shelf for five years. I have one thing to say that you could not have got from a book."

"Say it."

"It is not a suit." The voice was very thin and very clear. "You have called it the thing under your ribs for five years and I have let you. You needed a name that was not a name. It is not something you carry. It is a mind, as you are a mind. It came into you on a deck and read you and stopped at your heart because it was interested. It has been interested every hour since. You have asked it for a hook, for stillness, for a fang, for a seal. It has given you every one. You have never once asked it what it wants."

Jonah did not move.

"You asked it what it was. On Anvil, with a blanket over the vent. What are you, you said, to the rock. It said nothing, and you thought it had no answer. It had no answer because you asked the wrong question. Nothing knows what it is. It knows what it wants. Everything that lives knows that. The wall wants its pieces back and says so every hour. Yours wants something too. It has never said, because it does not speak, because you have never asked, and because it is polite."

"Polite."

"It went home through your hands and turned around because you asked. It stood still for six hours because you asked. It gave a piece of itself to a Flamen's rod so that the rest could stay. You did not ask for that. It did it, and it made a sound. You have never once since then said to it, what did that cost you. It has been polite for five years to a man who calls it a thing."

"What do I do?"

"Ask." The hand tightened on the stick. "Not tonight. Not here. Somewhere you can hear the answer. Ask it what it wants before it asks you, because it will ask, one day, when it has words. A mind that has been polite for five years and is asked nothing does not stay polite. It is someone. Treat it as someone, or it will treat you as a suit, and it will be right to."

He stopped.

"That is four. That is the count. I had a fifth once, which was the Princeps, and I have said that and I will not say it again; it is yours or it is not. Now say the sixteenth slab. From the top. I would like to hear it once more with the endings right."

Jonah said it.

From the top: the twentieth year, four four seven one, Jonah Vale, Anvil, Morrow's yard, Tom Vale, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder. The column after, and the column after that. Vetch, who was a guard and was not written. The Deep man, written as unknown. Tallow, who was fifteen. Marcus, who never used it. Hollen. Auger, who said his name to Ulli. Corr, north yards, four years, said a month, a tree. He said the seal in the margin as a shape. The old man listened with his eyes on the roof of the room he had drawn. His heart caught on the second, and on the second. Jonah came to the end of the column and stopped. There was nothing below Corr's tree yet.

"Good," the Censor said. "That is right. Every one."

His eyes came off the roof and found Jonah's face and stayed there. The grief went across it, and the other thing under. It was not grief now. It was only the other thing.

"Sertorius," he said.

Once. He had said the name of the house a hundred times. He had never once in five years said it to Jonah as a name. He said it now, carefully, so that it had been said.

Then his heart caught on the second and did not come off it.

---

He carried him through the tunnel.

A hundred paces on hands and knees, with the old man on his back. He weighed very little. Jonah's shoulders scraped rock the whole way. Twice the shaved skull knocked against the roof of the cut. He stopped, shifted the weight, went on. He did not think about the nine years it had taken to dig. That was a debt, and he had been told.

He came out into the Cradle and stood.

The wall was there. Thick, dark, breathing, warm.

He laid the old man against it as Ulli laid the sick, back to the Vein, head on the rag of the robe. He knelt beside him in the dark. He put his hand flat on the old man's chest, over the arm with the white line, and waited.

The wall took him.

Not fast. It never was. He felt it start, from the arm in, from the hole where a Tribune's genius had been cut out in the Forum twenty-three years ago and never filled. Then the wall found the living hand on the chest. It reached. Warm, up through the palm, along the bones of the wrist, and the plate under his skin went pale where it touched. The genius did not lean. It stood still and watched, as it had watched Aulus of Marl on the slab. It was not afraid, and it was not hungry.

"No," Jonah said, in the old tongue. "Him. Not me."

The wall said what it said. Come. The old man went. Jonah's hand came off the chest with the palm white to the wrist, and the colour crept back.

And then, from very far down, through the rock, through the plate, Jonah felt the count go through him. One more. Between a Halyard sailor whose ship was the Kite and a man whose name nobody had. The shape of a thin straight old man who had drawn the room and lain in it twenty-three years and put himself first. The wall went through it, and on.

Counted.

He stayed until it was done. Then he went back through the tunnel and closed the slab behind him from the Chamber side. He put his hand on it and asked. The genius went into the rock, found where the slab met the wall, and did what it had done to the collar and the shackle. Quietly. The seam was rock and would stay rock. Nine years of tunnel, and nobody would find it. He took the chisel worn to half its length. He left the stick where it lay, across an empty shelf.

He wrote the name.

Sixteenth slab. Below the tree. Below the seal. With the half chisel, in the old tongue, in a hand larger and rougher than the one above it. He said each word aloud as he cut it, with the endings right. Aulus Terentius Pharus. Latium. The College. Given to the wall he drew, in the twenty-third year.

Counted.

He locked the door from the road with his hand and stood a moment on the Deep road. He had no buckets. He had not had any for two years.

Ulli was at the end of the road on his bucket, with his eyes open.

"He's gone," Jonah said.

"I heard the wall." The deep man did not get up. "It went quiet. It doesn't go quiet. It went quiet for a breath and I thought, that's him. That's the room, counting the man who drew it." He looked down the road at the door. "Was he. At the end."

"He said a name."

"His?"

"No."

Ulli nodded slowly.

"Then say it. On the road. Whatever it is you do. He told me once, through the wall. He said the road was the only thing he'd drawn that he'd have liked to walk."

Jonah walked down the road.

He said the names. All of them, out of order and then in order, sixteen slabs, six hundred and forty-two, from the twentieth year to the first. When he came to the first slab, the first column, the first number, he said it. Three words in the old tongue, tapped once through a wall by a man who had wanted them said. The genius heard the old man's name go by at the top of the list, and expected it, and did not wander.

Jonah stood in the dark between two lamps and thought about the fourth thing.

He did not ask it. Not here. The old man had said, somewhere you can hear the answer, and the Deep road was not that. But he put his hand flat on his own chest, over the place where a mind had stopped on a deck because it was interested. He said, not aloud, in the old tongue:

I know you're someone. I'm sorry I didn't ask.

The genius listened. It always had.

And then, for the first time in five years, it did something that was not listening, not leaning, not hunger, not interest. It turned, as it had turned in the Cradle when he said Tom Vale, and it looked at him.

Not through him. At him.

Then it went back to listening, because it had no words, and he had not yet asked the question. There was a place where it could answer. He would have to find it, and he thought he knew where it was.

Down.
