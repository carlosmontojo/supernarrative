# Chapter 22: Names

The old man made him read the slabs until he could say them in the dark, and then he made him say them in the dark, and then he died.

It took the rest of the year. Jonah would think of it, later, as the year of the columns, because that was what it was: three hours a night on the floor of the Chamber, now, not the Cradle, with the sixteen slabs leaning against the rock and the lamp turned to a thread and the old man on the shelf with the stick across his knees, coughing, saying, "Fourth slab. Ninth column. Begin."

And Jonah would begin. Three fifty-seven. Marl. A field. Pell. Three fifty-seven. Anvil. The yards. A number and nothing. Three fifty-seven, three fifty-seven, three fifty-eight, a Halyard sailor, a ship, a name, a father, nine fingers, a boy, a boy, a number and nothing. He said them the way he'd said the names on the road for five years, out of order and then in order, and the old man on the shelf said, "Wrong. Three fifty-eight, the Halyard sailor, his ship was the Kite, not the Kestrel, you are putting your own ship in a dead man's column, again," and Jonah said it again.

Six hundred and forty-one. That was the count, by the autumn. Six hundred and forty-one men who had come down the Throat in twenty-two years and gone into the wall, and four hundred of them with nothing beside the number, and two hundred and forty-one with a name, and eighty with a father or a field or a ship, and one with a tree, which was Corr's, because Corr had said, once, in the Cradle in his first year when the wall had reached for him and let go, that there'd been a tree on Anvil in the north yards that he'd climbed as a boy, and the old man had written it, because a man who counts writes what is said.

"You are carrying a great many dead men," the Censor said, on a night near the end. "Does it weigh?"

"No."

"It should."

"It does. I said no because you asked if it weighed and it doesn't weigh, it's not a bucket. It's." Jonah looked at the slabs. "It's the place where I keep things. It's got bigger. You said there was always room."

"There is." The old man coughed. It went on. "That is the only thing about it that is kind. I have said so. I am saying it again because I will not have many more chances and it is the thing I would like you to have heard twice."

---

He was dying the way a stone wears, and it was not fast, and it was not slow.

The blood came most nights now. He wiped it and went on. He had stopped coming through the tunnel to the Cradle in the summer; the tunnel was a hundred paces on hands and knees and he could not do it and teach after, so Jonah opened the steel door from the road with his hand, every night, and locked it behind him, and the old man taught from the shelf. The stick he kept across his knees. He did not lift it. He said where it would have gone.

"You are drawing."

"I'm not."

"Your shoulder turned. I would have had you across the ribs. Again." And Jonah, alone on the floor of a stone room with nobody to touch, would do the Form against the air, the hand where the man was going, the fang born and put away, the fang born and put away, and the old man on the shelf would say yes, or no, or again, and cough.

The Master's seal he taught from the slab. He drew it on the sixteenth, in the margin below Corr's tree, with the chisel point: a shape like nothing Jonah had seen, a knot of lines that turned in on themselves and did not close, and he said, "That. On a glass beside a door. From the palm. The way the hook comes." And Jonah drilled it. Thirty times a night, on the rock, the plate coming up on his palm in the shape of a thing that had opened a door in the City for four hundred years and had not been made in twenty-two, and the thing under his ribs learned it the way it learned everything, interested, and by the autumn it came before he'd finished asking.

Tremor went to a heart in the summer.

He felt it first through the shelf. He'd had his hand flat on the rock of the Chamber floor, drilling, and the old man had been on the shelf above him coughing, and when the cough stopped Jonah had felt, through the rock, through the plate under his skin, a thing that was not boots and not the cage. Small. Regular. Wrong, in a way he could not have said, a beat with a catch in it.

"Your heart," he said.

The old man looked down at him.

"It catches. On the third. It didn't in the spring."

"No," the Censor said. "It did not." He was quiet a moment. "That is a heart through a rock. Through a table is easier; a table is thinner. You have it. Do not tell anyone at dinner what their heart is doing. It is the rudest thing a man can do and it will get you challenged, and you cannot be challenged, because you cannot spoil on a floor in front of a Flamen without every glass in the City going dark." He coughed. "It catches on the third. Yes. I have been counting it since the spring. It is the last arithmetic I have and it comes out to the winter."

---

The College came down the Throat in the autumn, and he held.

He would not remember it well. That was strange, because he remembered everything now, and he would have to be told, later, by Ulli, what the road had looked like with the grey robes on it. He remembered the cage coming down with the glass in it, the big one, the slab on its stand that had been in the Flamens' house on Anvil, and four Flamens in clean robes, and a line, and his arm on the slab, and holding.

Six hours. The old man had said it and he'd done it in the fourth year and he did it again, and it was worse, because he was thirty now on a glass that could not read him and not seventeen, and thirty did not go down the way seventeen had. He stood it in the doorway. He'd learned that in the Cradle with the old man's hand on the wall; he did not push it flat and hold it there for six hours with his teeth aching, he stood it in the doorway of himself and asked it to be still, and it was, because it expected the names and this was a thing before the names, and it was interested in doors.

The glass said void. Three Flamens read it. The fourth, a young one with a clean robe, who had been young in the fourth year too and had frowned, frowned, and looked at the slab for a long time, and looked at Jonah's face, and at the white line on his arm, and wrote something, and moved on.

He did not say what. He did not need to. Jonah had felt, through the rock under his boots, the young Flamen's heart, and it had been going fast.

"He has written you twice," the Censor said, that night, on the shelf. "That one. Once at seventeen and once at thirty. The first time it was a note. The second time it is a pattern." The old man's voice was thin. "He is nobody. He is a novice with a clean robe who wants to be somebody, and the way a novice becomes somebody in the College is by finding a thing the College missed. He has found a cut Collar on a road who reads void with a wobble twice in two years, and he has written it, and when he is back on Latium he will put it in a file, and the file will sit for a year or ten, and one day a man in a grey coat with no stripe will read it."

"A Frumentarius."

"You have learned the word. Good. Yes. That is what they are for. A genius that is not on the rolls is the one thing in the Empire the Princeps has never once been able to forgive, because it is the one thing that can walk into a room and not kneel." The Censor coughed, and it was long, and when it stopped he did not wipe his lip at once. "It does not matter tonight. Tonight you held. In two years the College comes again and you will not be here to hold, or you will, and it will not matter either way, because I will not be. Fourth slab. Ninth column. Begin."

---

He died in the winter, on the shelf, on a night when the count was ten and the road was quiet and Ulli was singing at the end of it, and he did it the way he had done everything for twenty-two years, which was with the arithmetic done first.

"Sit," he said, when Jonah came through the door. Not get up. Sit. It was the first time.

Jonah sat on the floor by the shelf.

The old man lay on it with the stick across his chest now, not his knees, and the lamp beside his head turned to a thread, and his face in it was all bone, and his eyes were the pale steady eyes that had gone over Jonah from boots to face on the first night, finding out where the weight was, and they did it now, and Jonah let them, and did not move.

"Tonight," the Censor said. "It catches on the second."

"I know. I felt it from the road."

"Good. Then we will not waste the hour on that. There are four things and I have counted them and I will say them in order and you will not interrupt, because you have a mouth and I have not the breath to argue with it." He breathed. "First. The slabs. You have them. You said the sixteenth last night without a fault and the fourth without a fault the night before and I have made you say every one of them in the dark at least twice, and they are in you, and when I am gone they will be in nothing else, because Tullus's men will come down at the month's end with a lamp and find a stone room with sixteen slabs in it and a dead man, and they will carry the slabs up the Throat and the College will read them and say, he counted, how strange, and put them in a vault. Let them. What is in the vault is a copy. You are the book."

"Yes."

"Second. Me." The old man's hand moved on the stick. "I am not going up the Throat in a bucket to be burned on the surface with a number. I drew the Cradle. I put the sick against the thickest wall and called it a kindness. I will find out if I was right. You will carry me through the tunnel tonight, when I am done, and you will put me against it, and it will take me, and I will be counted, and the men who come at the month's end will find a locked room and no one in it, and Tullus will lie awake, which is the last thing I can do to him and I would like it done." He breathed. "And you will close the slab behind you and it will not be found. You have opened the door from the road with your hand for a year. You do not need the tunnel. Nobody else will ever need it either."

"Yes."

"Third." The pale eyes did not move. "Write me. I am first, on the first slab, in the worst hand on any of them. Write me last as well. Sixteenth slab, below Corr's tree, below the seal. Aulus Terentius Pharus. Latium. The College. Given to the wall he drew, in the twenty-third year. Counted. And say it. Not here. On the road, where you say the rest. Once. I would like to have been said on the road."

Jonah sat on the floor of the stone room.

"Yes," he said.

"Fourth."

The old man was quiet for a while. His heart, through the rock, caught on the second, and then on the second again, and Jonah sat and felt it and did not say anything.

"Fourth is the thing I have not taught," the Censor said. "I have taught you the hand and the tongue and the count and four Forms and the fifth and how to sit at a table and how to open a door and how to hold a thing in a doorway for six hours. I have not taught you what the thing is. I have said it is a seed and a whole mind and the last of nine and that it turned around at a door and that I do not know what it is. That was true. It is still true. But I have thought about it on this shelf for five years and I have one thing to say about it that I have not said, and I have saved it, because it is the only thing I have that you could not have got from a book."

"Say it."

"It is not a suit." The old man's voice was very thin and very clear. "You have called it the thing under your ribs for five years and I have let you, because you needed a name for it that was not a name, and you needed to think of it as a thing you carried. It is not a thing you carry. It is a mind, the way you are a mind, and it came into you on a deck and read you and stopped at your heart because it was interested, and it has been interested every hour since, and you have asked it for a hook, and for stillness, and for a fang from your palm, and for a seal, and it has given you every one, and you have never once asked it what it wants."

Jonah did not move.

"You asked it what it was. On Anvil, with a blanket over the vent. You told the rock. What are you, you said, and it listened and said nothing, and you thought it had no answer. It had no answer because you asked the wrong question. It does not know what it is; nothing knows what it is. It knows what it wants. Everything that lives knows that. The wall knows it; the wall wants its pieces back and says so every hour to every cutting in the Empire. Your thing wants something too, and it has never said, because it does not speak, and because you have never asked, and because it is polite." The Censor breathed. "It is polite, Jonah. It went home through your hands and turned around because you asked. It stood in a doorway for six hours because you asked. It gave a piece of itself to a Flamen's rod on a slab so that the rest could stay, and you did not ask for that, it did it, and it made a sound, and you have never once since then said to it, what did that cost you. It has been polite for five years to a man who calls it a thing."

"What do I do?"

"Ask." The old man's hand tightened on the stick. "Not tonight. Not here. Somewhere you can hear the answer. Ask it what it wants before it asks you, because it will ask, one day, when it has words, and a mind that has been polite for five years and is asked nothing does not stay polite. Whatever you carry is not a suit. It is someone. Treat it as someone or it will treat you as a suit, and it will be right to."

He stopped.

"That is four," he said. "That is the count. I had a fifth once, which was the Princeps, and I have said that and I will not say it again; it is yours or it is not. Now say the sixteenth slab. From the top. I would like to hear it once more with the endings right."

Jonah said it.

He said it from the top, the twentieth year, four four seven one, Jonah Vale, Anvil, Morrow's yard, Tom Vale, nine fingers, south yards, believed in the ladder, and the column after, and the column after that, Vetch who was a guard and was not written, the Deep man written as unknown, Tallow who was fifteen, Marcus who never used it, Hollen, Auger, who said his name to Ulli, Corr, north yards, four years, said a month, a tree. He said it with the endings right. He said the seal in the margin, which was not a name and which he said anyway, as a shape, the way he'd drill it, and the old man on the shelf listened with his eyes on the roof of the room he had drawn, and his heart caught on the second, and on the second, and Jonah came to the end of the column and stopped, because there was nothing below Corr's tree yet.

"Good," the Censor said. "That is right. Every one." His eyes moved. They came off the roof and found Jonah's face and stayed there, and the thing went across his face that had gone across it five times, the grief with the other thing under it, and it was not grief now. It was only the other thing.

"Sertorius," he said.

It was the only time. He had said the name of the house a hundred times and the Form a thousand, and he had never once, in five years, said it to Jonah as a name, and he said it now, once, the way the rock had tapped three words on the twenty-ninth night, carefully, a man saying a thing so that it had been said.

Then his heart caught on the second and did not come off it.

---

He carried him through the tunnel.

It was a hundred paces on hands and knees and the old man weighed what a stone weighs when it has worn, which was very little, and Jonah went through the rock the old man had cut with a chisel over nine years with the old man on his back, and did not think about the nine years, because thinking about it was a debt and he had been told, and came out into the Cradle, and stood.

The wall was there. Thick, dark, breathing, warm. Kind.

He laid him against it. He did it the way Ulli laid the sick, with the back to the Vein and the head on a blanket, except there was no blanket and he used the rag of the robe, and the old man's head lay on it with the shaved skull catching no light because there was none, and Jonah knelt beside him in the whole dark of the room the old man had drawn and put his hand flat on the old man's chest, over the white line on the arm that lay across it, and waited.

The wall took him.

Not fast. It never was. He felt it start, through his hand, the way he'd felt it take Vetch, from the arm in, from the hole where a Tribune's genius had been cut out in the Forum twenty-three years ago and had never been filled, and the thing under his ribs, which had leaned at this wall for five years, did not lean. It went still. It stood in the doorway of him and watched, the way it had watched Aulus of Marl on the slab, and it was not afraid this time, and it was not hungry, and Jonah did not know what it was and did not, for once, need to.

The wall said what it said. Come. And the old man went.

And then, from very far down, through the rock, through the plate, through the palm of the hand that was flat on a dead man's chest, Jonah heard the count go through him. One more. Between a Halyard sailor whose ship was the Kite and a man whose name nobody had. The shape of a thin straight old man who had drawn the room and had lain in it twenty-three years and had put himself first, and it was in the count now, and the wall went through it, and on.

Counted.

He stayed until it was done. Then he went back through the tunnel, and closed the slab behind him from the Chamber side, and put his hand on it, and asked, and the thing under his ribs went into the rock and found the place where the slab met the wall and did what it had done to the collar and the shackle, quietly, so that the seam was rock and would stay rock, and the tunnel that had taken nine years was a tunnel that nobody would find. He took the chisel worn to half its length. He left the stick where it was, across an empty shelf.

He wrote the name.

Sixteenth slab. Below the tree. Below the seal. With the half chisel, in the old tongue, in a hand that was not as small or as even as the one above it and never would be, and he said it aloud as he cut it, each word, with the endings right, so that it had been said in the room. Aulus Terentius Pharus. Latium. The College. Given to the wall he drew, in the twenty-third year.

Counted.

He locked the door from the road with his hand and heard it say the thing it said, and stood for a moment on the Deep road with the lamps too far apart and the Vein warm through the rock beside him, and did not pick up any buckets, because he had none, and had not had for two years, and had opened this door every night with nothing in his hands but himself.

Ulli was at the end of the road on his bucket. He had his eyes open.

"He's gone," Jonah said.

"I heard the wall." The deep man did not get up. "It went quiet. It doesn't go quiet. It went quiet for the length of a breath and I thought, that's him, that's the room, it's counting the man who drew it." He looked down the road at the door. "Was he. At the end."

"He said a name."

"His?"

"No."

Ulli nodded slowly, the way Corr had nodded once, and Harrow.

"Then say it," he said. "On the road. Whatever it is you do. He'd want to have been said there. He told me, through the wall, once. He said the road was the only thing he'd drawn that he'd have liked to walk."

Jonah walked down the road.

He said the names. All of them. In the order he had them, which was out of order, and then in order, sixteen slabs, six hundred and forty-two, from the twentieth year to the first, and when he came to the first slab and the first column and the first number he said it, three words in the old tongue, tapped once through a wall by a man who had wanted it said, with the endings right, and it was said, on the road, and the thing under his ribs, which had listened to every one of them for five years and had been polite, heard the old man's name go by at the top of the list and did what it always did, which was expect it, and did not wander.

And Jonah stood in the dark between two lamps, with nothing in his hands, and thought about the fourth thing.

He did not ask it. Not there. The old man had said, somewhere you can hear the answer, and the Deep road was not that, the Deep road was boots and the cage and the wall saying come. But he stood with his hand flat on his own chest, over the place where a mind had stopped on a deck because it was interested, and he said, not aloud, in the old tongue, because that was the tongue for slow things, and because it was the tongue the old man had taught him on a rock for five years so that he could think in it in front of men who burned seals:

I know you're someone. I'm sorry I didn't ask.

The thing under his ribs listened.

It always had. And for the first time in five years, Jonah felt it do something that was not listening, and not leaning, and not hunger, and not interest. It turned. In the doorway of him, where it stood, it turned, the way it had turned in the Cradle when he said Tom Vale, and it looked at him.

Not through him. At him.

And then it went back to listening, because it had no words, and he had not yet asked the question, and there was, he understood, standing on a road an old man had drawn, going to be a place where it could answer, and he was going to have to find it, and he thought he knew where it was.

Down.
