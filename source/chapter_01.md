# Chapter 1: Derelict

The derelict had been dead for four hundred years and it was still a better ship than the Kestrel.

Jonah could see that from three kilometres out, which was as close as anyone sane brought a barge to the inner rings of Quirinus. Quirinus was the gas giant that Anvil went round. The rings were where the Empire had thrown its dead ships for four hundred years. The hull hung in the debris the way a coin hangs in water, dark and thin, turning very slowly. Nothing around it had touched it. Four centuries of wreckage. Cutters, haulers, one whole legion transport broken in half. All of it drifting and grinding, eating itself in the ring plane. This one hull sat in the middle of it clean. No pitting. No frost. The rock and ice went around it.

"That's not right," Jonah said.

"Fly," Captain Ochoa said.

Jonah flew.

The Kestrel was two hundred metres of cargo spine with a cabin on one end and a drive on the other, and it handled like exactly that. You did not fly a barge through ring debris. You parked a barge on the ring plane's edge and sent a cutter in on a tether. If the cutter came back with a salvage worth the fuel, you were a genius. If it didn't, you were a Collar who'd wasted a Blank's fuel. There was a scale of consequences for that which everybody on the Limes could recite. Jonah had recited it himself, twice, to inspectors who wanted to hear it.

The scale was the Empire. At the bottom were Collars: peregrines, people with no citizenship, with a ring of black metal round the neck that any Stripe could lock with a word. Jonah had worn his for nineteen years. Above them were Blanks: citizens, with a cutting under the skin of one arm and a thin white line where it had gone in. Above the Blanks were the Stripes. Thin purple on the arm for the equestrian order, broad purple for the senatorial, a whole lorica for each of them, and the law in their hands. The Limes was the frontier, the belt of moons and rocks at the edge of the settled worlds where the Empire kept its yards and its mines and the people who worked them. The Kestrel did not have a cutter. Morrow's yard had sold the cutter in the spring to make a payroll. So the barge went in.

He had the drive at a whisper and the manoeuvring jets doing all the work, and his hands were doing what they did, which was mostly not thinking. A slab of hull plate the size of a tenement block came around on the left, slow, tumbling end over end with a rhythm you could count. He counted it, and took the Kestrel under it on the fourth beat with forty metres to spare. Forty metres was a lot. He heard Marchand let out a breath behind him anyway.

"We're going to die," Marchand said, "and I want it noted that I said so."

"Noted," Jonah said. "Do you want it in the log or just in your heart?"

"Fuel's at thirty-one. If we're not on the return burn by twenty-two we don't make Anvil. I'm saying it so it's said."

"It's said."

"You're a peregrine, Vale. If this barge scrapes so much as paint, it's my name on the report and your neck on the block. You'll be dead before you're sorry, and I'll still be filling in forms."

"That's a beautiful thought, Silas. Write it down. Somebody'll want it for a wedding."

Ochoa said nothing. Ochoa had said very little since the burn out of Anvil, which was normal, and had checked the chart exactly zero times, which was not. Jonah had been flying for Ochoa for six years. The man checked the chart the way other men breathed. He checked it in dock. He checked it in his sleep, probably. And for eleven hours he had sat in the second seat with his hands folded and looked at the ring plane like he was watching for a face in a crowd. Every time Jonah had asked for a heading he had given one without looking at anything. Every heading had been right.

Twenty years, Jonah thought. Somebody gave him this twenty years ago and he's been walking to it in his head ever since.

He didn't say it. You didn't say that kind of thing to Ochoa. You said it to Mara, later, in the dark, and she told you what it meant.

"Captain," he said instead. "It's clean."

"I see it."

"Nothing's clean in here. The ice is going around it."

"I see it, Jonah."

Ochoa's hand came up off his knee and rested on Jonah's shoulder for a moment, which had happened perhaps four times in six years, and then went back.

"Bring us alongside," he said. "Gentle. And then you and I are going in. Bram and Hollis are staying on the winch. Silas is staying on the fuel. Nobody is going to touch anything I don't tell them to touch. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Say it back."

Jonah glanced at him. Ochoa was looking at the hull. His jaw was doing something Jonah had never seen it do.

"Nobody touches anything you don't tell them to touch," Jonah said.

"Good."

---

Up close it was a chapel.

Jonah didn't have another word for it. He'd broken ships for four years before Morrow put him in a pilot's seat. He'd been inside more hulls than most Thinstripe officers ever would. He knew what a warship looked like from the inside, what a hauler looked like, and what a Broadstripe's yacht looked like when it came to Anvil to die with the wine still in the racks. This wasn't any of those. The corridors were too tall. The walls had things carved into them, letters he could half read, the old Latin that the Flamens used on the sides of their cutters and that nobody on the Limes was supposed to be able to read at all. The Flamens were the Empire's priests, if a priest was a man in a white robe who put your arm on a slab of dark glass and read a number off it. They cut the loricas. They put them into arms and took them out again. Nobody was made a citizen without one in the room, and nobody was graded without one. Jonah could read it because manifests were written in it and he'd wanted to know what he was carrying. Most of these words weren't on manifests. MATER. LAMINA. He didn't know the rest.

No frost on the walls. No dust. Four hundred years in vacuum and the place looked like somebody had swept it that morning.

"Captain."

"I know."

"You keep saying that."

"Then stop telling me things."

Their suit lights went ahead of them down the corridor. Ochoa walked like a man walking through his own house at night. Left at the first junction, past two sealed doors without a glance, right at a place where the corridor widened into something that might have been a hall. Jonah stopped counting turns because it was obvious the counting was being done for him.

"You've never been here," Jonah said.

"No."

"But somebody told you the way."

Ochoa didn't answer that. He'd stopped at a door.

It wasn't like the others. The others were hatches, metal, the kind you could cut through in an hour with a good torch and a bad temper. This one was stone. Actual stone, dark, with a grain in it, set into the metal of the hull like somebody had wanted to make a point. There was one word on it, cut deep. Jonah read it before he could stop himself.

"Semen," he said. "It says seed."

"I know what it says."

"Why does a ship have a door that says seed on it?"

Ochoa put his glove flat on the stone. Nothing happened. Then the stone moved, not sideways like a hatch but back and down, all of it, into the floor, with no sound at all because there was no air to carry one. Jonah felt it through his boots. A long, low grinding that went on for longer than it should have.

Beyond it was a room the size of the Kestrel's cabin. Stone floor, stone walls. In the middle, on a low block, a box.

Jonah had been expecting something bigger. He'd been expecting, if he was honest with himself, gold, or a lorica, or something that a Gracchan or a Flamen or whoever had sent Ochoa here would pay a captain's wage for. A lorica was the thing a Stripe carried under his skin. A genius, the Flamens called it: a living piece of matter that grew into a man's arm and gave him plate to wear, a blade to draw, and a number on the glass. The whole Empire ran on them, and the Empire had never once made one. It found them. Every lorica in the settled worlds had been cut, the story went, from a handful of old ones, and cut small, so that it would grow into a citizen and then stop. A whole one, uncut, was a thing out of a Founding Day song. As for the Gracchans, they were the men in the City who said the Senate should rule and the Princeps should not. The Princeps cut a few of them every generation to keep the argument fresh. The box was the size of a man's head. It was the same dark stone as the door. It had no lid that he could see and no lock.

Ochoa went to it and stood over it and didn't touch it.

"Jonah," he said, over the suit channel, and his voice was very steady, which meant it was costing him. "I'm going to tell you something tonight. All of it. Back on Anvil, in Morrow's office with the door shut, you and me and him. You've earned that and I should have done it a year ago. But not here and not on the channel. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Say it back."

"You're going to tell me all of it tonight. In Morrow's office. With the door shut."

"Good." Ochoa put his hands on either side of the box. "Now don't touch this."

"I'm not touching it."

"I mean it. Not with the glove. Not with anything. If I drop it, let it fall."

He lifted it. It came up off the block without any resistance, which surprised Jonah for a reason he couldn't have named. Then the lid, which had not been there, was there, a seam in the stone across the top that opened a finger's width. Jonah looked inside because there was nothing in the universe that could have stopped him looking inside.

It was wrapped in something. Cloth, once. There was a shape under the cloth, no bigger than his fist, and the shape was moving. Not much. The way a sleeping thing moves. He watched the cloth rise and settle, rise and settle, in a room with no air, on a ship that had been dead since before the Principate had a name. Every hair on his body stood up under the suit at once.

"Captain," he said. "It's breathing."

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"Tonight."

Ochoa closed the box with his thumbs. He held it against his chest with both arms, turned around, and walked out of the stone room. Jonah followed him. Behind them the door came up out of the floor and closed. Jonah looked back once. He couldn't have said why. The word on the door looked the same as it had. It looked, if anything, a little relieved.

---

They were forty minutes back aboard the Kestrel. Jonah had the barge turned and lined up for the run out, with the box strapped down in the cabin under Ochoa's seat and Ochoa's boots on either side of it. Then the Vigilant came around the tumbling legion transport at the edge of the ring plane and lit them up.

It was a patrol cutter. Sixty metres, fast, all drive and guns and a stripe painted down the side so nobody could mistake it for something that had to obey traffic law. Jonah had been boarded by cutters before. Everybody who flew for a living on the Limes had been boarded by cutters before. You cut your drive, you opened your hold, you stood in a line with your hands where they could be seen. An officer walked down the line in his plate and looked at each of you like you were a line item that had been miscounted. Then he took ten per cent of whatever you were carrying as a fee for having looked, and you said thank you, sir.

The Vigilant's hail came through on the open channel. A woman's voice, Blank by the sound of it, bored.

"Barge Kestrel, registry Anvil, Morrow yard. Cut your drive and stand to for inspection. Salvage from a founding-era hull falls under the Lex Metallica and is Imperial property. Prepare your manifest."

"They saw us come out," Marchand said. His face had gone the colour of yard-bread. "They saw us come out of it. Captain, they saw."

"Cut the drive," Ochoa said.

"Captain, we can run. Vale can run. Back into the rings, they can't follow a barge into the rings, nobody can, that's the whole point of him."

"They can sit at the edge for a month and we have fuel for six hours. Cut the drive, Jonah."

Jonah cut the drive.

"Silas, get the manifest. Bram, Hollis, in the hold, hands out. Jonah, with me."

Ochoa unstrapped the box from under his seat and stood up with it. For a moment Jonah thought he was going to hide it. Then he understood that there was nowhere on the Kestrel to hide anything from a man with a lorica. A lorica could hear a heartbeat through a bulkhead, and the box was breathing.

"Sir," Jonah said. "What are we going to tell him?"

"The truth," Ochoa said. "That we were about to report it."

"He's going to take it."

"Yes."

"After everything. Twenty years. He's just going to walk in here and take it."

Ochoa looked at him, and for the first time since the burn out of Anvil there was something in his face that Jonah recognised. It was the look he got when a hull was coming apart faster than the numbers said it should.

"How did you know it was twenty years?"

"You didn't check the chart. Not once. You've had it in your head a long time."

Ochoa nodded slowly.

"Tonight," he said. "I promise you. Now come and stand in a line."

---

The boarding party was three men.

Two of them were Blank marines in Tiro plate, which meant they looked like men who had been dipped in dark glass and left to set. It covered them from the neck down, close as skin, with a dull shine on it where the hold lights caught it. At the throat, the armpits and the joints of the hands there were fine seams where the plate met plate. Jonah had seen a hundred of them. They stood by the airlock with their hands near the rifles on their hips and looked at nothing, and they were exactly as frightening as a wall.

The third man was the officer, and he had no plate on at all.

He didn't need it. He walked into the Kestrel's hold in a patrol uniform with the sleeve rolled to the elbow so that the narrow purple stripe on his forearm was where everyone could see it. The stripe was the plate, or it was where the plate lived, and every Collar in the hold knew it. He was mid-thirties. Good-looking in the way that men who've never been told no are good-looking. He glanced at the line, Ochoa, Jonah, Marchand with the manifest held out in both hands, Bram and Hollis with their hands open. Then he glanced past the line at the box on the deck at Ochoa's feet, and his eyes stayed there.

"Decurion Varro," he said. "Vigilant. Captain."

"Ochoa. Kestrel, out of Morrow's yard on Anvil."

"You've been inside the hull."

"Yes, Decurion. We were about to report it."

"Of course you were." Varro walked down the line without hurrying. He did not look at Bram or Hollis. He looked at Marchand's manifest for about as long as it takes to decide not to read something. He stopped in front of Jonah.

"Name."

"Vale. Like the goodbye, not like the valley."

"Caste."

Jonah touched the black ring at his throat. He didn't need to. The decurion was looking right at it. But he liked making people say things out loud.

"Guess."

Varro didn't guess. He looked at Jonah for a moment with an expression Jonah had seen before on inspectors, on quaestors' clerks, and once on a Broadstripe's dog. Then he moved on, and Jonah felt the muscles in his back unlock one at a time. Not today. He was going to get flogged one day for his mouth and everybody who loved him had told him so, but not today.

He had just put two hundred metres of barge through a debris field no suited pilot would have crossed. He'd done it on six hours of fuel with a captain who wouldn't talk and a purser who wouldn't stop. If the Empire wanted to flog him for it, it could get in line.

Varro had stopped in front of Ochoa. He was looking down at the box.

"Open it."

"Decurion, with respect, I'd ask that it be opened by a Flamen. It's a relic. I think it's a relic. I don't know what it does."

"You carried it out."

"I carried it out closed."

"Open it, Captain."

Ochoa bent down. He put his hands on either side of the box the way he had in the stone room. He looked up at Varro from a crouch, and Jonah saw him decide something, and then he opened it.

The seam in the lid parted. The cloth inside rose and settled, rose and settled.

One of the marines said something under his breath. Varro didn't. Varro looked into the box for a long moment, and his face did nothing at all. Jonah would think about that later. It was the face of a man who had just seen the price of a moon and had decided, in the same second, how many people needed to be alive to dispute the claim.

"Close it."

Ochoa closed it. He stood up.

"The salvage is claimed under the Lex Metallica," Varro said. "The vessel is impounded. The crew will be," he said. The fang came out of his forearm and went through Ochoa's throat and back in. He finished, "compensated at the standard rate."

Ochoa sat down. He did it carefully, the way he did everything, and then he lay down, and that was less careful.

Jonah had known him for six years. He had never seen him sit down carefully. He had never seen a fang either, not this close, not going in. It had looked like a bar of dark glass for about as long as it takes to blink. Then it was gone, and the decurion's arm was just an arm again. The narrow purple stripe on it was still there, because of course it was.

Nobody moved. Bram made a sound. Marchand had dropped the manifest and didn't seem to know it.

"You," Varro said. "Peregrine. What's in the box?"

Jonah looked at the box. He looked at Ochoa. There was a lot of blood and it was still coming. Ochoa's eyes were open and looking at the hold ceiling. His mouth was moving. Jonah decided that whatever was in the box, it was his now.

He didn't decide it with words. He would try, later, to find the words he'd decided it with, and there weren't any. There was Ochoa on the deck and there was the box. There was a man with a stripe who had killed the only captain Jonah had ever had in the middle of a sentence about money. There was the thing in the box, breathing. It belonged to Ochoa, and Ochoa had wanted it kept from exactly this, and Jonah had said it back, twice.

He dropped to one knee and put his hand in the box.

He heard Varro laugh. It was a short, real laugh, surprised, the kind you'd give a dog that had tried to open a door. And then the decurion said one word, not loudly, and Jonah's body stopped.

It was the collar. He knew that even as it happened. Every Collar knew it. You grew up knowing that the ring at your throat could be told to hold you, by anyone with the right to tell it. When it did, your body would stand or kneel or lie exactly where it was until somebody let you go. He had seen it done to other men. He had seen Gaspar Roake held on his knees in Founding Square for four hours for looking at a Thinstripe's wife. He had never had it done to him. He had thought about it, at night, the way you think about drowning.

It was worse than that. It wasn't like being held. It was like being replaced. Everything from the neck down belonged to somebody else, and the somebody was walking toward him across the hold at an easy pace. Jonah's hand was in the box, because he'd got it there before the word. His glove had torn on the stone lip going in, and the skin of two fingers was against the cloth.

The cloth was warm.

Varro's boots stopped in front of him.

"Let's be clear about what happened here," Varro said, conversationally, to the hold. "The captain of the barge resisted a lawful claim and was put down. The peregrine attempted to seize Imperial property. The rest of you stood there, which was wise." He crouched. His face came level with Jonah's. "I'm going to take your hand out of that box. It's going to hurt, because you've got two fingers through your glove and I don't care about them. And then I'm going to decide whether you're worth the paperwork. Understand me?"

Jonah couldn't nod. He looked at Varro instead. He tried to put everything he thought about Varro into the look, and it was a lot, and Varro smiled at it.

Under the cloth, the thing moved.

Not the way it had been moving. Not breathing. It turned, the way a head turns, toward the two fingers of skin against it, and Jonah felt it notice him.

He would never find a better word than that. He would spend years looking. It noticed him the way you notice a light going on in a room you thought was empty. There was no sound and no light. Nothing moved in the hold. Something four hundred years alone in a stone box on a dead ship turned toward the first warm thing that had touched it in all that time, and looked.

And then it came in.

---

It was not like putting on a suit.

Everything he'd ever heard about investiture was about putting on a suit. Blanks who'd been through it talked about it in the yard bars like it was a wedding night. The Flamen puts the cutting on your arm and it goes into you, they said. It's cold, then it's warm, then it's you. Then you have a friend under your skin for the rest of your life, a stupid, loyal, dog-brained friend who'll hold a knife off your throat and never ask why. That was a suit going on. That was a cutting: a sliver of a lorica, cut small in the College and fused, so that it grew into a man up to a fixed number and stopped there. Blanks got one. Stripes got the whole thing, or a bigger piece of it, with a name for it and a house behind it.

This was a mind coming in.

It came up through his two fingers, into his hand, up his arm. It was reading him as it came. That was the only way he could think of it. It went through his arm the way a man goes through a house he's thinking of buying. It looked at the bones. It looked at the tendons. It found the old break in his wrist from the yards and stopped there for a moment, interested. Then it went on up into his shoulder and his chest. He felt it find his heart and stop again. This time the interest was different, and his heart went from fast to very fast to something he didn't have a name for.

The collar let go.

He felt it happen. The thing coming in reached his throat and met the collar there, the ring, the dog-brained little lorica the Empire had put on him at birth to hold him, find him, and tell him when to kneel. The thing in him did not so much break it as eat it. That was what it felt like. Something that had been holding his neck for nineteen years was simply not there anymore. The ring was still around his throat but it was a ring now, a piece of black metal, and his body was his.

Then his body was not his either, because the plate came out.

It came out everywhere. It came out wrong. Later, when he had learned what plate was supposed to do, he would understand how wrong. It should have deployed from the marks, from wherever the lorica lived, in a shape it had been cut to make. This came out of him like sweat. It came out of his hands, his neck, his back, his face, dark glass, all at once. It had no shape at all. It hurt in a way that was not like being cut or burned or crushed but like being born. He screamed, and the scream had plate in it too.

Varro had gone back three steps. His face had changed.

"What," he said. "What did you."

Jonah stood up. He didn't do it. His legs did it. He was standing in the middle of the Kestrel's hold covered in something that was still moving, still deciding what it wanted to be, sliding over him in plates and ridges and long ropes of glass that shifted while he watched. His right hand was on fire from the inside, and something was pushing out through the back of it.

The fang came out of the wrong arm.

He knew it was the wrong arm. He didn't know how he knew. It came out of his right forearm, not from the inside where a Stripe's fang lived but from the back of the wrist. It came out crooked, a length of dark glass as long as his hand, bent like a bad knife. It was the ugliest thing he'd ever seen and it was his.

Varro laughed again.

It was a different laugh. It was the laugh of a man who has been frightened for two seconds and has just remembered who he is. Whatever was happening to the peregrine, it was a peregrine, with a crooked shard sticking out of the wrong side of his arm. Varro was a Thinstripe of the equestrian order with fourteen years of plate on him, and there was a procedure for this. Fourteen years was a grade in the twenties. A grade was the number the Flamens read off a man's arm on the glass: how much lorica he carried, how hard it hit, how much it could take. Tiro was one to nine. Miles was ten to nineteen. Principal was twenty to thirty-nine, and above that were the Centurions and the Tribunes and the Legates, and Jonah had never seen one. He knew the bands the way every Collar knew them, from below, as the list of people who could kill him without a trial.

The decurion's plate came up. It came out of the stripe on his arm and over him like water running the wrong way, clean, fast, fitted. It was over him in the time it took Jonah to breathe in. His fang came out of the inside of his forearm, straight, a proper blade, a forearm's length, and he set his feet.

"All right," Varro said. His voice came through the plate flat and close. "All right, Collar. Let's see it."

He came in fast.

Jonah did not know how to fight. He wanted that understood, later, by the only person he ever told all of it to. He had been in yard fights. He had been in bar fights. He had never held anything sharper than a cutting torch with intent to hurt somebody. He was standing in the middle of a hold with his body full of something that was still learning what a body was. A man who had killed other men with the thing on his arm was coming at him with it.

Varro cut. It was a long, fast, sweeping cut, low to high, the kind you use to open someone from hip to shoulder, and it should have done that.

It skidded.

There was plate on Jonah's hip. There should not have been. It had been on his shoulder a heartbeat before. He had not moved it. The thing in him had. Varro's fang met it and went off it sideways with a sound like a hull cracking in the cold. Varro's whole body went with the fang, half a step, turned. For one heartbeat the seam at his throat was toward Jonah and open.

Jonah put the crooked fang in it.

He did not aim. He did not think "throat seam". He would learn the words later, in a room made of rock, from an old man with a stick. His right arm went out the way an arm goes out to catch something falling. The wrong-side blade on the back of his wrist went into the gap between the plate on Varro's chest and the plate on his jaw. It went in easily. That was the thing he remembered. How easy it was. Like putting a hand into warm water.

Varro made a sound. He tried to bring the fang back around, and his arm came halfway and stopped. His plate did something. It rippled, all over, a shiver running across it. Then it started to come off him, sliding back toward the stripe on his arm the way it had come out. Underneath it the decurion was a man in a uniform with a bar of dark glass through his throat. His eyes were on Jonah's face, and he looked surprised.

Jonah pulled the fang out. He didn't want to. The thing in him did. And Varro sat down on the deck, not carefully. Then he lay down, and did not move again.

The marines ran.

He heard them go. Boots on the deck, the airlock cycling, one of them shouting something into a channel Jonah couldn't hear. He didn't turn around. He was looking at Varro, and at the stripe on Varro's arm, which was still there, a narrow band of purple on skin, and which was nothing now. Just a mark. A mark on a dead man.

The plate was going back into him.

He felt it draw in, from everywhere, slowly, the way a tide goes out. It was not smooth. It caught on things. Some of it seemed not to want to go. The crooked fang went last, back into the back of his wrist, and he felt it fold up in there like a knife in a pocket. Then it was gone. He was standing in the Kestrel's hold in a torn work suit with blood on his hand, and it was not his blood.

He was still standing. That was the part he noticed. The stripe was on the deck and he was still standing.

He didn't like that he noticed it. He noticed that too.

---

Ochoa was still alive.

Jonah didn't understand how. Later he would learn that a fang through the throat seam of a man with no plate does what a knife does, and a knife through the throat is not always fast. He got down on the deck beside the captain and put his hand under his head. Ochoa's eyes came off the ceiling and found him.

"Captain. Captain, don't. Don't talk."

Ochoa's mouth moved. There was blood in it. He got a hand up, which should not have been possible, and got it onto the front of Jonah's suit, and held on.

"The seal," he said.

It came out as air and blood and about half of the consonants. Jonah heard it anyway.

"I've got it. I know where it is. Captain, don't."

"Vibius."

"What?"

"Vibius. The seal. Vibius." The hand on his chest tightened. "Promise."

"I promise. I'll get it there. Whatever it is, wherever it goes, I'll take it there myself. I promise. Captain, you were going to tell me. Tonight. All of it. Tell me now."

Ochoa looked at him. There was something in his face that might have been the start of a smile, or might have been the last thing a face does.

"You," he said, "shouldn't have touched it."

And then Jonah was holding the head of a man who wasn't there.

He stayed like that for a while. He didn't know how long. Bram was crying somewhere behind him, big ugly sobs. Hollis was saying the same thing over and over, which was "he just did it, he just did it, he just did it." Nobody was saying anything to Jonah at all. He was aware, very distantly, of a hatch at the top of the hold ladder that led to the cabin, and of the fact that it had been open, and of the fact that it was closed now.

He put Ochoa's head down on the deck.

Inside him, something was waiting. It wasn't doing anything. It had come in. It had eaten his collar. It had put a blade through a Thinstripe's throat, and it had gone back to wherever it went. Now it was sitting behind his heart like a dog that has done something enormous and is waiting to find out whether it was good.

It was hungry. He could feel that. It was very, very hungry, and it was looking at Varro.

"No," Jonah said, out loud, to nobody in the hold.

The thing did not argue. It didn't have anything to argue with. But it did not stop looking, either.

The open channel crackled.

"Barge Kestrel." It was the woman's voice again. It wasn't bored anymore. "This is the Vigilant. Our boarding officer is not responding and our marines report a hostile lorica aboard your vessel. You have thirty seconds to surrender the vessel and all persons aboard, or we will fire on you. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight."

Jonah looked at the box on the deck. It was empty. The cloth in it was just cloth now.

He looked at the ladder to the cabin. He looked at the man he'd killed, and the man who'd been killed. Then at Marchand, who was staring at him from across the hold with a face that Jonah would remember for the rest of his life and never quite manage to read.

"Twenty-two," the channel said. "Twenty-one."

"Bram," Jonah said. "Strap in."

He went up the ladder.
