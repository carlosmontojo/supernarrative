# Chapter 33: Smugglers

She asked on the third day, not the second, and she asked on the bridge, with Pike at the helm and the Belt in the port, and the question was not the one she had said it would be.

"What did you do it for?"

Jonah stood. He could stand; his legs had come back on the second day the way a road comes back to a man who has been off it, badly and then all at once, and he had walked the length of the hold forty times with a hand on the casks and eaten three bowls of Belt fish and slept in the cask with the lid off and Pike on the stool without the bar, and on the third morning Hal had come down the ladder and said, she wants you, and he had gone up.

The Heron's bridge was small. It was a Halyard bridge, built without a licence by people who had built ships for four hundred years and did not care what the Empire thought a bridge should have in it, and it had a helm and a chart glass and two seats and a port the width of a man's arms, and through the port was the Belt: rock, a great deal of it, going by slowly, with the sun somewhere behind and the shadows long. He had not seen a sun in seven years. He looked at it for the length of a breath and then at her.

"For what," he said.

"The cask. The mark. Seven years on a rock and a man teaching you on a stone with a stick, Pike says, every mark in the Empire. Thirty-one days in a box. Kaspar's arm." Yara had her feet up on the chart glass. She was not looking at him; she was looking at the Belt. "You said you'd a letter and a girl and a father you'd killed, and I believed that, because a man doesn't say it that way if it's not true. But nobody sits in a cask for a letter. What did you do it for?"

He thought about the old man on the rock of the Cradle, sitting across from him being a Broadstripe's wife, saying: they will ask you why, and you will have a why ready, and it will be true, and it will not be the whole of the truth, and the part you keep back is the part you keep.

"Four names," he said.

Yara did not move.

"Men on Anvil. And one in the City. They took a thing off me seven years ago that I didn't know I had, and they put me on a dock with a rod on my collar to keep it, and I've had seven years to find out what it was and what it's worth, and I'm going to go and see them about it." He kept his voice level. It was the road's tongue; he had not used the old one on her and did not intend to. "That's what I did it for. The letter's what I'll do first, because a girl who's counting shouldn't have to count past sixteen, and after that it's the four."

"And when you've seen them."

"Then I'll know what I am. That's the answer you gave me a month for. I've not got it yet. It's on the other side of four men."

Yara took her feet off the chart glass.

She looked at him then, and it was the count, the whole of it, and he stood on her bridge and let her, and did not do anything with his face. She had a Halyard's face. It had been made in the Belt by people who looked at rock all day and at each other very carefully, and it did not give a thing away, and there was a thing behind it that was working very hard not to be seen, and he knew what it was, because he had one.

"You've a trick," he said.

Her face did nothing.

"Your heart. On the ladder. You slowed it coming down, on purpose, and you've been holding it slow three days, every time I'm in a room with you, and it's costing you. You've not slept. Your hands have started to do a thing at the ends of the fingers that you've been putting in your pockets." He did not look at her hands. "I know what it is because I did it six years under a glass. Hush. You've a Blank's cutting and it came with Hush, and Hush is a thing a Stripe puts on a room to shut the Collars up, and you've turned it round and put it on yourself, so that a Flamen's glass gets nothing, so that you run dark. It buys hours. Six, if you're good. Then two days you can't sleep and your hands go." He stopped. "Nobody taught you. Nobody teaches that. You found it the way I found it, because you needed to not be read, and you've been doing it ten years, and you've been doing it to me since I came out of the cask because you didn't know what I was and you're a woman who doesn't let a thing she doesn't know read her."

Pike, at the helm, did not turn round.

"That's a thing to say to a captain on her own bridge," Yara said.

"It's a thing I'd want said to me. Once. By somebody who knew what it cost." He put his hands behind his back, the way the old man had taught him a man stands who is not going to use them. "Stop doing it. To me. I'll not read you. I can't; I've no glass, and the thing in me doesn't read people, it reads loricae, and it's read yours already, on the ladder, and it knows you've a nine and Seal and Hush and a Blade you've not drawn in anger in years, and that's all it knows and all it wants. Let your heart go. You'll sleep."

Yara looked at him for a long time.

Then she let it go. He felt it, through the deck, through the plate, through three days of a heart held down by a hand: it came up, the way his had come up out of the well, slowly, through the names of things, and it was a fast heart, and it stayed fast, and she sat on her bridge and let a man she did not know hear it, and her face did nothing, and her hands came out of her pockets.

"Halyard," she said.

"Yes."

"You know what a Halyard is with a Blank's stripe."

"I've thought about it. Three days." He did not look away. "There's one way a Halyard gets a stripe. The Empire doesn't give citizenship to people who build ships without a licence; it gives it to people who tell it where the ships are being built. A yard. Your family's, I'd think, because nobody else's would have been worth a stripe. And then you took the stripe and the money that came with it and you bought a Halyard ship from the people who had one left, and you named it the Heron, and you've run it dark for ten years with a crew of Halyards who know exactly what you did and sail with you anyway, because you've never once cheated one of them, and you've cheated every Vigilant and every dock clerk and every Flamen from here to the Interior." He breathed. "That's the count. I'll not say it again. You'll not have to run from me for it."

The bridge was quiet.

Pike, at the helm, said, to the Belt: "He talks."

"He does," Yara said. Her voice had not changed. "Pike. Go below."

"Captain."

"Go below, Pike. I'll not put a fang in him. I'd lose." She did not take her eyes off Jonah. "Go and sit with Kaspar. He likes you there. Tell him the man from the cask is going to Anvil and we're taking him, and I'll tell you why after."

Pike went.

---

"Anvil," Jonah said, when the hatch was shut.

"You said the four were on Anvil."

"Three. I didn't say I'd asked you to take me."

"You didn't have to. You told me on the ladder where I was going. A yard in the Belt. Thirty-eight casks on a College dock a week later with a story." Yara put her feet back on the chart glass. "The yard's Anvil. Lower docks. It's the only yard in the Belt that'll take two casks of the Vein off a Halyard skimmer and not ask, because it's a Halyard yard, or it was till the Empire annexed the rock ninety years ago and made it a place they build barges. There's a man there. He takes the skim and he melts it down and he sells it up the Interior a shard at a time to people who'd rather not have their names on a College manifest." She was quiet a moment. "You knew that. You've known it since you came out of the cask and heard the hull. You know what a Halyard ship sounds like. You flew a barge."

"The Kestrel. Out of Anvil's lower docks. Six years."

"Then you know the yard."

"I know Morrow's yard," Jonah said. "I know a man named Ezra Morrow who has a law book in his office he's not supposed to have, and a petition he was going to send to the City, and a man on the Kestrel's manifest who wrote that the hold was empty. I know whose yard paid the melt seven years ago when a barge came in with a hull ring the Vigilant wanted looked at. That's where you're going. That's who takes your skim." He looked at the Belt going by. "I'm not going to ask you to take me anywhere else. I've been trying to get to Anvil for seven years and I've been in a cask in your hold going there for a month without knowing, and I'd like to know what you want for it, because a Halyard captain doesn't give a thing away, and you've a use for me, and you counted it on the ladder."

Yara looked at him.

"Seal a cask," she said.

He waited.

"Two casks. The skim. With the mark, the right way round, from outside, the way a Flamen does it. Two casks of the Vein with the College's knot on the lid go up the Interior as harvest, not as melt. Not a shard at a time to men who'd rather not; whole, to a College dock, with a slate that says they came from Tessera on a contractor's freighter and the contractor's name is the Heron." Her voice was flat. "That's ten years of running dark paid in one dock. That's the Heron's own yard, on a rock with no Empire on it, and a crew who never has to say the word skim again. That's what I counted on the ladder. A man who can make the College's mark is worth more than every Vigilant in the Belt, and he's in my hold, and he can't stand up." She shrugged. "He can now."

Jonah thought about it.

He thought about it the way the old man had taught him, all at once, without looking, and it came out, and it came out to a thing he had not expected, which was that he could not.

"No," he said.

"You've not heard the rest."

"I've heard the whole. I'll not do it, and it's not because I don't want to, and it's not because of what you did for the stripe. I'd seal you a hundred casks for a ride to Anvil and think it cheap." He took his hands from behind his back. "It won't take."

"You made it from inside. Backward. Pike saw."

"From inside, on a lid I'd shut over myself, and it took because it was true. I'd shut it. It said, this is shut, and it was. It said nothing else." He held up his right hand, palm out, the way the old man had held up a stick. "The College's mark on a cask of skim says, this is shut and this is counted and the College counted it. That's a lie. Not a big one. A Belt lie, the kind every dock in the Empire tells every day and nobody dies of. And it won't close. I know it won't, because I've had one not close on me, at the road's end of the Deep, with a Flamen's rod on the lid and me inside being a slab, and that was the best lie a thing with a name ever told, and the mark came apart on it, and that's why I'm on your ship and not on a College dock with the harvest." He lowered the hand. "The plate won't hold on a lie. A man taught me that with a stick and I didn't believe him and it cost me a cask. It's not a rule. It's what the thing is. I can't make it lie for you any more than I can make it lie for me."

Yara did not say anything for a while.

"Show me," she said.

He showed her.

He did it on the chart glass, because it was the only dark glass on the bridge, and he put his palm flat on it beside her boots and asked, and the shape went out, the knot of closed lines, and it went into the glass, and it did not close. It went in the way it had gone into thirty-one's lid at the road's end, looking for a thing to be true about, and there was nothing there, a chart glass on a Halyard bridge with nothing shut in it and nothing counted, and the lines let go of each other, and were not, and Yara watched them not be, from a hand's width away, with her heart going fast.

"Again," she said. "On the port. Say it's shut."

"It's not shut. It's a port."

"Say it."

He put his hand on the port and asked and the mark went in and came apart, and she watched that too.

"Now the hatch," Yara said. "It's shut. Pike shut it."

He put his hand on the hatch. It was steel, not glass; the mark did not go into steel the way it went into the Vein, it went in the way the plate had gone into the Chamber's lock, slower, asked, and it went in, and it closed. The knot took. It sat on a Halyard freighter's hatch the right way round, the College's, a thing that said this is shut, over a hatch that was, and Yara looked at it for a long time and then got up and put her own hand on it, on the mark, and Jonah felt her cutting through the steel feel the College in it and not like it.

"That's real," she said.

"Yes."

"A Flamen would read that as a Flamen's."

"A Flamen would read it as a Flamen's on a hatch that's shut. On a cask of skim he'd read a knot that came apart, and he'd ask why, and he'd ask who, and he'd have your name off the slate before he'd finished asking." Jonah took his hand off the hatch. "I've told you the truth about what I can do. I'd have lied to you, if it'd take. It won't."

Yara stood with her hand on the mark on her own hatch.

"A man who can't lie," she said.

"A man whose plate can't. I lie fine. I lied to a prefect for seven years and a Master of Readings for six hours and I'll lie to four men on Anvil until they're dead." He met her eyes. "But not with the mark. And not to you, because you asked me on the ladder what I was and I told you I didn't know, and that was the first true thing I've said to a stranger since a woman on a stool asked me my name, and I'd like to keep it."

---

She named her price at the port, with the Belt going by, and it was not money and it was not casks.

"You'll come back," she said.

He did not understand.

"Not to me. Not for me. I've a wife on Sable's side of the Belt and a ship and a crew and I don't want a thing that eats loricae in any of them." Yara did not turn round. "Here's what I counted, on the ladder, and it wasn't casks; that was the thing I said so I'd have a thing to say. I counted a man who came out of a sealed cask in my hold, and stopped eating my mate when a cutting said please, and lay down on my deck and told me where I was going and what I'd done for my stripe and that he'd not say it again. And I thought, that's a thing the Belt's never had. Not the mark. Him. A man the Empire made in a hole, who can do to a Stripe what a Stripe does to us, and who stops." She put her hand on the port. "You're going to Anvil for four names. Then you're going to the City for the fourth. And then you're going to be something, because a man doesn't do that and stay what he was, and I don't know what it'll be and neither do you, and here's the price. When you're it. Whatever it is. You come back to the Belt, once, and you stand on a Halyard deck, and you tell me what you are, first, before you've told the Empire. Before you've told anybody with a stripe. So there's one place it was said where the Empire wasn't in the room."

Jonah stood beside her.

"And if what I am's a thing you don't want on your deck."

"Then I'll put you off it. And you'll go." She looked at him. "That's the other half. You'll not run from me. You'll come when you said and you'll go when I say, and you'll not once put your plate up on my ship, whatever you are by then, and you'll not once lie to me, because your plate can't and I'll know. That's the price. It's not fair. It's a Halyard ship."

He thought about a deep man on a bucket with his eyes shut saying, I'm asking you, once, to take one.

"Yes," he said.

"Say it the way you said it to Pike. So it's said."

"When I'm the thing I'm going to be, I'll come to the Belt, once, first, and stand on your deck and say it. And I'll go when you tell me. And I'll not run." He put his hand flat on the port, beside hers, on the cold. "That's the count. It's yours."

Yara took her hand off the port.

"Then you're crew," she said, "as far as Anvil. Hal's cousin. Off the Belt. You've no name on my slate and you'll not need one; a Halyard crewman's a thing a dock clerk doesn't write down. You'll wear a hood on the docks because there's a white line on your arm and a man with a reading on file somewhere, and you'll not go near Morrow's yard while my casks are in it, because I'll not have you eaten on the same dock I'm being paid on." She went back to her seat and put her feet up. "And there's a thing you'll do for me before Anvil. Not the price. A thing."

"Say it."

"Kaspar."

Jonah waited.

"He's got half a cutting. It's alive. It's in his arm and it's grey to the elbow and it doesn't plate and it doesn't fang and it's in there, and he lies in the hold and feels it. You did that." She said it without weight. "You said a cutting said please and you stopped. Go and put your hand on it and ask it what it wants. You can hear them. You said so. He's my mate and he's Pike's sister's boy and he's twenty years on my ship and he's got a thing in his arm that nobody on this ship can talk to, and you can." She looked at the Belt. "Ask it what it wants. Then tell him. Then we'll see."

---

He went down.

Kaspar was where he had been, on a pallet between the casks with his arm bare on a folded coat, and Pike was beside him on the stool, and the mate looked at the man from the cask coming down the ladder and his face did the thing a face does that has been told a thing is coming and would rather it did not.

"Captain sent me," Jonah said.

"I know what she sent you for."

"Then give me the arm."

Kaspar did not. He lay and looked at Jonah and Jonah stood over him and did not reach, and after a while Pike said, "Give him the arm, boy," and the mate held it out, grey to the elbow, shining, with a thing in it that had been six grades and was three, and Jonah knelt and put his palm on it.

He asked.

Not the way he had asked a shackle, no, or a lock, open, or a plate, that. He asked the way he had asked the wall in the Mouth, plainly, in the old tongue, through the plate on his palm into a living cutting that had had half of itself eaten by the thing asking: what do you want.

It told him.

It did not have words. It had one want, the way the shackle had had one, and it was not hold. It was the thing a cutting wants that has grown in a boy's arm for sixteen years on a ship in the cold: it wanted to close. To seal. It had learned that one trick, the seams, and it had been the whole of what it was, a thing that shut the cold out, and half of it was gone and the seams were open and it was cold, and it wanted, with everything it had left, to shut them, and could not, because the part of it that shut was in Jonah.

He knelt with his hand on the mate's arm and understood that.

"It wants to close," he said. "It's cold. The half I took was the half that shuts, and it's in me, wrong, and the half that's left is the half that feels the cold and can't do anything about it." He did not take his hand away. "That's what it wants, Kaspar. It wants its Seal back. I can't give it back; I don't know how, and if I did it'd come out of me the way it went in, wrong. But I can tell it something."

"Tell it what."

Jonah looked at the arm.

Then he said, in the old tongue, aloud, with his palm on a living cutting, the thing he had said to a College shackle at the road's end of the Deep, except that it was the other way round, and he had never once tried it the other way round, and did not know if it went:

"Close."

The arm went grey to the shoulder.

Not plate. It was not plate; it was the cutting doing the one thing it had left, with everything it had left, because a voice it knew had told it in the only tongue it had, and the grey went up the arm from the elbow to the shoulder and the shining went with it and the seams, the ones a lorica has at every joint, shut. Kaspar made a sound. It was not the scream. It was the sound a man makes when a thing that has been cold for three days is not.

"It's warm," the mate said.

"Yes."

"It's not plating. It's just." He looked at his arm. "Warm."

"That's all it's got left. It'll not plate for you and it'll not fang, ever again, and I did that. But it'll close. When it's cold. If you ask it." Jonah took his hand off the arm. "In the old tongue. Occlude. Say it the way I said it. It knows the word now."

Kaspar looked at him.

"I don't speak the old tongue."

"You speak one word of it. That's one more than a Blank's supposed to." He stood up. His knees made a sound; he was twenty-five and they had been on a rock. "Say it to it at night. It'll come. It's a thing that wants to do the one thing it's got, and nobody's ever asked it in a way it understood, and now somebody has."

He went up the ladder.

Pike said, behind him, to the mate, to the hold, to nobody: "He talks."

And Kaspar, on the pallet, with his arm grey and warm to the shoulder, said, very quietly, to the arm, in a Belt accent with Halyard under it, one word in a tongue he did not speak, and the arm heard it.

---

Yara was on the bridge with her feet on the chart glass, and did not turn round when he came up.

"Well?"

"It wants to close. I told it to. It did. He's warm." He stood at the port. The Belt went by, rock, and there was a thing in the far dark that was not rock, that was a yard-world with a hull ring above it catching a sun he had not seen in seven years, and he had flown out of it on a Collar barge with an old captain saying nothing, and he was coming back to it in a hood, as a Halyard crewman with no name on a slate. "He'll not plate again. I said that too."

"He knows."

"And I said the other thing. That I did it." He watched the yard-world. "I'll say it again if he wants it. Once a day till Anvil. It's not much."

"It's what a guard has," Yara said.

He turned round.

She had not moved. She sat with her feet up and the Belt in the port and her heart going fast because she had let it, and she was not looking at him, and she said, to the chart glass: "That's a thing you say. In your sleep. Pike hears it. It's what a guard has, it's not much. And a number. Four four seven one. And names. A long list of names, in the old tongue, every night, with the lid off. He's stopped counting them; he says it takes too long."

Jonah stood at the port and could not find the door of the place where he kept things.

"There's one more thing," he said, when he could. "Not a price. A count. I've a foreman on Tessera with a girl on Marl. Ada. Thirteen. The south road past Pell's forty. She's writing to a number the Throat wrote wrong, and her father's alive, and I said a man would go to her and say so, and the number, and a thing he said." He looked at the yard-world. "I'll not be on Marl. I'll be on Anvil in a hood, and then I'll be somewhere worse. You run the Belt. Marl's on the far side of it."

"That's a long way for a message."

"Yes."

"And it's not on the slate."

"No," Jonah said. "It's on mine. I'm asking you to carry it. That's all. Once, to take one."

Yara was quiet for a long time.

"What's the thing he said?"

"The north forty never made quota."

She almost smiled. It was a Halyard's almost; it did not reach anything.

"That's a farmer's thing," she said. "Marl. They sing to the fields." She put her feet down. "Write it. The name and the road and the number and the thing. Pike'll take it; he's Belt-born and he's got a sister on Marl's side who owes him. It'll be a season. She'll get it."

"Thank you."

"Don't." Yara stood up and went to the helm. "You'll pay for it. Not now. When you come back to my deck and tell me what you are, you'll tell me if she got it, and if you don't know, you'll have failed the count, and I'll know that too." She put her hands on the helm. "Go and sleep. In the cask, with the lid off. Five days to Anvil. Learn your plate. It's wrong; I felt it on the hatch. Pike says it comes up in lumps."

"It does."

"Then fix it," Yara said, "before you meet four men with it," and did not say anything else, and Jonah went down.
