# Chapter 33: Smugglers

She asked on the third day, not the second, on the bridge, with Pike at the helm and the Belt in the port. It was not the question she had said it would be.

"What did you do it for?"

Jonah stood. His legs had come back on the second day, badly and then all at once. He had walked the hold forty times with a hand on the casks, eaten three bowls of Belt fish and slept in the cask with the lid off, Pike on the stool without the bar. That morning Hal had come down the ladder and said, she wants you.

The Heron's bridge was small: a helm, a chart glass, two seats, a port the width of a man's arms. Halyard-built, without a licence, by people who had built ships for four hundred years and did not care what the Empire thought a bridge needed. Through the port went the Belt, rock, a great deal of it, with a sun somewhere behind. He had not seen a sun in seven years. He gave it one breath and looked at her.

"For what," he said.

"The cask. The mark. Seven years on a rock, a man teaching you every mark in the Empire with a stick, Pike says. Thirty-one days in a box. Kaspar's arm." Yara had her feet up on the chart glass and her eyes on the Belt. "You said a letter and a girl and a father you'd killed. I believed that; a man doesn't say it that way if it's not true. But nobody sits in a cask for a letter. What did you do it for?"

The old man had told him this would come, sitting across from him on the Cradle rock, being a Broadstripe's wife. They will ask you why. Have a why ready. True, and not the whole truth. The part you keep back is the part you keep.

"Four names," he said.

Yara did not move.

"Men on Anvil. And one in the City. They took something off me seven years ago that I didn't know I had, and put me on a dock with a rod on my collar to keep it. I've had seven years to find out what it was and what it's worth. I'm going to go and see them about it." Road's tongue; he had not used the old one on her and did not mean to. "That's what I did it for. The letter first, because a girl who's counting shouldn't have to count past sixteen. After that, the four."

"And when you've seen them."

"Then I'll know what I am. That's the answer you gave me a month for. It's on the other side of four men."

Yara took her feet off the chart glass.

She looked at him then, the whole count. He stood on her bridge and let her. She had a Halyard's face, made in the Belt by people who looked at rock all day and at each other very carefully. It gave nothing away. Behind it something was working very hard not to be seen. He knew what it was, because he had one.

"You've a trick," he said.

Her face did nothing.

"Your heart. You slowed it coming down the ladder, on purpose. You've held it slow three days, every time I'm in a room with you, and it's costing you. You've not slept. Your fingers have started to shake at the ends, and you've been putting them in your pockets."

"Careful."

"I know what it is because I did it six years under a glass. Hush. Your cutting came with it. A Stripe puts Hush on a room to shut the Collars up. You've turned it round and put it on yourself, so a Flamen's glass gets nothing and you run dark. It buys six hours if you're good. Then two days you can't sleep and your hands go."

"Nobody taught me that."

"Nobody teaches it. You found it because you needed to not be read, same as I did. Ten years, I'd say. And you've been doing it to me since I came out of the cask, because you didn't know what I was."

Pike, at the helm, did not turn round.

"That's a bold thing to say to a captain on her own bridge," Yara said.

"It's what I'd want said to me. Once. By somebody who knew what it cost." He put his hands behind his back, the stance the old man taught for a man who is not going to use them. "Stop doing it. To me. I can't read you; I've no glass. Tacitus doesn't read people, it reads loricae, and it's read yours already, on the ladder. You've a nine. Seal and Hush, and a Blade you've not drawn in anger in years. That's all it knows and all it wants. Let your heart go. You'll sleep."

Yara looked at him for a long time.

Then she let it go. He felt it through the deck, through the plate: three days of a heart held down by a hand, coming up. It was a fast heart, and it stayed fast. She sat on her bridge and let a man she did not know hear it. Her hands came out of her pockets.

"Halyard," she said.

"Yes."

"You know what a Halyard is with a Blank's stripe."

"I've thought about it. Three days." He did not look away. "There's one way a Halyard gets a stripe. The Empire doesn't give citizenship to people who build ships without a licence. It gives it to people who tell it where the ships are being built."

"Go on."

"A yard. Your family's, I'd think; nobody else's would have been worth a stripe. You took the stripe and the money and bought a Halyard ship from the people who had one left. You named it the Heron. Ten years running dark with a crew of Halyards who know exactly what you did and sail with you anyway, because you've never once cheated one of them. You've cheated every Vigilant and dock clerk and Flamen from here to the Interior." He breathed. "That's the count. I'll not say it again. You'll not have to run from me for it."

The bridge was quiet.

Pike said, to the Belt: "He talks."

"He does," Yara said. Her voice had not changed. "Pike. Go below."

"Captain."

"Go below, Pike. I'll not put a fang in him. I'd lose." She did not take her eyes off Jonah. "Sit with Kaspar. He likes you there. Tell him the man from the cask is going to Anvil and we're taking him, and I'll tell you why after."

Pike went.

---

"Anvil," Jonah said, when the hatch was shut.

"You said the four were on Anvil."

"Three. I didn't say I'd asked you to take me."

"You didn't have to. You told me on the ladder where I was going. A yard in the Belt. Thirty-eight casks on a College dock a week later with a story." Yara put her feet back up. "The yard's Anvil. Lower docks. The only yard in the Belt that'll take two casks of the Vein off a Halyard skimmer and not ask, because it's a Halyard yard. Was, till the Empire annexed the rock ninety years ago and made it a place they build barges. There's a man there who takes the skim and melts it down. He sells it up the Interior a shard at a time, to people who'd rather not be on a College manifest."

"I know."

"You've known since you heard the hull from inside the cask. You know what a Halyard ship sounds like. You flew a barge."

"The Kestrel. Out of Anvil's lower docks. Six years."

"Then you know the yard."

"I know Morrow's yard," Jonah said. "I know a man named Ezra Morrow with a law book in his office he's not supposed to have, and a petition he was going to send to the City. I know a man on the Kestrel's manifest who wrote that the hold was empty. And I know whose yard paid the melt seven years ago when a barge came in with a hull ring the Vigilant wanted looked at."

"Morrow's."

"That's where you're going. That's who takes your skim." He looked at the Belt going by. "I'll not ask you to take me anywhere else. I've been trying to get to Anvil for seven years, and I've been in your hold going there for a month without knowing it. What do you want for it? You've a use for me. You counted it on the ladder."

Yara looked at him.

"Seal a cask," she said.

He waited.

"Two casks. The skim. With the mark, the right way round, from outside, a Flamen's mark. Two casks of the Vein with the College's knot on the lid go up the Interior as harvest, not melt. Whole, to a College dock, with a slate that says Tessera, contractor's freighter, and the contractor's name is the Heron."

"That's ten years of running dark paid in one dock."

"That's the Heron's own yard, on a rock with no Empire on it, and a crew who never has to say the word skim again. That's what I counted on the ladder. A man who can make the College's mark is worth more than every Vigilant in the Belt. He's in my hold, and he can't stand up." She shrugged. "He can now."

Jonah thought about it, all at once, the old man's way. It came out somewhere he had not expected, which was that he could not.

"No," he said.

"You've not heard the rest."

"I've heard the whole. I'll not do it. Not because I don't want to, and not because of what you did for the stripe. I'd seal you a hundred casks for a ride to Anvil and think it cheap." He took his hands from behind his back. "It won't take."

"You made it from inside. Backward. Pike saw."

"From inside, on a lid I'd shut over myself, and it took because it was true. I'd shut it. The mark said, this is shut, and it was. It said nothing else." He held up his right hand, palm out. "The College's mark on a cask of skim says, this is shut and this is counted and the College counted it. That's a lie."

"A Belt lie. Every dock in the Empire tells it every day and nobody dies of it."

"And it won't close. I know, because I've had one not close on me. At the road's end of the Deep, with a Flamen's rod on the lid and me inside being a slab. That was the best lie Tacitus ever told, and the mark came apart on it. That's why I'm on your ship and not on a College dock with the harvest." He lowered the hand. "The plate won't hold on a lie. A man taught me that with a stick. I didn't believe him and it cost me a cask. It's not a rule. It's what the plate is."

Yara said nothing for a while.

"Show me," she said.

He did it on the chart glass, the only dark glass on the bridge. He put his palm flat beside her boots and asked. The knot went out, went into the glass, and did not close. It went looking for something to be true about. There was nothing there: a chart glass with nothing shut in it and nothing counted. The lines let go of each other and were gone. Yara watched them go from a hand's width away, with her heart going fast.

"Again," she said. "On the port. Say it's shut."

"It's not shut. It's a port."

"Say it."

He put his hand on the port and asked. The mark went in and came apart. She watched that too.

"Now the hatch," Yara said. "It's shut. Pike shut it."

He put his hand on the hatch. Steel, not glass; the mark went into steel slower, asked, the way the plate had gone into the Chamber's lock. It went in, and it closed. The knot took. The right way round, the College's, over a hatch that was shut. In the same moment the seam at his throat slammed shut with it. The wrong-shaped Seal, doing what it did. He stood with one hand on the hatch and the other at his neck, working the seam open, and it came open in a lump. Yara watched that as well. Then she got up and put her own hand on the mark. He felt her cutting, through the steel, feel the College in it and not like it.

"That's real," she said.

"Yes."

"A Flamen would read that as a Flamen's."

"On a hatch that's shut. On a cask of skim he'd read a knot that came apart. He'd ask why, then who. He'd have your name off the slate before he'd finished asking." Jonah took his hand off the hatch. "I've told you the truth about what I can do. I'd have lied to you, if it would take. It won't."

Yara stood with her hand on the mark on her own hatch.

"A man who can't lie," she said.

"A man whose plate can't. I lie fine. I lied to a prefect for seven years and a Master of Readings for six hours. I'll lie to four men on Anvil until they're dead." He met her eyes. "But not with the mark. And not to you. You asked me on the ladder what I was and I said I didn't know. That was the first true thing I'd said to a stranger since a woman on a stool asked me my name, and I'd like to keep it."

---

She named her price at the port, with the Belt going by. It was not money and it was not casks.

"You'll come back," she said.

He did not understand.

"Not to me. Not for me. I've a wife on Sable's side of the Belt, and a ship and a crew. I don't want something that eats loricae in any of them." Yara did not turn round. "Here's what I counted on the ladder, and it wasn't casks. Casks was something to say. I counted a man who came out of a sealed cask in my hold and stopped eating my mate when a cutting said please. Who told me where I was going. Who told me what I'd done for my stripe, and that he'd not say it again."

"That's not much of a count."

"It's one the Belt's never had. Not the mark. Him. A man the Empire made in a hole, who can do to a Stripe what a Stripe does to us, and who stops." She put her hand on the port. "You're going to Anvil for three names and the City for the fourth. After that you'll be something, because a man doesn't do that and stay what he was. I don't know what, and neither do you."

"No."

"Here's the price. When you're it, whatever it is, you come back to the Belt, once, and you stand on a Halyard deck. You tell me what you are, first. Before you've told the Empire. Before anybody with a stripe. So there's one place it was said where the Empire wasn't in the room."

Jonah stood beside her.

"And if what I am's something you don't want on your deck."

"Then I'll put you off it, and you'll go." She looked at him. "That's the other half. You'll not run from me. You'll come when you said and go when I say. You'll not once put your plate up on my ship, whatever you are by then. You'll not once lie to me, because your plate can't and I'll know. That's the price. It's not fair. It's a Halyard ship."

He thought about a deep man on a bucket with his eyes shut, saying, I'm asking you, once, to take one.

"Yes," he said.

"Say it properly. So it's said."

"When I'm the thing I'm going to be, I'll come to the Belt, once, first, and stand on your deck and say it. I'll go when you tell me. And I'll not run." He put his hand flat on the port beside hers, on the cold. "That's the count. It's yours."

Yara took her hand off the port.

"Then you're crew," she said, "as far as Anvil. Hal's cousin. Off the Belt. No name on my slate; a dock clerk doesn't write down a Halyard crewman. You'll wear a hood on the docks, because there's a white line on your arm and a man with a reading on file somewhere. You'll not go near Morrow's yard while my casks are in it. I'll not have you eaten on the same dock I'm being paid on." She went back to her seat and put her feet up. "And there's a job before Anvil. Not the price. A job."

"Say it."

"Kaspar."

Jonah waited.

"He's got half a cutting. It's alive. It's in his arm and grey to the elbow. It doesn't plate and it doesn't fang. He lies in the hold and feels it. You did that." She said it without weight. "You said a cutting said please and you stopped. So you can hear them. Go and put your hand on it and ask it what it wants. He's my mate and Pike's sister's boy, twenty years on my ship, with something in his arm nobody on this ship can talk to. You can." She looked at the Belt. "Ask it what it wants. Tell him. Then we'll see."

---

He went down.

Kaspar was on a pallet between the casks, arm bare on a folded coat, Pike beside him on the stool. The mate watched the man from the cask come down the ladder with the face of someone who has been told what is coming and would rather it did not.

"Captain sent me," Jonah said.

"I know what she sent you for."

"Then give me the arm."

Kaspar did not. Jonah stood over him and did not reach. After a while Pike said, "Give him the arm, boy." The mate held it out, grey to the elbow, shining, with a cutting in it that had been six grades and was three. Jonah knelt and put his palm on it. The arm jerked under his hand, once, the whole of it, and then lay still.

He asked.

Not as he had asked the shackle, no, or the lock, open, or the plate, that. He asked the way he had asked the wall in the Mouth, plainly, in the old tongue, through his palm into a living cutting that had had half of itself eaten by what was asking: what do you want.

It told him.

No words. One want, as the shackle had had one, and it was not hold. It wanted to close. To seal. Sixteen years in a boy's arm on a ship in the cold, and it had learned that one trick, the seams. That had been the whole of what it was. Now half of it was gone and the seams were open and it was cold. It wanted, with everything it had left, to shut them. It could not, because the part that shut was in Jonah.

"It wants to close," he said. "It's cold. The half I took was the half that shuts, and it's in me, wrong. The half that's left feels the cold and can't do anything about it." He kept his hand on the arm. "That's what it wants, Kaspar. Its Seal back. I can't give it back. I don't know how, and if I did it'd come out of me wrong. But I can tell it something."

"Tell it what."

Jonah looked at the arm. Then he said, in the old tongue, aloud, with his palm on a living cutting, what he had said to a College shackle at the road's end of the Deep, turned round. He had never tried it turned round. He did not know if it went.

"Close."

The arm went grey to the shoulder.

Not plate. The cutting was doing the one thing it had left, with everything it had left, because a voice it knew had told it in the only tongue it had. The grey went up from the elbow to the shoulder and the shine went with it. The seams at every joint shut. Kaspar made a sound, not the scream, the sound a man makes when something that has been cold for three days is not.

"It's warm," the mate said.

"Yes."

"It's not plating. It's just." He looked at his arm. "Warm."

"That's all it's got left. It'll not plate for you and it'll not fang, ever again. I did that. But it'll close, when it's cold, if you ask it." Jonah took his hand away. "In the old tongue. Occlude. Say it the way I said it. It knows the word now."

Kaspar looked at him.

"I don't speak the old tongue."

"You speak one word of it. That's one more than a Blank's supposed to." He stood. His knees made a sound; he was twenty-five and they had been on a rock. "Say it at night. It'll come. It's got one thing left to do, and nobody's ever asked it in a way it understood. Now somebody has."

He went up the ladder.

Behind him Pike said, to the mate, to the hold, to nobody: "He talks."

And Kaspar, on the pallet, with his arm grey and warm to the shoulder, said very quietly, in a Belt accent with Halyard under it, one word in a tongue he did not speak. The arm heard it.

---

Yara was on the bridge with her feet on the chart glass, and did not turn round.

"Well?"

"It wants to close. I told it to. It did. He's warm." He stood at the port. Rock went by, and in the far dark something that was not rock: a yard-world with a hull ring above it, catching a sun. He had flown out of it on a Collar barge with an old captain saying nothing. He was coming back in a hood, a Halyard crewman with no name on a slate. "He'll not plate again. I said that too."

"He knows."

"And I said the other part. That I did it." He watched the yard-world. "I'll say it again if he wants it. Once a day till Anvil. It's not much."

"It's what a guard has," Yara said.

He turned round.

She had not moved. Feet up, the Belt in the port, her heart going fast because she had let it. She said, to the chart glass: "That's something you say. In your sleep. Pike hears it. It's what a guard has, it's not much. And a number. Four four seven one. And names. A long list of them, in the old tongue, every night, with the lid off. He's stopped counting. He says it takes too long."

Jonah stood at the port and said nothing for a while.

"There's one more thing," he said, when he could. "Not a price. A count. I've a foreman on Tessera with a girl on Marl. Ada. Thirteen. The south road past Pell's forty. She's writing to a number the Throat wrote wrong, and her father's alive. I said a man would go to her and say so, and the number, and one thing he said." He looked at the yard-world. "I'll not be on Marl. I'll be on Anvil in a hood, and then somewhere worse. You run the Belt. Marl's on the far side of it."

"That's a long way for a message."

"Yes."

"And it's not on the slate."

"No," Jonah said. "It's on mine. I'm asking you to carry it. That's all. Once, to take one."

Yara was quiet for a long time.

"What did he say? The foreman."

"The north forty never made quota."

She almost smiled. A Halyard's almost; it did not reach anything.

"That's a farmer's line," she said. "Marl. They sing to the fields." She put her feet down. "Write it. The name and the road. The number and the line. Pike'll take it. He's Belt-born and he's a sister on Marl's side who owes him. It'll be a season. She'll get it."

"Thank you."

"Don't." Yara stood and went to the helm. "You'll pay for it. Not now. When you come back to my deck and tell me what you are, you'll tell me if she got it. If you don't know, you'll have failed the count, and I'll know that too." She put her hands on the helm. "Go and sleep. In the cask, with the lid off. Five days to Anvil. Learn your plate. It's wrong; I felt it on the hatch. Pike says it comes up in lumps."

"It does."

"Then fix it," Yara said, "before you meet four men with it," and said nothing else. Jonah went down.
